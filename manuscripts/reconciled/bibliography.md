# 51. Bibliography

![Bibliography — logo](enterprise-applications-and-financial-systems/posts/bibliography/assets/images/bibliography/logo.jpeg)

> **IN THIS SECTION, YOU WILL:** Find where the book's evidence comes from, what kind of evidence each source provides and which chapters rely on it, by topic or by chapter.

> **KEY POINTS:**
>
> * **Sources are listed because they were consulted.** Every entry was cited in a chapter's **Sources and Evidence** section, and its **Cited in** line names those chapters. Nothing is added for completeness, and a listing does not mean every page of a document was read.
> * **Evidence types answer different questions.** Vendor documentation describes what a product does, standards and regulation say what is required, practitioner sources and books describe how work is commonly done, and news and analyst reports record events and opinions. Weigh each claim by the kind of source behind it.
> * **Keep documented facts, recommendations and Northmere distinct.** The sources support the facts. The architectural recommendations are my judgement, and Northmere, the group the book follows, is fictional: no source here describes it, and no source validates the recommendations.

> **[FAILURE PATTERNS THIS SECTION ADDRESSES](#where-finance-architecture-goes-wrong):**
>
> * **The Borrowed Roadmap** — Vendor documentation is the largest evidence type on this page, and it describes capability, not fit. Labeling it as vendor documentation keeps a product's own account from standing in for an independent judgement about whether it suits your estate.

This page lists the external sources the chapters cite. The research was conducted in **October 2026**, and most entries were consulted then; where a chapter recorded a specific access date, version or release (for example Oracle release 26C or a dated SAP announcement), the entry keeps it. Web access during the research was partly limited: **some vendor pages blocked automated access**, and those sources are cited as found, from the page title, the publisher's summary or a secondary report, with the limitation noted in the entry where the chapter recorded it. Vendor documentation is a living source. Pages are renamed, moved and revised between releases, so check a vendor page against the release you run before relying on a detail.

**R** marks a consulted source; the numbers identify, they do not rank. Identifiers run in topic order and stay stable: if a source is removed, its number is retired rather than reused, so a reference to an identifier keeps meaning the same document. Each entry gives the identifier, the linked title, the author or publisher, the year or version where known, one clause on what the source supports, the **evidence type** and the chapters that cite it. The register holds **442 sources**. The evidence types, with the number of sources of each, are:

| Evidence type | Sources | What it is | How to read it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Vendor documentation | 206 | Product documentation, learning material, release notes, press releases, trust pages and the vendor's own filings | Authoritative on what the product does and how the vendor describes it; silent on fit, cost of operation and alternatives |
| Standard or regulation | 36 | Accounting and audit standards, technical specifications and legal texts | Authoritative on requirements; an architecture applies them, and interpretation for a specific entity belongs to its auditors and advisers |
| Regulator or official | 19 | Regulator guidance, official announcements, court decisions and filings pages | Authoritative on the official position at its date; check for later updates |
| Practitioner | 142 | Articles, guides, engineering blog posts, conference talks and commentaries by practitioners, consultancies, law firms and implementers, plus encyclopedia entries | Describes common practice or interprets primary sources; secondary, so follow it to the primary source where one exists |
| Book | 14 | Books and book-length references | Durable concepts and vocabulary; editions matter |
| Research | 12 | Papers, experience reports and analyst research | Findings hold within the study's method and sample; analyst forecasts are opinions |
| News | 13 | Trade press and news reporting | Records events and claims at a date; not independent verification of a vendor's statements |

Some chapters also cite the book's own research briefs, written in October 2026 to collect and check the vendor, regulatory and practice facts the chapters use. Those briefs are working notes, not public evidence. They are not listed here, and a chapter that relies on them states so in its own sources section. Wherever a brief's fact matters, the chapter cites the public source behind it, and that source is listed below. Plain-language definitions of the terms used in the entries are in [Glossary](#glossary).

## Topic Index

Identifiers R001–R388 run in topic order, so each topic in the first edition of the register is one contiguous block. Sources added later take the next free numbers, from R389 onward, and sit under the topic they serve; the topic's paragraph lists them after its first range. A source cited by chapters in several topics sits under the topic it serves best; its **Cited in** line lists every chapter that uses it.

**Enterprise architecture practice (R001–R029, 29 sources).** The role, methods and vocabulary of enterprise architecture: frameworks, capability and portfolio models, decision records, and the organizational ideas (Conway's Law, team types) behind the operating model. Start with [The Chief Architect's Job: Making Many Systems Tell One Story](#chief-architect-role), [Map What Finance Does Before What Runs It](#capability-mapping) and [Architecture Governance That Speeds Decisions](#architecture-governance).

**Integration, data and ledger engineering (R030–R075, 46 sources).** Integration patterns, event streaming, idempotency, data contracts and lineage, master data, and the engineering of double-entry ledgers and money types. Start with [Files, Events and APIs: How Finance Systems Talk](#integration-architecture), [Never Update, Only Append: Designing Custom Ledgers](#designing-financial-ledgers) and [One Number, Many Views: Finance Data from Operations to Analytics](#finance-data-flows).

**Accounting, controls and regulation (R076–R112 and R389, 38 sources).** Accounting standards, internal control over financial reporting and its disclosure, audit standards, tax and e-invoicing regulation, data retention and erasure, and operational-resilience law. Start with [Accounting for Architects: Ledgers, Journals and Revenue](#accounting-for-architects), [Controls by Design, Not by Audit Season](#controls-and-auditability) and [Global by Design: Entities, Currencies, Jurisdictions and Brands](#global-scale).

**SAP (R113–R151, 39 sources).** SAP S/4HANA documentation, SAP learning material, release and maintenance announcements, and practitioner explanations of the Universal Journal, contract accounting and Central Finance. Start with [SAP S/4HANA: One Journal, Clean Core](#sap-s4hana).

**Workday (R152–R177, 26 sources).** Workday Financial Management, Accounting Center, integration limits, security model, release practice and company filings, plus university implementation guides. Start with [Workday: The Org Chart Behind the Ledger](#workday).

**Oracle (R178–R205, 28 sources).** Oracle Fusion Cloud Financials and Accounting Hub documentation: ledgers, reporting currencies, import and extraction interfaces, and lifecycle announcements. Start with [Oracle Fusion: An Accounting Hub for High-Volume Events](#oracle-fusion).

**BlackLine and close automation (R206–R227, 22 sources).** BlackLine filings, product and partner material, and the close-automation alternatives from SAP, Oracle, OneStream, Trintech and FloQast. Start with [BlackLine: Matching, Certifying and Closing](#blackline).

**OneStream and EPM (R228–R240, 13 sources).** OneStream ownership and filings, its dimensional model, and the consolidation products it is compared with. Start with [OneStream: Consolidating Actuals, Planning the Future](#onestream).

**Payments (R241–R309, 69 sources).** Payment-provider documentation for Adyen and Stripe, payment regulation in the EU, card and local-method schemes, bank statement formats, and orchestration. Start with [Payments for Finance Architects: Eleven Reasons the Numbers Disagree](#payments-fundamentals), [Adyen: One Platform from Checkout to Settlement](#adyen), [Stripe: Every Movement Is a Balance Transaction](#stripe) and [Payments-to-Ledger: Draining the Suspense Swamp](#payments-to-ledger).

**Satellite platforms (R310–R330, 21 sources).** Tax, treasury, billing, procurement and disclosure platforms, together with the e-invoicing mandates that shape them. Start with [Around the Core: Where Specialist Platforms Earn Their Place](#satellite-platforms).

**AI and automation (R331–R361, 31 sources).** Vendor AI and agent announcements, the Model Context Protocol, audit and control guidance for generative AI, EU AI Act changes and analyst surveys. Start with [Automate the Work, Not the Accountability](#ai-in-finance).

**Modernization, governance and strategy (R362–R384, 23 sources).** Migration and legacy-displacement patterns, evolutionary architecture, technical debt, resilience engineering and long-horizon strategy methods. Start with [Changing the Engine in Flight: Modernization Patterns for Finance](#modernization-patterns), [Technical Debt on the Balance Sheet](#technical-debt-and-fitness) and [Built to Be Replaced: Evolving the Finance Estate Over a Decade](#long-term-evolution).

**FinOps and technology spend (R414–R442, 29 sources).** The FinOps Framework and the FOCUS billing-data specification, provider documentation on billing timing, commitments, tags and marketplace purchases, the accounting and tax guidance on cloud arrangements and intra-group services, and the practitioner and survey sources on how technology spend is managed. Start with [FinOps Meets the Ledger: Technology Spend as Financial Data](#finops).

**Public case studies (R385–R388, 4 sources).** Public reporting on large finance-system implementations that went wrong, used as evidence that the failure patterns occur, not as accounts of internal design. Start with [Build, Buy or Compose: Choosing What to Own](#build-versus-buy) and [The Technology Is the Easy Part: Leading a Finance Transformation](#finance-transformation).

**Public case studies: Uber (R390–R413, 24 sources).** The Uber engineering talks and blog posts behind the Part V case study, from the 2018 payments-platform talks to the 2026 ten-year retrospective, with the author's 2019 analysis and the practitioner and tool sources it names. They record what Uber's engineers said at each date, not an independent account of the platform. Start with [Uber's Payments Platform: A Ledger at the Center of Money Movement](#case-uber-payments).
