# 12. Plan Investor Support: Match the Help to Company Priorities

![Plan Investor Support: Match the Help to Company Priorities — logo](private-techuity/posts/09-plan-investor-support/assets/images/plan-investor-support/logo.png)

> **IN THIS SECTION, YOU WILL:** Match business priorities to four ways of working with an investor, connect them to the company’s internal organization, and write a blueprint that makes authority, capacity and review explicit.

> **WHY INVESTORS CARE:** An investor pays for the help it offers, such as a pricing specialist or a technology partner, expecting it to raise the company’s value. Help aimed at a problem the company does not have, or never used, is money spent for nothing.

> **WHY YOU SHOULD CARE:** Every offer of help brings extra work for your people: meetings, data requests, integration and follow-up. Unless someone decides what the help is for, who directs it and which planned work makes room, it lands on the roadmap’s engineers, and the roadmap slips.

> **KEY POINTS:**
>
> * **Choose the arrangement based on the work and the missing capability.** Management may need a decision, experienced judgment, specialist expertise or someone with time to lead execution. Each calls for a different contribution.
> * **Design investor involvement and company organization separately, then connect them.** Shared investor expertise can support locally managed businesses. A company can centralize a service while its investor works mainly through the board.
> * **A usable blueprint includes commitments from both sides.** Name who decides, who supplies people and funding, which company work is set aside to make room, and when the arrangement will be reviewed or ended.

> **[DYSFUNCTIONS THIS SECTION ADDRESSES](#where-investment-goes-wrong):**
>
> * **The Nodding Room** — Connects business priorities to explicit commitments from both the investor and the company.
> * **The Accidental Gatekeeper** — Separates oversight, advice and delivery roles, with authority agreed for each.
> * **The Ratchet Roadmap** — Names which company work must make room for the support being offered.

An investor, a firm that has put money into the business expecting a financial return, offers the company “**the full operating platform**”: access to a technology partner, a pricing specialist, an acquisition team and a standard reporting package. The chief executive officer, the person who leads the company overall, welcomes the help. The chief technology officer, the executive responsible for technology, sees four new streams of work for the same engineers: connecting to the partner’s systems, changing pricing in the product, preparing data on companies to buy, and producing the investor’s reports. Before anyone adds that work to the roadmap (the product work the company has planned, and when it expects to do it), the company must settle two things. First, **what would the offer make possible**: which goal does each piece of help serve, and is it one the company has? Second, **who decides**: who chooses which help to accept, who directs it, and which planned work is dropped or delayed to make room?

An **operating model** is how a business is set up to carry out its strategy: who does which work, who decides what, and how information reaches the people who need it. A **blueprint** writes that down for one piece of work, specifically enough that people can discuss it and check it against what happens. For the pricing specialist, for example, it would say what the specialist works on, who in the company directs the work, which engineers implement the changes, how much of their time it takes, who approves the new prices, and when everyone reviews whether it is working.

The chapter runs in four steps. It first separates two designs: how **the investor takes part** (overseeing, advising, lending specialists or doing some of the work) and how **the company organizes itself** (which decisions stay with each business, which standards and services are shared). It then shows which combinations suit which **business priority**, such as growth, profitability, acquisitions or scaling. Next it describes four blueprints for investor involvement, from board oversight alone to investor people working inside the company. Finally, it follows a fictional group as it chooses a combination and writes a blueprint you can reuse. The four blueprints are the book’s own synthesis, meant to be adapted. They are neither a standard industry classification nor a ranking of investors. The closing chapter of Part III, [Work With Technology Operating Partners](#technology-operating-partners), examines one role within them, the technology operating partner; this chapter covers the wider working arrangement.

## Connect Two Designs

Working with an investor involves two designs. The first concerns **how the investor works with the company**: **oversight**, **advice**, specialist **support**, and any responsibility given to the investor’s people who join in delivering the work. The second concerns **how the company organizes its work**: which **decisions** remain with product or business-unit leaders, which **standards** are common, and which services or teams operate across units.

The investor also has an **internal operating model** for running its own business: keeping the fund’s records and accounts, reporting to the people whose money it holds, and running its own systems. That is a separate design, and the company does not need to understand it in full. It matters where it reaches into the company. If the investor reports to its own investors every quarter, the company may have to deliver figures in the investor’s format and by the investor’s deadline. If the investor uses its own reporting system, someone in the company has to feed it. Both are work that lands on the company’s finance and engineering teams. [S92: McKinsey, operational complexity](https://www.mckinsey.com/industries/private-capital/our-insights/how-private-equity-is-tackling-operational-complexity)

To connect the two designs, start with two investor documents: the **investment thesis**, which explains why the investment should succeed, and the **value creation plan**, which lists the business changes meant to make it succeed. Turn each important assumption in them into the company work it requires. Suppose the plan assumes that customers will accept a 10% price rise: someone must redesign the price list, engineers must change billing, and sales must handle the customers who object. Different assumptions demand different things. Growing an existing product draws mainly on **product judgment**; cutting the cost of serving customers draws on **engineering capacity**; integrating acquired companies draws heavily on management attention. Bain’s February 2026 analysis argues for starting this work early, by examining commercial, operational and technology opportunities together during **diligence**, the investigation an investor runs before committing money. [S94: Bain, Welcome to a New Era in Private Equity](https://www.bain.com/insights/welcome-to-a-new-era-global-private-equity-report-2026/)

For each proposed improvement, identify **what is missing**. A **decision blocked** at the board (the group of directors that governs the company, oversees its direction and approves its largest decisions) needs an approval route. A technology officer facing an **unfamiliar acquisition**, the purchase of another business, may need experienced advice. A team testing whether an **artificial intelligence (AI)** feature, such as one that reads a customer’s written complaint and proposes an answer, is good enough to put in front of customers may need a specialist. A company **managing several integrations** at once may need dedicated execution capacity.

## Fit Both Designs to the Business Priority

Start from the **business priority** and ask what work it requires, then ask **what the company lacks** to do that work: a decision, experienced judgment, specialist skill or people with time to lead it. Choose **investor support for that specific gap**, not the whole platform on offer. A company growing its existing product with a capable team may need nothing more than board oversight; one cutting costs without anyone who knows how to renegotiate cloud contracts may need a specialist for a few months. The numbers in the table refer to the four blueprints described in the next section. Each row is a starting point to test against your own situation, not a prescription.

| Business priority | Choose investor involvement for the gap | Connect it to company work |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Grow the existing business | **1: Board oversight** when the team can execute. **2: An operating adviser** when product, pricing or market assumptions need experienced challenge. | Give the **chief product officer** (the executive responsible for product direction) and the technology officer clear authority over investment and roadmap decisions. Fund both discovering what customers need and building it; review adoption and retention alongside revenue. |
| Improve **profitability**, the ability to earn more revenue than the costs of earning it | **3: Shared specialists** for scarce pricing, purchasing or technology-cost expertise. **4: Dedicated execution** when implementation exceeds available management capacity. | Assign each change to the executive who controls the work. Before expanding shared services, test the full implementation and running costs, the effect on cash and the service quality. |
| Grow by buying other businesses | **2: An operating adviser** to help choose how deeply to combine the businesses. **4: Dedicated execution** when several integrations need sustained coordination. | Decide which benefits require common standards or shared services and which depend on local product teams. Fund the work of moving customers and systems across, alongside continuing delivery. |
| Scale beyond founder dependence | **2: An operating adviser** to help establish delegation and leadership capability. **3: Shared specialists** for recruitment, security or other specific gaps. | Give each area a permanent owner inside the company, and build clear product-team responsibilities and repeatable delivery practices. Review decision delays, leadership coverage and service reliability. |

The table uses a few financial and operating terms. **Revenue** is the income a company earns by supplying its products or services, counted **when the company delivers** them rather than when the money arrives. **Profit** is what remains after the costs of earning that revenue. **Cash** is the money actually in the company’s bank account, which arrives and leaves on its own timetable. Revenue and cash receipts can diverge in either direction: work delivered in March may not be paid for until June, while a customer who pays for a year’s subscription upfront hands over the cash long before the company has earned most of it. Both sales are commitments; only one has reached the bank account so far.

The table also uses two operating measures: **adoption**, how many customers start using a product or feature, and **retention**, how many keep using it. Either can be stated as a count of customers or the revenue they represent, so say which one you mean.

The same growth goal could need an **adviser** to test a product decision, a **specialist** to evaluate an AI feature, or a **temporary leader** to cover a vacancy. Conversely, one specialist team could support both growth and cost work. Choose based on the constraint, then confirm the people, authority, funding and review date before adding the work to the plan. If the required capacity cannot be committed, narrow the work or sequence it.

## Four Blueprints for Investor Involvement

Investors organize this help very differently. Many have an **operating group**: a team whose job is to help the companies the investor owns, rather than to find new investments. McKinsey’s November 2018 survey of 45 firms found no standard make-up for these groups; they combine the investor’s own **internal employees** and **external advisers** in varied ways. Its 2021 guidance for chief executives describes the forms the help can take: **operating teams**, **functional experts**, **senior advisers** and **networks of peers**. For a company leader, that variety makes “the investor will help” too loose to plan around: it could mean a weekly call with an adviser or a team of specialists working inside the company for months. The four designs below turn that variety into practical choices. [S91: McKinsey, operating groups](https://www.mckinsey.com/industries/private-capital/our-insights/private-equity-operating-groups-and-the-pursuit-of-portfolio-alpha) [S95: McKinsey, portfolio-company CEOs](https://www.mckinsey.com/industries/private-capital/our-insights/a-playbook-for-newly-minted-private-equity-portfolio-company-ceos)

A company does not have to pick one blueprint for everything. It can use different ones for different work: **board oversight** for the core product, say, and a **shared pricing specialist** for a six-month pricing project. Each arrangement can also end on its own; when the pricing project is done, the specialist leaves while the investor stays on the board. Review the mix whenever the business priority or the missing capability changes: an acquisition, a new chief executive or a missed target can each call for a different arrangement.

{id: plan-investor-support--blueprint-1-board-oversight}
### Blueprint 1: Board Oversight

This model fits a company whose **leaders have the capability and capacity** to execute an agreed plan. The investor contributes through the board, investment decisions and selected discussions. Product and technology leaders turn the plan into priorities, staffing and delivery through the company’s management structure.

![The board agrees the plan and reviews it with company leaders, who make delivery decisions and allocate funded product and engineering capacity.](private-techuity/posts/09-plan-investor-support/assets/images/plan-investor-support/blueprint-1-board-oversight.jpeg)

**Figure 2:** *Board oversight works through an agreed plan and review. Company leaders retain delegated delivery decisions and need funded people to carry them out.*

**Authority and resources.** Write down which decisions management can take alone and which need the board’s or someone else’s approval. Name at least three: **spending outside the budget** (the spending and income plan the board has approved for the year), **significant changes of direction** such as dropping a product line, and **senior appointments** such as a new head of engineering. For each, record who approves and how quickly. Management also needs a funded plan and people available to deliver it. Help from the investor counts as a resource only once it is specific. “You can call on our network” is not a resource; “our former head of sales will spend two days a month coaching your sales lead until June” is.

**Working practice.** Agree which figures the board sees each month, for example revenue, cash, hiring against plan and progress on the main initiatives, so that every discussion starts from the same numbers. Agree too how a decision gets made between board meetings: who management contacts, in what form, and how fast an answer is due. McKinsey’s May 2021 interviews describe private-equity boards that engage closely between meetings. That can coexist with management leading delivery, provided everyone knows which of those contacts are advice and which are decisions. Otherwise a board member’s passing suggestion can redirect a team as if it were an instruction. [S97: McKinsey, the private-equity learning curve](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/strategy-and-corporate-finance/our-insights/climbing-the-private-equity-learning-curve)

For a product team, this might work as follows. The board agrees what growth it is funding and within what limits: for example, raise sales to existing customers by 20% this year, with no more than four new engineers. The product and technology officers then decide how to get there: which features to test, which experiments to run and which engineering changes to make. They go back to the board only in two cases: when the evidence shows an **assumption was wrong**, such as customers not buying the new add-on, or when meeting the goal would **need more money** than they are allowed to approve.

**Review and change.** At each review, check three things: how **customers and the business** are doing, whether **past forecasts** turned out accurate, and whether the **decisions management** needed arrived in time. Then match the fix to the cause. If approvals keep arriving weeks late, change the approval route, for example by raising the amount management may spend without asking. If the same technical problem keeps coming back, such as rising cloud costs nobody in the company knows how to cut, bring in an adviser or specialist. If the team simply has too much to do, cut the work or add people. Find the cause first; adding more board reporting rarely fixes any of these and usually takes more management time.

{id: plan-investor-support--blueprint-2-an-operating-adviser}
### Blueprint 2: An Operating Adviser

An **operating adviser** is an experienced person, often a former executive, whom the investor provides to **advise and challenge** the company’s leaders. The adviser does not do the work; the company’s own people still do it. This blueprint fits when the company has the people to do the work but **lacks experience for one specific, time-limited problem**. An adviser might help a first-time technology officer judge whether an acquired company’s software can be merged with its own, test a product officer’s case for a large product investment, or coach a leadership team through a change it has not made before, such as its first round of layoffs.

![An operating adviser contributes judgment under an agreed remit; a company leader makes the decision and the company team supplies implementation capacity.](private-techuity/posts/09-plan-investor-support/assets/images/plan-investor-support/blueprint-2-operating-adviser.jpeg)

**Figure 3:** *The adviser contributes experience and challenge to a company decision. Management keeps the agreed authority and commits the people needed for implementation.*

**Authority and resources.** Name the company executive who owns the decision and the work; the adviser supports that person and **does not replace them**. Then agree the adviser’s **remit**, the scope and limits of their work: which question they help with, how many days a month they are available, which documents and people they may see, who pays for their time, and what they are expected to produce, such as a recommendation by a set date. Also make clear whether the adviser **reports back to the investor** on how well management is performing. The three roles are different. Advice can be ignored; an evaluation can affect an executive’s job; an approval can stop a plan. Leaders speak more openly to someone who only advises than to someone who is also judging them. The written agreement recording all this, the **charter**, belongs in [Set the Terms of Help: Agree the Work, Authority and Handover](#set-terms-of-help).

**Working practice.** Attach the adviser to a decision the company actually has to make, and pair them with the company person who owns it. General coaching with no decision attached tends to produce opinions nobody acts on. Suppose the company has bought a smaller rival. The technology officer and the adviser compare the options: keep both products, move the rival’s customers onto the company’s product, or merge the two. They test each option against the same facts: which systems depend on which, what has been promised to customers, and how many engineers are free. The adviser brings **experience of past mergers** and **challenges weak assumptions**; the technology officer assigns the people and makes the decision within their authority. Success is a decision the technology officer can defend, and a team that knows how to carry it out after the adviser has gone.

A broad calendar invitation or a monthly presentation supplies little evidence of that contribution. Agree what the next review will examine: a **resolved uncertainty**, a **better option**, a **capability learned** or a **decision ready** for approval.

**Review and change.** Watch for two warning signs. The first is a **growing pile of recommendations**: the adviser suggests ten changes a month and the team can implement two. The second is teams treating the adviser’s **suggestions as orders**, dropping planned work because “the investor’s person said so”. Possible fixes: narrow the adviser’s brief to the one or two questions that matter most; pay for specialists to do the work the recommendations require; or, if the company really needs someone to lead the work, appoint an executive with formal authority. When the agreed problem is solved, end the advisory work. If the adviser stays on as a coach, agree that as a new arrangement with its own terms, rather than letting the old one drift on. [Clarify the Adviser’s Role: Are They Helping, Assessing or Deciding?](#clarify-adviser-role) explains how to handle a change of role.

### Blueprint 3: Shared Specialists

This blueprint fits expertise that a company needs again and again, but not enough to employ a full-time expert. A company of 200 people may need a security review twice a year, a pricing analysis before each price change, or a recruiter only when it hires a senior executive. Other examples are building and maintaining the systems that hold the company’s data, and testing whether an AI system works well enough for its intended use. An investor that owns several such companies can share one set of specialists across them, so each company gets the expertise without carrying its full cost. The specialists may work for the investor itself, for an outside firm the investor has contracted, or under another service arrangement, provided everyone has agreed in advance who supplies them and on what terms.

![Shared specialists have competing requests; a named assignment agrees people, dates and cost with an assignment owner inside the company, whose local team implements and operates the result.](private-techuity/posts/09-plan-investor-support/assets/images/plan-investor-support/blueprint-3-shared-specialists.jpeg)

**Figure 4:** *Shared expertise becomes usable through a specific assignment. Agree people, dates and cost, with a named company lead and local capacity to use the work.*

**Authority and resources.** Get a **named specialist and dates**, not just “access to our security team”: for example, “Maria, five days in March, to review our customer data handling”. Then settle four questions. When several companies want the same specialist in the same month, who decides who goes first? Who pays for the specialist’s time? What must the company provide, such as data, documents or engineers’ time? And who in the company signs off that the work is done? A specialist also needs a **counterpart in the company** with time to explain how things work there and to act on the result; without one, the report goes into a drawer. Finally, if the investor requires all its companies to follow a common policy, such as one security standard, find out who set it, who can grant an exception, and how to ask for one.

**Working practice.** Shared specialists can provide two very different things, and they need different arrangements. The first is a **reusable method**: a way of doing something that each company takes and adapts. A standard set of tests for checking an AI system, for example, can be copied by each company and adjusted to its own product; once handed over, it needs no shared upkeep. The second is a **shared operational service**: a running system several companies depend on every day. An identity service, the system that checks who a user is and lets one sign-in work across several products, is an example. If it fails, customers of every company using it cannot log in. So it needs a named owner, agreed standards such as how quickly problems are fixed, someone to call when it breaks, and a budget that keeps paying for it year after year.

Some investors build **large shared-specialist teams**. Vista Equity Partners, a firm that invests in software companies, describes on its value-creation page (consulted in September 2026) **in-house AI engineers** who work alongside the companies it owns, **standard operating methods**, and **peer learning** between companies. That shows what such a platform can offer. It does not show that any particular company needs all of it. Take only the parts that fill a gap in your own plan, and agree terms for each as described above. [S93: Vista, Value Creation](https://www.vistaequitypartners.com/about/value-creation/)

Take a **pilot**, a limited trial before wider use, of an AI feature that drafts replies to customer support requests. Shared specialists might **design the tests** that show whether the drafts are good enough, and work out how the feature would connect to the existing support system. They cannot supply the rest. The product officer must still say **which customer problem** the feature solves and how success will be judged. The technology officer must still **find engineers** to build the feature and keep it running after the specialists leave. And **data access** must be agreed for this assignment specifically. Being owned by the same investor does not by itself allow the specialists to see one company’s customer data, or to combine it with another company’s data to train or test the AI.

**Review and change.** At each review, ask four questions. Were the specialists available when the company needed them? How much of their work did the company actually accept and use? What did it all cost, including the company’s own people’s time? And can the company now do any of this itself, or is it as dependent as before? Three signs call for a change: the **company waits** weeks in a queue behind other companies; it must use a tool the investor mandates even though the **tool does not suit its business**; or it still **cannot work without experts** who are rarely free. The fix might be a written commitment on response times, hiring its own specialist, switching to another provider, or stopping work that no longer earns its cost.

### Blueprint 4: A Temporary Leader or Team

This blueprint fits work that needs someone leading or coordinating it **full-time for months**, when the company’s own leaders have no time to spare. Examples are merging an acquired company’s systems and teams, moving the business onto a new billing or customer-management system, or covering for a chief financial officer who has left until a permanent one is hired. Three kinds of help fit here, and they are not interchangeable. A **program leader** coordinates many related pieces of work, such as the twenty projects of an integration, without managing the people doing them. An **embedded** delivery team joins the company’s own teams and does part of the work alongside them. An **interim**, or temporary, executive fills a vacant leadership role and holds its authority until a permanent hire arrives. Decide which one the work needs and describe that role specifically. This is the arrangement Figure 1 labels *Embedded*.

![An illustrative program leader reports to the company’s chief executive officer, labelled CEO in the diagram, and works with assigned people inside the company, with scope, budget and a planned handover to a permanent owner; investor review runs through the same chief executive.](private-techuity/posts/09-plan-investor-support/assets/images/plan-investor-support/blueprint-4-dedicated-execution.jpeg)

**Figure 5:** *In this illustrative program assignment, the company agrees the reporting line, authority, people and budget. The diagram’s CEO is the chief executive officer. A planned handover keeps continuing ownership visible.*

McKinsey’s June 2026 article discusses adding leadership for major change inside the companies investors own, and the burden on executives who must lead that change while still running the business. The role it discusses, the **chief transformation officer**, is an executive appointed to lead one large program of business change, such as a cost reduction across the whole company. Do not confuse it with the chief technology officer, which shares the abbreviation CTO. Whether your company needs such a role depends on the specific work. Ask whether the program is too big for current leaders to run alongside their jobs, and whether a dedicated leader would deliver enough to justify the cost. [S99: McKinsey, five practices reshaping PE value creation](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/transformation/our-insights/unlocking-full-potential-five-practices-reshaping-pe-value-creation)

**Authority and resources.** Write down four things for the appointee: whom **they report to**, what **budget they control**, which **decisions they may take** alone, and **what work** their people will do. The answers differ by role. A program leader may keep track of how the pieces of work depend on each other and chase delays, but cannot tell the sales or engineering head what to prioritize. An interim technology officer, if formally appointed, can hold the role’s executive authority, such as setting engineering priorities and hiring. Also decide two things before work starts. How does the chief executive settle disputes, for example when the program leader and the head of sales both want the same engineers? And who stays responsible for keeping existing customers’ services running while the change is under way?

**Working practice.** Put the assignment **into the company’s main plan**, not into a separate plan run by the investor. Show which employees work on it and for how much of their time, who takes over their usual work, and which other commitments move later as a result: if three engineers join the integration, say which product release slips. Hold progress reviews on three questions: what is finished, what is still waiting on something else, and which decisions are needed now. Report to the investor from **the same review**, so there is one set of facts rather than two versions of progress. Adding people does not automatically add capacity: newcomers need time to learn the company’s systems and take time from the people who teach them. [Assess Capability: Can the Team Deliver?](#assess-capability) and [Scale the Team Up: Headcount Is Not Capacity](#scale-the-team-up) show how to tell the difference.

**Review and change.** Agree two things at the start: a date for the **first review**, and the condition for **handing the work back** to the company, for example “when the two billing systems have run as one for three months without a major failure”. At each review, check three things: is the **work progressing**, are existing customer **services stable**, and can the company’s permanent staff **keep the change running** once the temporary people leave? Watch for two failures. A **program office**, a small team that helps plan and track the work, can end up only collecting status updates while nobody is free to do the work itself. And an interim leader can become someone the company cannot do without, with no successor in sight. Whether to extend, change or end the assignment is decided by whoever approved it, with its cost and the work it has pushed aside shown in full.

## Decide What the Company Will Share

A separate question is how much the company should **centralize**: which work and decisions it runs once for everyone, and which it leaves to each business or product. The investor’s involvement does not settle this. An investor that only sits on the board can own a company that runs one shared engineering team; an investor that lends many specialists can own one whose businesses each run everything themselves. The question comes up most clearly when acquisitions turn one company into a group of businesses: should they share one finance system, one sales team, one product platform? But it applies equally to product divisions within a single company.

**Several businesses owned by the same investor are not necessarily one group.** A **subsidiary** is a company controlled by a parent company, which can direct it; a set of such companies under one parent is a **corporate group**. Two software companies that happen to share an investor need not be one: neither can instruct the other, and they have no shared management unless someone deliberately creates one. That makes two kinds of cooperation very different. Companies with the same investor might **share lessons at peer meetings**, or **buy cloud services together** on a discount the investor negotiated; each still decides for itself. Moving a function such as payroll or security into a group’s shared service is a much bigger step: one business now depends on another for work it used to do itself. Either way, agree in writing who pays what, who is responsible when something goes wrong, and which information each side may see.

The following company designs can coexist across different activities:

| Company design | Where work and decisions sit | What must be made explicit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Local operation | Business units retain the activity, people and most decisions. | Required reporting, minimum controls and the decisions reserved elsewhere. |
| Common standards with local delivery | Units choose implementation within shared rules; this is often called a federated arrangement. | Who sets the rules, funds the work of following them, resolves exceptions and maintains the connection points between systems. |
| Shared service or integrated function | A group team performs a defined activity for several units. | Service ownership, funding, priority-setting, service quality and the authority left with unit leaders. |

Start with the **specific benefit** that sharing would bring, and share only what that benefit requires. Three examples. If the owners want to compare the businesses’ results side by side, the businesses must define their figures the same way (what counts as a customer, when revenue is recorded) and agree in writing who supplies which data, in what format and to what quality. If a customer has to use two of the group’s products to get one job done, the products may need a single sign-in and a way to pass data between them. If the group wants a lower price from a cloud provider, the businesses may need to sign one joint contract instead of several separate ones. None of these requires merging teams or systems beyond that point.

Sharing **changes who is responsible** for what. Suppose a central group team now chooses and pays for the cloud servers that one business unit’s product runs on. The unit’s leader can no longer control that cost, or how reliably the servers run. So judge the unit leader on what they still control, such as sales, customer retention and product delivery, and not on server costs or outages that the central team decides. In turn, the central service needs its own named owner, judged on its cost and reliability. Otherwise, when the product goes down, each side blames the other. The choices about how far to combine acquired businesses, and what the transition costs, are developed in [Plan Acquisitions and Separations: Account for Extra Work, Not Just Expected Value](#plan-acquisitions-separations).

## Keep One Plan, Reviewed as Often as Its Decisions Need

Investor help works best when it feeds into one plan that the company and the investor have built together, rather than into a separate investor plan alongside the company’s own. Spencer Stuart’s account of the relationship between investors and chief executives recommends building the improvement plan together, and agreeing which measures will track it and what reporting the investor requires. The plan then needs regular reviews, and how often depends on how quickly its decisions have to be made. The rhythm below, weekly, monthly and periodic, is the book’s suggested starting point for a company whose business is reasonably stable. A company in crisis or in the middle of an acquisition may need to review more often. [S96: Spencer Stuart, Partners in Value Creation](https://www.spencerstuart.com/research-and-insight/partners-in-value-creation-unlocking-the-vital-bond-between-investors-and-ceos)

Each review looks at different things. A **weekly company review**, among the executives, covers only what has gone off track since last week: a late release, a service outage, an unhappy major customer, an unexpected cash shortfall. It also clears decisions that are holding up priority work. A **monthly business review**, which the investor may join, looks at results against the plan, the latest forecast for the year, what the changes under way are costing, and any decisions that need the board or the investor. A **periodic strategy review**, perhaps twice a year, steps back to ask whether the reasons for the investment still hold and whether the working arrangement with the investor still fits. If the company already holds meetings that do these jobs, use them rather than adding new ones.

Reviews go wrong when people **mix up three different numbers**. The **budget** is the financial plan the board approved: what the company said it would earn and spend this year. The **forecast** is the current best estimate of what will actually happen, updated as results come in. A **stretch target** is a more ambitious number that depends on extra actions succeeding or conditions turning out well; it is a goal, not an expectation. If the budget says €20 million revenue, the forecast now says €19 million and the stretch target is €22 million, a review that treats €22 million as the plan will judge a good team as failing. For every measure that matters, name one owner and write down exactly how it is calculated. And never show a financial figure on its own; pair it with the operating facts that explain it. Show **recurring revenue** (the part the company earns again each period from continuing arrangements such as subscriptions, rather than one-off sales) next to how many customers renew and how much they use the product. Show the cost of running a service next to how much work it handles and how reliably it runs. Show spending next to the future commitments it creates, such as multi-year contracts.

In a **crisis**, the rhythm must speed up. If the company is struggling to pay suppliers, salaries or lenders on time, or a serious service failure is losing customers, decisions may be needed **daily rather than monthly**. McKinsey’s guidance for the **chief financial officer**, the executive responsible for the company’s money, stresses understanding three things where they are relevant: **cash flow**, the money coming in and going out over a period; the **balance sheet**, the statement of what the company owns and owes on one date; and **debt conditions**, the requirements a lender attaches to borrowed money, such as keeping earnings above an agreed level. These limit what the operating plan can do. A product improvement that pays back in eighteen months is no use if the cash runs short in six. So every proposed improvement should show when it needs money and when it returns it, next to when cash will actually be available; [Understand Cash Flow: Confirm the Cash Before You Commit](#understand-cash-flow) explains why profit and cash differ. [S98: McKinsey, the PE company CFO](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/strategy-and-corporate-finance/our-insights/the-pe-company-cfo-essentials-for-success)

Agree in advance which events **bypass the normal rhythm** and go straight to the decision-makers, so nobody waits for the next monthly review while a problem grows. Typical **escalation triggers** are: promised **funding arriving late**, a **service outage** that affects many customers, a **specialist** the plan depends on becoming **unavailable**, or evidence that a key assumption in the plan **no longer holds**. For each trigger, name who must respond and how fast, for example “the chief executive and the investor’s board member, within two working days”. A useful escalation says three things: what will happen **if nothing is decided**, **which decision** is needed, and what is **still unknown**. “The data migration has slipped four weeks; unless we move two engineers from the new feature, we miss the customer’s contract date; we do not yet know whether the feature can wait” is an escalation. “The migration is at risk” is not.

## Example: Three Software Businesses Decide What to Share

Here is an **independent fictional example**, separate from the Rotaline story. An investor owns a group of three software businesses. Its plan assumes the group can win larger customers by selling them all **three products together**, as one package. Today, though, each business runs separately: its own product leaders, its own promises to customers, its own release dates. Before anyone has checked what joining the products up would actually require, someone proposes building one common **platform**: a shared technical foundation that all three products would be rebuilt to sit on. That is a large, multi-year engineering commitment, proposed before anyone knows whether customers need it.

Instead of starting with the platform, the group chief executive and the leaders of the three businesses first **agree what problem customers have**, in words a customer would use. Today a customer using two of the group’s products has two accounts, signs in twice and calls two different help desks. What they want is one sign-in that works for both products and one place to ask for help. That problem needs far less than a common platform. So the group decides three things. Each business keeps its own product plan and release dates. All three adopt the same security rules and report their figures the same way. And together they investigate a **shared identity service**: one system that checks who a user is and lets them into both products with a single sign-in. Rebuilding all three products on one technical foundation is not rejected, only postponed: it stays an option to evaluate once the shared sign-in shows whether customers value the products working together.

The group starts from the table earlier in this chapter, in the row for growing by buying other businesses, since the group was **assembled through acquisitions**. It is missing two things: nobody in the three businesses knows how to build an identity service, and nobody has time to coordinate work across three teams. So it combines Blueprint 3 (a shared specialist, for the identity expertise) with Blueprint 4 (a temporary leader, for the coordination), while the board keeps its usual oversight (Blueprint 1). Concretely:

- **The specialist:** a shared identity specialist from the investor is committed for six weeks.
- **The leader:** a company program leader is given a twelve-week assignment to coordinate the work and run the pilot, reporting to the group chief executive.
- **The engineers:** two named company engineers each set aside one day a week during the specialist’s six weeks, twelve engineer-days in total. Their managers postpone specific internal improvements to free that time.
- **The money:** before work starts, the chief financial officer confirms there is funding for the specialist and for the expected cost of running the service afterwards. The program leader’s twelve weeks and the engineers’ days need no new money: they are salaries already in the approved budget, freed by the postponed improvements.

Who decides what is settled before work starts. **Product leaders** keep deciding their own product plans, as before. **The program leader** decides the order of the pilot’s steps, but cannot take extra engineers or change what has been promised to customers; those conflicts go to the group chief executive. **Going live**, letting real customers use the new sign-in, needs a separate approval. In this group the **chief technology officer** gives it, after checking that the service passes the company’s security and reliability tests. That is a one-time yes or no. It is different from the **service owner**: the named manager who, once the service is live, is responsible for keeping it running every day, handling failures and paying for it. The group will not let anything go live until that owner is named. Another company could give the go-live approval to someone else, such as the head of security; what matters is that a named person holds it, not a committee or a checklist that nobody owns.

At the six-week review, the pilot works, but only for a handful of test accounts: those users can sign in once and reach both products. Making it work for every customer of both products would mean **migrating** them, moving each existing account onto the new service. That migration would need the same engineers the group has already promised to a release for a customer. The group cannot do both. The specialist’s six weeks are also over and will not be extended, so from week seven the group can take on only work its own engineers know how to do without outside help.

The group chief executive, within the **agreed delegation**, defers the wider migration and approves a smaller next step. Over weeks seven to ten, the two engineers keep the same one day a week each — eight more engineer-days — to run the single sign-in with two volunteer customers and measure what running it costs. Their managers hold the same internal improvements deferred for those four weeks. The program leader’s existing twelve-week assignment covers the coordination, and no new funding is requested: the service costs for two customers fall inside the amount already approved.

Even this small trial has an **owner and an end**. The **service owner** is the manager of one product’s existing support team. If either volunteer customer has a problem, they contact that team the usual way; only a problem in the sign-in service itself goes to the two engineers, within their reserved days. The trial is approved until week ten and no longer. At week ten, both customers go back to their old separate sign-ins. They made that a condition of volunteering, and switching them back takes about one engineer-day, already counted within the eight reserved days. So the trial ends cleanly whatever the later review decides: when the engineers’ reserved time runs out, no customer is left on a service that nobody supports.

The decision also changes the numbers, and the board sees that straight away in a **revised forecast**, the current best estimate that replaces what the budget assumed. The plan had been for the combined offer to reach **general availability**, open to every intended customer rather than a few trial users, in the last quarter of the group’s financial year. The new estimate puts it one quarter later, in the first quarter of the next financial year. So revenue the budget counted on earning this year now arrives next year, and this year’s results will fall short of the budget by that amount. Even the new date is an estimate, not a promise: nobody has yet funded the wider migration, and that depends on decisions the twelve-week review has not yet taken. Three pieces of evidence will shape the next proposal: whether the pilot worked technically, what the two volunteer customers say, and a full estimate of what running the service will cost.

In weeks eleven and twelve, nothing is built and no customer uses the new sign-in; the engineers are back on their usual work. The program leader uses the two weeks to write the next proposal for the twelve-week review. It sets out what the next step should be, for example migrating one product’s customers first, and exactly what it would take: which people, for how many days, and how much money, both to build it and to run it each year.

At that review, management can decide whether the program assignment should end, continue with a narrower scope, or be replaced by a permanent group capability. Restarting the service for customers would be one of those decisions, with its own approval and its own committed people and funding; nothing beyond week twelve is reserved. The example shows only a sequence of decisions. It claims nothing about **revenue actually earned**, meaning income from products or services already supplied rather than income forecast. Nor does it claim anything about **investment returns**, the investor’s eventual gain or loss on the money it put in.

## Write Down the Arrangement in a Short Blueprint

Capture the arrangement in a **short written record** that both the company and the investor read and can question: if the investor thinks the company has under-resourced the work, or the company thinks a promised specialist never appeared, the record is where that shows. The six fields below are the book’s proposal for what the record should contain. Fill them in alongside the engagement and initiative records in [Practical Tools for Ownership and Technology Decisions](#toolkit), which track each piece of investor help and each piece of company work:

1. **Purpose and boundary.** The business priority, assumption to test or outcome to improve; the chosen combination of investor involvement and company design; the companies, functions and decisions covered.
2. **Work and ownership.** The accountable company executive, people doing the work, dependencies and continuing service owner.
3. **Authority.** Decisions each person can make, matters requiring consultation or approval, and the route for resolving disagreement. Check the record against actual delegations and governing arrangements.
4. **Resources and cost.** Investor commitments, company capacity, funding source, charges, implementation cost and the work displaced. Record when each contribution becomes available.
5. **Evidence and review.** Starting position, outcome measures, reporting definitions, review dates and triggers for escalation or a change of plan.
6. **Continuation or exit.** Handover requirements, capabilities to retain, dependencies to remove and who can extend or end the arrangement.

Fill in the **cost field** even when the investor presents the help as part of its platform, as if it were free. Someone pays for it, and it may be the company. The investor guide published by the **U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC)**, the United States regulator for investments, names two areas where competing interests can arise: deciding who bears a cost, and payments a company the fund owns makes for services. Here is a concrete case. The investor recommends a security provider; the company pays that provider; and the investor also earns a fee from the provider. The recommendation may still be the right one, but the company should know about the investor’s fee before accepting it. So for each piece of support, ask three questions: who pays, how much, and does the investor or anyone connected to it earn anything from the arrangement? [S01: SEC, Private Equity Funds](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/investment-products/private-investment-funds/private-equity)

For AI work, the record needs five more entries. **Data:** which data the system may use, for example the company’s own support tickets but not another company’s. **Testing:** who checks that the system works well enough, and against what standard. **Human review:** what a person checks before a result reaches a customer or a decision, such as reading every drafted reply before it is sent. **Running cost:** what it costs each month to operate, which can grow with use. **Operation:** who keeps it running once it is live. A shared specialist team can bring methods and rare expertise, but it cannot decide why the company needs the system or take responsibility for what it does; the company must do both. [Clarify AI Strategy: Three Different Investment Questions](#clarify-ai-strategy) and [Work With Technology Operating Partners](#technology-operating-partners) develop those choices.

A written blueprint does not tell you how the investor’s people will behave once the work starts. Spencer Stuart’s survey of 1,015 senior leaders reports that respondents value the **investment firm’s culture** and its approach to working in partnership. But the survey records what leaders say they want; it does not show that a written blueprint leads to better results. So check behavior directly. Before agreeing the arrangement, talk to executives at other companies who have worked with the same investor team, and ask three things: who actually made decisions, whether the promised people turned up when needed, and what happened when the company and the investor disagreed. [S100: Spencer Stuart, private equity and top leaders](https://www.spencerstuart.com/research-and-insight/private-equity-is-still-a-magnet-for-top-leaders)

## Adapt the Blueprint to the Ownership Arrangement

The same blueprint works for other kinds of investment, but the kind of investor does not tell you who has authority over what. Three common kinds: **venture** investment backs young businesses whose growth is uncertain but could be large; **growth** investment helps an established business expand; a **buyout** buys control of a business, often paid for partly with borrowed money. A **minority investor**, one that owns less than half the company, may still have negotiated specific rights, such as a veto over new borrowing. It may offer an adviser or a specialist without having any control over the company’s operating decisions, so the company decides whether to use them. And when several investors hold shares, each may have different rights and expectations: one may be able to block a sale while another can appoint a board member. So **do not assume authority from the label**. Find out, from the signed agreements, whose approval each kind of decision actually needs, and what each investor has committed to provide. [Clarify Authority: Decide Who Decides Before You Disagree](#clarify-authority) explains how.

What the blueprint should watch most closely also differs by situation. A **venture-funded team** still testing whether anyone wants its product should base each new commitment, such as a hire or a second pilot, on two things: what it has learned from customers so far, and its **runway**, how many months its cash will last given the money it expects to receive and pay out. Calculate the runway from the net shortfall, not from total spending. A business holding €120,000 that pays out €30,000 a month while receiving €20,000 is losing €10,000 a month, so it has about twelve months, not four. A **growth plan** that depends on selling and delivering the same product again and again should watch whether new customers actually start using the product and whether the team can deliver to them on time. A **buyout plan** paid for partly with borrowed money must also fit when cash is actually available and the conditions the lender set, such as a minimum level of earnings. These are examples of conditions to check, not fixed rules for every type of investor.

The arrangement is not set once and left alone. Revisit it whenever the situation changes: the company buys another business; a **new executive** arrives; the financing changes, through **new investment**, **new borrowing** or a **refinancing** (replacing an existing loan with a new one); delivery runs into a **serious constraint**, such as losing key engineers; or the **company is sold** to a new owner. When you revisit it, carry over what still applies: commitments still owed to customers or lenders, and the skills the company has built, such as a team that now runs the identity service. You know the arrangement is working when people can make the decisions they need to, the funded work is getting done, and there is enough evidence to decide the next step.

When the need for specific support is clear, [Choose the Right Help: Compare Investor Support With Other Options](#choose-right-help) compares sources for that capability gap.

## Questions to Consider

1. *Which business priority are you pursuing, and which missing decision, skill or capacity would this investor involvement supply?*
2. *Which company activities benefit from common standards or shared delivery, and why?*
3. *What have the investor and the company each committed to provide, by when and at whose cost?*
4. *Who can change priorities when the proposed work conflicts with an existing commitment?*
5. *What evidence would make you continue, revise or end this arrangement?*
