# 26. Manage Technical Debt: Fund the Fix by the Cost, the Risk and the Speed It Buys

![Manage Technical Debt: Fund the Fix by the Cost, the Risk and the Speed It Buys — logo](private-techuity/posts/25-manage-technical-debt/assets/images/25-manage-technical-debt/logo.jpeg)

> **IN THIS SECTION, YOU WILL:** Learn to put technical debt on a carrying cost with three columns, cost, risk and speed, using figures the company already has; to sort the debt by where it came from so each part has an owner and a remedy; to present a reduction project to the board by what it protects, removes and keeps open; and to fund a large refactor or migration in tranches, each released by a measured gate.

> **WHY INVESTORS CARE:** An investor hears “technical debt” as a request for money that changes nothing a customer can see, and hears “rewrite” as a year of engineering with the benefit at the end. It will fund either when it can see, per month, what the debt costs, what it risks and what it slows, and what each tranche of spending removes.

> **WHY YOU SHOULD CARE:** A percentage, an issue count or a rewrite proposal gives the board nothing to decide with. A register with three columns per item lets the same board that questions the cloud bill fund the fix for the same reasons, and lets you say which debt you are choosing to carry, until when, and why.

> **KEY POINTS:**
>
> * **Debt is a carrying cost per month with three columns.** What it costs to run (workaround hours, old licences and hosting), what risk it carries (incidents, unsupported or insecure components, a restore that fails, a vendor’s dated retirement) and what it slows (lead time on the changes customers pay for, deals the product cannot take). Never a count, a percentage or a ratio.
> * **Present the fix by what it buys.** The revenue it protects or enables, the cost or risk it removes, and, only where a real dated decision is being kept open, the decision it preserves. The board will not see the failures the fix prevented, so show the register before and after.
> * **Large projects are transitions, funded in tranches.** Each tranche is released against a measured reduction in the three columns; the rewrite whose benefit arrives at the end is right only when a hard date forces it, and living with an item is a decision with a stated cost and a review date, not an absence of one.

**Technical debt** is the cost a company carries because its software was built, or has aged, in ways that make it slower, riskier or more expensive to change than it needs to be. Some of it was a deliberate shortcut taken with a date attached. Some of it is growth that outran a design that was right at the time. Some of it arrived with a company that was bought, and some of it is a platform or a vendor that is going away. The word suggests a single number that could be paid off; what the company actually carries is three costs every month, and a **refactor** (reworking software without changing what it does for customers), a **migration** (moving software or data from one platform, system or vendor to another) or a **rewrite** (replacing a system with a new one) is a project that buys those costs down.

The chapters that follow in this part put a metered bill, the cloud bill in [Critically Evaluate Cloud Costs: A Lower Bill Is Not Always Better](#cheaper-cloud-bill) and the AI bill in [Critically Evaluate AI Costs: Measure the Return per Task and per Period](#ai-worth-its-cost), on a unit of useful work, one comparable basis and the reasons it changed before deciding what to commit, and [Build And Test Resilience: Backups Are Not Enough](#prove-you-can-restore) tests the risk behind a saving before the saving is counted. Technical debt is the bill without an invoice. This chapter gives it the same treatment first, because its register is where the three columns the later chapters deepen come from. Larkspur is the fictional scheduling-software company this book follows. Alex leads technology as chief technology officer (CTO), Priya leads product, Ines is the chief executive who leads the company, Sam leads finance, and Morgan is the technology adviser to Larkspur’s investor. Every figure is fictional, and the shared Larkspur ledger that runs through the chapters [Plan the First Hundred Days: Turn Expectations Into Funded Work](#first-hundred-days) and [Manage the Handover: Carry Forward the Evidence and Obligations](#handover-of-obligations) is not touched.

Larkspur has already paid one such debt down. The chapter [Plan for Growth: Decide What (Not) to Change in Your Systems](#growth-into-design) chose to separate the country-specific billing rules out of the **invoicing module**, the part of the software that prepares customer invoices, into a component Larkspur owns. The month-four replay of last year’s invoices passed, the second country went live in month eight, and the billing engineer now maintains the component; a new country is configuration rather than a change to fragile code. That project is the model for what follows: a stated requirement, a gate, a fallback and a benefit that arrived when the tranche did, not at the end of a rewrite. It is October 2027. Morgan, preparing the board’s annual technology review, has asked Alex two questions in one sentence: how much technical debt does Larkspur have, and would it not be simpler to rewrite the old parts?

## A Carrying Cost With Three Columns

The first question has a wrong answer that sounds precise. A static-analysis tool will produce an issue count; a consultant will produce a percentage of the codebase; both put a number on something the board cannot act on. The useful answer is a **carrying cost**: what the debt costs the company each month while it remains, in three columns that the board already knows how to read.

| Column | What it counts | Where the figure comes from |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Cost carried** | Engineering hours spent on workarounds, manual steps and repeated fixes; licences and hosting for the old component that a current design would not need | The trace from [Scale the Team Up: Headcount Is Not Capacity](#fix-decisions-before-hiring); the cloud bill’s unit figures from [Critically Evaluate Cloud Costs: A Lower Bill Is Not Always Better](#cheaper-cloud-bill); labelled cash or cost-based estimate |
| **Risk carried** | Incidents the component causes; components no longer supported or patched; a restore that fails or takes too long; a vendor’s retirement with a date | The incident log; the vendor’s support dates; the restore evidence from [Build And Test Resilience: Backups Are Not Enough](#prove-you-can-restore); the retirement notice from [Critically Evaluate AI Costs: Measure the Return per Task and per Period](#ai-worth-its-cost) |
| **Speed lost** | Lead time on the change types customers pay for; releases that cannot ship; deals the product cannot take because of a limit the design imposes | The same trace, per change type; the sales log of declined or delayed deals, stated as revenue not won, never as a loss |

The third column is the one a cost discussion leaves out, and it is often the largest. A design that caps the size of customer the product can serve does not appear on any bill. It appears as prospects the sales team stopped calling.

![An open ledger page ruled into three columns headed Cost carried, Risk carried and Speed lost, each with simple objects: coins and a clock, a cracked shield and a torn calendar, and an arrow slowed by a wall.](private-techuity/posts/25-manage-technical-debt/assets/images/25-manage-technical-debt/carrying-cost-three-columns.jpeg)

**Figure 1:** *Technical debt is a carrying cost read every month in three columns: what it costs to keep, what risk it carries and what it slows. A count of issues or a percentage of the codebase belongs in none of them.*

Alex builds the register with Priya and Sam over two weeks. Five items are enough to show the shape; a real register is rarely longer than ten, because an item that cannot be put on the three columns is not yet understood well enough to fund.

| Item | Origin | Cost carried, per month | Risk carried | Speed lost |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Invoicing module, remaining tangles.** The country rules are out; pricing and discount logic is still coupled into the module and still passes through the two specialists | Growth outran the design | About 6 engineer-days of workarounds and specialist review, a cost-based estimate of about €3,000 | Two invoice incidents a quarter, each customer-visible | Pricing changes still take about 8 working days; the catalogue of pre-approved rule types from the hiring chapter cannot grow without a code change |
| **2. Reporting stack on an unsupported framework version.** The vendor’s security patches ended on 31 March 2027 | Ageing platform | €1,500 of hosting on servers kept only for it, cash; about 2 engineer-days of patch workarounds, cost-based | Unsupported for six months; one known class of vulnerability unpatched; outside the scope of the June restore test, and a rehearsal took two days to bring back | Blocks the security questionnaire of Larkspur’s largest prospect, whose review requires supported components by the first quarter of 2028 |
| **3. Scheduling engine, single-node design.** Each region’s schedule computation runs on one large server | Growth outran the design | About €4,000 of hosting above what a design that spreads the work would need, cash; rightsizing in the cloud chapter’s sense is blocked by the design | One server per region; a failover rehearsal took 45 minutes against the two-second, 99.9% promise; the last two incidents of the year came from it | Customers above about 400 technicians cannot be served; two such prospects declined this year, about €6,000 a month each in revenue not won; every performance fix takes weeks |
| **4. AI sorting feature, prompt logic in code.** The trial’s shortcut, recorded on 11 December 2026 with Priya as owner and a revisit at 30 customers | Deliberate, dated shortcut | Every prompt change is a release: about 2 engineer-days a month, cost-based | Low | The construction work in [Critically Evaluate AI Costs: Measure the Return per Task and per Period](#ai-worth-its-cost) waits on the release cycle instead of a settings change |
| **5. Retiring vendor model.** Notice received 30 September 2027: the model the sorting feature runs on retires on 31 March 2028 | Retiring vendor | None yet | The feature stops on the date unless the successor passes the release-gate samples | None |

Two lessons are already in the table. **Origin decides the owner and the usual remedy.** Growth that outran a design is the product and engineering leaders’ to fix, in tranches, as the invoicing extraction was. An ageing platform or a retiring vendor has a date that nobody inside the company chose, and its remedy is sequenced by that date. An acquired system that never learned to work with the rest, which Larkspur does not have and the chapter [TeamSystem: Each New Owner Inherits Progress and Unfinished Work](#teamsystem) shows accumulating over years, belongs to whoever owns the integration decision in [Plan Acquisitions and Separations: Account for Extra Work, Not Just Expected Value](#acquisition-adds-work-first). And **a deliberate shortcut with a date and an owner is not a failure**; item 4 was the right call in December and is being revisited on its own terms. An undated one is a failure of record, whatever the code looks like.

## Where the Debt Came From

The origins deserve a moment each, because the board will ask, reasonably, how the company got here, and the answer is different for each item.

**A deliberate, dated shortcut** is a decision the company made with its eyes open: ship the trial with the prompt in code, revisit when the feature has thirty customers. The record is what makes it debt rather than neglect. The only failure available is forgetting the date, which is why the register carries the revisit condition next to the item.

**Growth that outran a design** is the ordinary condition of a company that is doing well. The single-node scheduling engine was the right design for customers with forty technicians; it was not wrong when it was built, and it is wrong now. The invoicing module coupled its country rules because there was one country. This is the debt the chapter [Plan for Growth: Decide What (Not) to Change in Your Systems](#growth-into-design) taught the company to treat as a design choice among options, and it is the debt this chapter funds in tranches, because it has no external date and its benefit can be taken in stages.

**An acquired system not yet integrated** carries the previous owner’s shortcuts and its own; the chapter [Plan Acquisitions and Separations: Account for Extra Work, Not Just Expected Value](#acquisition-adds-work-first) treats the integration as a boundary transition, and the chapter [TeamSystem: Each New Owner Inherits Progress and Unfinished Work](#teamsystem) shows what happens when the transitions are deferred deal after deal. Larkspur has none today. The register has a row for it because the investor’s plan has a third country and, possibly, a purchase.

**An ageing platform or a retiring vendor** is the one origin with a date the company does not control. Its remedy is not a design question but a scheduling one, and it goes first when the date is closer than the size of its carrying cost would suggest.

## Present the Fix by What It Buys

Morgan’s second question, about rewriting, is answered by first answering a question Morgan did not ask: what would a fix buy? The board reads a technology investment the way it reads any other, and the author’s [Grounded Architecture](https://grounded-architecture.io/economics) framework, applied to a portfolio in the appendix chapter [Appendix: Grounded Architecture Across an Investment Portfolio](#grounded-architecture-portfolio), sorts the benefits of architecture work into what raises or protects revenue and what reduces cost or risk, with a third kind of value that is easy to overstate: the decision a design keeps open. Larkspur uses those three as ordinary words.

- **Revenue protected or enabled.** Two prospects above 400 technicians, about €12,000 a month between them, that the scheduling engine cannot serve; the largest prospect’s security review, which the reporting stack fails. Revenue not won is stated as such: it is a reason to fund the work, not a loss the company has booked.
- **Cost or risk removed.** €1,500 a month of hosting kept for the old reporting stack; about €4,000 a month of oversized servers; a 45-minute failover against a two-second promise; an unsupported component with a known vulnerability class.
- **A decision kept open.** Splitting the schedule computation from the rest of the engine keeps open, at a known price, the later decision to replace the engine’s core, which Larkspur would take only if the evidence after the split says the ceiling remains. Gregor Hohpe’s description of architecture as selling options, the right but not the obligation to make a later decision at a price fixed now, is the idea; its value rises with uncertainty, and Larkspur has plenty. [Architecture: Selling Options](https://architectelevator.com/architecture/architecture-options/)

The third kind is used with a named decision and a date, never as a general virtue, because it cannot be priced. Neither can the first two with precision. Martin Fowler’s argument that high internal quality makes later change cheaper is the reason the invoicing extraction paid for itself within two years, and his own caveat is that nobody can measure the functionality a team delivers well enough to draw the crossover exactly. [Is High Quality Software Worth the Cost?](https://martinfowler.com/articles/is-quality-worth-cost.html) So the fix is judged on the columns it moves in the register, before and after, with the dates the moves are measured. That is a claim the board can check.

There is a reason boards under-fund this work even when the register is clear, and it should be named rather than resented. Nelson Repenning and John Sterman showed why improvement programmes are starved: the benefit of a problem that never happened is invisible, while the effort to prevent it is on this quarter’s budget, so organisations keep working harder on the symptoms instead of working better on the cause. [Nobody Ever Gets Credit for Fixing Problems that Never Happened](https://web.mit.edu/nelsonr/www/Repenning=Sterman_CMR_su01_.pdf) The register is the answer. It turns “nothing happened” into a measured difference: incidents per quarter before and after, failover time before and after, lead time before and after. A board that is shown the after column is not being asked to take the fix on faith.

## Large Projects Are Transitions

Now the rewrite question, on the item where it is most tempting: the scheduling engine. Alex compares three ways to reduce item 3, on one basis. Effort is counted in **engineer-weeks**, one person working one week, and labelled as cash or as a cost-based estimate of existing time, as the neighbouring chapters do.

| | A. Rewrite the engine | B. Tranches with gates | C. Live with it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| What it is | A new engine that spreads the work across servers, built alongside the old one, switched over when complete | Tranche 1: split the schedule computation from the web layer so it can run on a second server and fail over. Tranche 2: partition computations by customer so the largest run on their own workers. Tranche 3, only if the evidence after tranche 2 says the ceiling remains: replace the computation core | Keep the design; state its cost and review it |
| Effort and cash | Four engineers for nine months, about 150 engineer-weeks, a cost-based estimate of about €300,000; €40,000 of cash for tooling and three months of **dual running**, operating the old and new engines side by side | Tranche 1: two engineers for three months, about 26 engineer-weeks, about €50,000 cost-based, €8,000 cash. Tranche 2: two engineers for four months, about €65,000 cost-based, €10,000 cash. Tranche 3: priced only if reached | Nothing new; the carrying cost continues |
| When the benefit arrives | Month nine at the earliest, all three columns at once, and nothing customer-visible before then | Tranche 1 at month three: failover and rightsizing. Tranche 2 at month seven: large customers. Each measured before the next is funded | Never |
| What waits | The customer portal redesign for two quarters; every performance fix during the build, because the old engine is frozen | The customer portal redesign for one quarter | Nothing; the declined prospects keep declining |
| Risk | The switch-over is the moment every risk arrives together; the old engine is frozen for nine months; if the new one is late, both are paid for | Each tranche touches the live engine, so each carries **regression risk**, the danger of breaking what already worked, and each is tested against last year’s schedules in replay before it goes live, as the invoicing extraction was | The next incident, and the next declined prospect |
| Money that no longer counts | After month five, about €170,000 of effort is spent whether the rewrite finishes or stops; the decision to continue is about the remaining €130,000 and the old engine’s frozen months, not about what is gone | After tranche 1, about €58,000 is spent and the failover benefit is already in hand; the decision to fund tranche 2 is about tranche 2 | Nothing is spent |

The rewrite is the option with the benefit at the end, and the table shows what that costs even when it succeeds: nine months in which the old engine is frozen, a switch-over in which every risk arrives at once, and a sum that stops counting only when the whole of it has been spent. The chapter [Plan for Growth: Decide What (Not) to Change in Your Systems](#growth-into-design) made the same point about money already gone: what has been spent should not tip the decision, and the tranche plan is the shape that keeps the remaining decision small.

![Two timelines: a rewrite drawn as one long bar with its only benefit at month nine and a price tag of 340,000 euro, against a tranche plan drawn as two shorter bars with gates and benefits at month three and month seven and a first-tranche price tag of 58,000 euro.](private-techuity/posts/25-manage-technical-debt/assets/images/25-manage-technical-debt/rewrite-versus-tranches.jpeg)

**Figure 2:** *The rewrite carries its whole benefit to month nine and its whole price with it. The tranche plan puts a measured benefit in hand at month three and again at month seven, and each gate decides whether the next tranche is funded.*

**When the rewrite is right anyway.** Item 2 is the case. The reporting stack’s framework is unsupported since March, a prospect’s security review requires supported components by the first quarter of 2028, and there is no half-way state that satisfies either: the stack is on the old version or the new one. Its migration is a small rewrite with the benefit at the end, six engineer-weeks and about €5,000 of cash, and it is done first, in November and December 2027, ahead of item 3 whose carrying cost is larger, because a hard date outranks a large number. The **sequencing rule** is written into the register: items with an external date are ordered by the date; the rest are ordered by carrying cost per tranche of effort.

![Five record cards in a row: the first two carry calendar icons under a bracket labeled Dated items first, the next three carry coin stacks of decreasing height under a bracket labeled Then by carrying cost, above an arrow labeled Order of work.](private-techuity/posts/25-manage-technical-debt/assets/images/25-manage-technical-debt/sequencing-rule.jpeg)

**Figure 3:** *The sequencing rule written into the register: items with an external date go first, in date order; the rest follow in order of carrying cost per tranche of effort. A hard date outranks a large number.*

Item 5 is the same rule with a longer fuse. The successor model is tested against the release-gate samples in January 2028, three months before the retirement date, so that the sixty-day decision window negotiated in [Critically Evaluate AI Costs: Measure the Return per Task and per Period](#ai-worth-its-cost) is not spent discovering the problem.

## The Decision, Recorded

Alex and Priya propose, Sam tests the cash, and Ines decides within the authority she has. The board is not asked for money now; it is told what the register says and what it will be asked for in the 2028 plan.

| Element | What was decided |
| --- | --- |
| Chosen | The reporting-stack migration in November and December 2027 (item 2), then tranche 1 of the scheduling engine from January to March 2028 (item 3), then the successor-model test in January (item 5). Item 4 is folded into the AI construction work already funded. Item 1 is carried, with its cost stated, until the pricing catalogue’s next extension needs it |
| Rejected | The engine rewrite, for the frozen months, the single switch-over and the sum that stops counting only at the end; living with item 3, because the two declined prospects and the failover time are now on the register with figures; deferring item 2 until item 3 is done, because item 2 has a date and item 3 does not |
| Funding | Item 2: €5,000 of cash from the 2027 operating budget, within Ines’s delegation; the six engineer-weeks come from the customer portal redesign, which waits one quarter and is told so. Tranche 1: €8,000 of cash and two engineers for a quarter, requested in the 2028 operating plan the board approves in January; tranche 2 is not requested until tranche 1’s gate is passed |
| Scarce capacity | The two invoicing specialists are not in any of this work; item 1 stays where it is partly for that reason. The engineers on tranche 1 are named, and the portal redesign is the work that moves |
| Authority | Ines authorizes item 2 and the January test under her delegation. Tranche 1 is inside the 2028 plan and is the board’s to approve with the plan; tranche 2 is a separate line the board sees only with tranche 1’s measured result. A rewrite would have been a board decision on its own, at €340,000 |
| Gate for tranche 2 | By 31 March 2028: failover of the scheduling service rehearsed in under five minutes; the region’s server rightsized with the hosting line down by at least €1,500 a month; no new incident attributable to the split in the quarter; lead time on performance fixes unchanged or better. All four met: tranche 2 is funded from April. Failover and hosting met but an incident attributable to the split: tranche 2 waits one further quarter of incident-free operation, then proceeds. Failover not met: the split is reviewed for design, not rushed, and tranche 2 is not funded until it is |
| Register cadence | Reviewed every quarter with the cloud bill; an item’s three columns are re-measured, an item with a new external date moves to the front, and any item carried is carried with a written reason and a review date |
| Evidence that would reopen the decision | A third incident from the engine before tranche 1 starts brings tranche 1 forward and delays the portal redesign a second quarter; the largest prospect withdrawing removes item 2’s date but not its unsupported status, so item 2 proceeds on risk alone; a purchase of another company adds an integration row whose date is the deal’s |

The counterargument is in the table, and it deserves to be said plainly: **some debt is worth carrying**, and saying so is honest. Item 1 costs about €3,000 a month of cost-based time and two incidents a quarter, and the work to remove it would take the two specialists the company can least spare. Larkspur’s investor expects to hold its share for about four more years, and item 1 may well be carried to that exit. What makes that a decision rather than neglect is that the cost is stated, the reason is written and a review date is set. The evidence that would change it is the same evidence that put it on the register: a rise in the workaround hours, a third incident in a quarter, or a pricing change the catalogue cannot absorb. A leader who says “we are choosing to carry this, at this cost, until this date” has answered the investor’s question; a leader who says “it is fine” has not.

## What to Say When the Investor Asks

**“How much technical debt do we have?”** Five items on a register with three columns each. Carried, they cost about €8,500 a month of cash and cost-based time, two incidents a quarter and a 45-minute failover, and they stop us serving customers above 400 technicians, about €12,000 a month of revenue we are not winning. Two of the five have external dates and go first. One we are choosing to carry, at a stated cost, to a stated date.

**“Why not just rewrite it?”** A rewrite of the engine is nine frozen months, one switch-over where every risk arrives together, and about €340,000 that stops counting only when all of it is spent. The tranche plan puts failover and rightsizing in hand by March for about €58,000, funds the large-customer work only against that result, and keeps the core replacement as a decision we can still take at a known price. The one rewrite we are doing is the reporting stack, because it has a date and no half-way state.

**“Why fund work customers will not see?”** Because the register shows what they do see: two incidents a quarter, a prospect we cannot pass a security review for, two prospects we cannot serve. The after column is the evidence: incidents, failover time, lead time and the hosting line, measured on the same basis before and after each tranche.

With the register kept and reviewed with the bill, the technology the company runs has a stated cost, a stated risk and a stated speed, and a plan for each. The next three chapters take one column each: [Build And Test Resilience: Backups Are Not Enough](#prove-you-can-restore) the risk a saving can hide, [Critically Evaluate Cloud Costs: A Lower Bill Is Not Always Better](#cheaper-cloud-bill) the cost of a metered service per useful unit, and [Critically Evaluate AI Costs: Measure the Return per Task and per Period](#ai-worth-its-cost) the return on the newest such bill. A register like this is also what a buyer’s investigation looks for in the chapter [Use Diligence: Correct the Plan Before It Is Signed](#diligence-corrects-the-plan), and the debt the company chose to carry is among the obligations the chapter [Manage the Handover: Carry Forward the Evidence and Obligations](#handover-of-obligations) passes to the next decision-maker.

{id: manage-technical-debt--questions-to-consider}
## Questions to Consider

1. *List the five largest items of technical debt you carry. For each, can you state its cost per month, the risk it carries and what it slows, from figures you already have?*
2. *Which of your items has an external date, a support end, a retirement, a customer’s review, and is it sequenced by that date or by its size?*
3. *For the project most often proposed as a rewrite, what would the first tranche be, what would its gate measure, and what would still be a decision after it?*
4. *Which debt are you choosing to carry, at what stated cost, until what date, and who has been told?*
