# 16. Clarify the Adviser’s Role: Are They Helping, Assessing or Deciding?

![Clarify the Adviser’s Role: Are They Helping, Assessing or Deciding? — logo](private-techuity/posts/13-clarify-adviser-role/assets/images/18-clarify-adviser-role/logo.jpeg)

> **IN THIS SECTION, YOU WILL:** Learn to define the investor adviser's assignment (what they are asked to do) and reporting line (who they report to), separate influence (the adviser's ability to persuade) from authority (the power to direct the company), and confirm any change of role before sharing sensitive information.

> **WHY INVESTORS CARE:** An investor has put money into the company expecting a financial return. Its **information rights** are whatever the company has agreed it may see and ask about. The adviser is one of the investor's main ways to exercise those rights and to shape the rationale behind its decisions. If the role is unclear, the investor can't tell whether the adviser's findings are independent.

> **WHY YOU SHOULD CARE:** An adviser who reports to the investment firm can play three roles. They can help you, assess you or, unless the assignment says otherwise, appear to decide for you. Before you share anything, find out which role the adviser holds. That determines what you should share and how the investor will use it.

> **KEY POINTS:**
>
> * Establish the **assignment and the reporting relationship**. The adviser reports to the investment firm, not the company. The company needs to know what work the adviser was asked to do and how their findings may feed the investor's decisions.
> * Separate **influence from authority**. Being close to the investor gives an adviser's suggestions weight, but not the right to decide. That right comes only from an executive role, a board of directors (the body that oversees company leadership), rights agreed with the shareholders who own the company, or an explicit assignment. An adviser has no authority beyond what those sources grant.
> * Treat a **change of role as a new agreement**. When coaching becomes assessment, or advice becomes delivery, settle four things first: what the information is for, what the investor's existing rights already cover, who authorized the new role and what employees will be told.

> **[DYSFUNCTIONS THIS SECTION ADDRESSES](#where-investment-goes-wrong):**
>
> * **The Accidental Gatekeeper** — An adviser's role quietly shifts from advising to assessing or delivering, and the company never agrees to it. The fix is to treat any such shift as a new agreement, made explicitly.
> * **The Ghost Veto** — Staff treat an adviser's closeness to the investor as the power to decide, so suggestions get followed like orders. The fix is to check what authority the adviser actually holds.

A technology specialist employed by the investor joins your planning meeting. They know the product well and **offer ideas**. Your engineers can't tell whether those ideas are suggestions to weigh, a new assessment of your work that will be reported to the investor, or instructions they must follow. Without a clear answer, engineers may either ignore useful advice or reprioritize work based on an offhand comment.

An **investor's technology adviser** is a person the investment firm employs or engages to form its own view of a company's technology and, often, to help improve it. This book calls that role the **Technology Principal**. Morgan holds it in the fictional Rotaline scenarios. A **chief technology officer (CTO)** leads technology inside the company. At Rotaline, that is Alex. The adviser can test assumptions and help improve the company's work, but the CTO stays responsible for running technology day to day. So the adviser's ideas are input for the CTO to weigh, not instructions engineers must follow.

What sets this adviser apart from other outside experts is not privileged access or a mix of responsibilities, because a consultant appointed by the board can have both. It is the **reporting line**. This adviser reports to the investment firm, so what the adviser sees in the company can shape the investor's decisions, such as whether to fund, hold or replace leadership. The company's technology leader must therefore agree, before any sensitive information is shared, what the adviser is there to do and what will be passed on to the investor.

Earlier chapters in Part III covered how to arrange the work, choose help and put an advisory engagement in writing. [Choose the Right Help: Compare Investor Support With Other Options](#choose-right-help) covers how to find and select that help, and [Set the Terms of Help: Agree the Work, Authority and Handover](#set-terms-of-help) covers the terms of an engagement. This chapter turns to the investor's technology adviser: who that person works for, what they are asked to do, and how the company's technology leader should handle that relationship before sharing anything sensitive.

## Establish the Assignment and the Reporting Relationship

The supplied investment-firm role brief describes in detail one adviser who works across the firm's portfolio companies. That is a single firm's arrangement, not a standard one: **many investors have no such adviser**, and the brief gives no adviser authority over a company's CTO or product leader. So don't assume the adviser can direct your technology decisions, and confirm who the adviser reports to and what they have been asked to do before you share anything sensitive. [P01: supplied role brief](#bibliography)

A **venture investor** backs young companies that could grow very large but could also fail. Your most useful contact there is often a **partner** (a senior person who decides which companies the firm invests in), a hiring specialist, or someone introduced for a single problem. A **growth investor** funds the expansion of a company that already has customers and sales revenue, and may offer advisers who have run that kind of expansion before. A **corporate owner** is another operating company that owns yours. It may supply specialists in how systems fit together across the group, or in protecting systems and data. Those specialists usually also enforce the parent's internal policy, so what you tell them may bind you to that policy. Some investors provide no operating support, so don't count on help from any of these.

Four questions settle most of the ambiguity: **Who employs** this person? What has the investor or owner **asked them to do** for this company? **How many hours** can they give? Which **decisions**, if any, may they make for you? Making decisions is not another form of help. It is authority, and it comes with an assignment only if someone entitled to grant it has said so. Unless that is confirmed, treat the person as an adviser whose suggestions you are free to decline.

Behind the four questions sits what the company has already promised its owners in writing. A **shareholder** is a person or firm that owns part of the company. The **shareholder agreement**, or **investors' rights agreement**, is the contract that spells out what those owners may do, such as what information they can see and which decisions they can take part in. If the **contract doesn't grant a right**, the person asking for it usually can't claim it, however senior or helpful they are.

**Qualifying investors**, meaning those who own at least the share of the company that the contract names, usually get two rights: to **inspect** the company's records and to **discuss its affairs** with senior executives. The same rights extend to anyone the investor authorizes to act for them, such as an adviser like Morgan. These rights let the investor monitor the investment and decide what to do with it. An investor or adviser below the threshold, or without written authorization, has no such right to see records or question executives.

Those rights have limits. A **trade secret** is valuable information the company keeps confidential, such as a method its competitors lack. The company can withhold it from an inspecting investor or adviser. **Legal privilege** shields certain lawyer–client communications from compelled disclosure, so the company can usually refuse to show or discuss its lawyers' advice, subject to the rules that apply.

One filed example is [S76: section 3.2 (Inspection Rights) of Avalyn Pharma’s amended and restated investors’ rights agreement of April 2025](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1540171/000119312526147573/ck0001540171-ex4_2.htm). It grants inspection and discussion rights to each major investor and its authorized representatives. It says nothing about what Rotaline’s investor may do. It shows that Rotaline’s **own agreement sets the adviser’s access**, so the company does not decide on its own what the adviser may see or ask. Read that agreement first.

## Understand Which Job the Adviser Is Doing

**Due diligence** is the investigation that informs an investment decision: the adviser checks the company's claims and reports what they found. **Coaching** helps a person develop their own judgment and practice, so the adviser asks questions and gives feedback but does not direct the work. **Operating support** helps the company improve how work gets done, so the adviser works on processes and recommends changes. A specific assignment may also give the adviser formal oversight or temporary delivery responsibilities, which means the adviser can then make decisions or own results.

These purposes can **overlap**, but everyone involved should know which ones apply. The last column states how employees should understand the adviser's words: as an assessment finding, a recommendation, or an authorized instruction. An assessment finding is a report to whoever commissioned the review. A recommendation is advice that the accountable leader may accept or reject. An authorized instruction is a directive the company has formally delegated. If employees cannot tell which of the three they are hearing, they may not know whether to act, wait for a decision, or ignore it, so the assignment has not been explained.

| Assignment | What company leaders may need | Boundary to establish | What employees should hear |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Investigation before an investment | A fair account of strengths, constraints and required funding | Evidence scope, access and how findings will inform the plan | An **assessment finding**, reported to the investor |
| Advice on an operating decision | Relevant options, experience and specialist judgment | Who is authorized to decide, which assumptions matter and what remains uncertain | A **recommendation** the accountable leader may accept or reject |
| Help delivering a change | People and practical work that resolve a constraint | Resources, company accountability and what happens after the assignment | A recommendation, unless the written assignment gives the adviser day-to-day direction of the work |
| Coaching or leadership development | Help learning and improving judgment | What may be shared, with whom and any connection to formal assessment | Nothing; a coaching conversation is not addressed to the team |
| Formal oversight | Review of results and risks on behalf of the board or the investor | The scope of the review, whether it rests on existing information rights or a new assignment, and where findings go | An **assessment finding**, reported to whoever commissioned the review; an instruction only where a decision has been explicitly delegated |
| Interim leadership | Temporary direction of a team or a delivery | The explicit assignment, the decisions it delegates, its duration, resources and who an unresolved question goes to next | An **authorized instruction**, announced by the company, for the delegated decisions; everything else still comes from the accountable leader |

Ask the adviser to state in writing what they owe the company and what they owe the investment firm, and **where those duties could conflict**. The adviser may also work on prospective investments and other companies, which limits how much time they can give you. Treat that as a planning input for the chapter [Set the Terms of Help: Agree the Work, Authority and Handover](#set-terms-of-help), not as a verdict on the relationship. Use it to decide how often you can expect them and how to cover the gaps.

## Separate Influence From Authority

**Authority** is the right to make a particular decision, such as setting engineering priorities or approving a hire. It comes from an executive role, board responsibilities, rights the shareholders have agreed to, or an **explicit assignment**; the sources and the map that records them are the subject of the chapter [Clarify Authority: Decide Who Decides Before You Disagree](#clarify-authority). A job title, closeness to the owner or an advisory assignment doesn't create that right. The adviser holds only the authority those sources grant, which is usually none, so the adviser's instructions bind no one unless a source gives them that force.

The investor's adviser can carry substantial influence even when the service is described as optional. Influence is not authority, but engineers may treat the adviser's requests as instructions. Direct access to engineers is useful during diligence or a scoped support assignment. It becomes destructive when the adviser, the CTO and the investment team each give engineers different priorities: a **second chain of command** then exists, one nobody authorized and nobody answers for. Engineers cannot satisfy all three, so they must guess which instruction to follow, and work slows or goes in the wrong direction. The team stops asking "what should we build?" and starts asking "whose instruction wins?"

The remedy is the decision map applied to the adviser by name. Write down three lists: the questions Morgan may recommend on, the questions the accountable company leader decides, and the questions that need board approval. Then share the map with the people doing the work. An engineer who hears a suggestion from Morgan should be able to say who is authorized to make the decision, without asking a manager. If the engineer can't, the second chain of command is still in place.

Morgan might work alongside the engineers to find out why a new release broke when it went live, then hand the resulting improvement plan to Alex, who decides what to do with it. The test is whether, once Morgan leaves, the company is more capable and everyone still knows who was responsible for what.

## When a Coaching Conversation Becomes an Assessment

The hardest case is an adviser whose role changes without anyone saying so: a coach quietly becomes an assessor, and the person being coached doesn't know. The case below is fictional, but the pattern is common.

For three months, Morgan has met with Alex monthly to **help him develop as a leader**: how he manages the engineering managers, where he avoids conflict, and which decisions he delays. Alex has been candid because candor makes coaching useful. Then the investment firm's **deal team**, the people at the firm who handle this particular investment, asks Morgan for a written view on whether Rotaline's technology leadership can carry the plan. The directors will read that paper before deciding whether the company should expand into a second country. Morgan now holds private admissions from Alex that no assessment interview would draw out. If Morgan writes the view honestly, those admissions become evidence against Alex in a decision about his company, and the coaching becomes an assessment he never agreed to.

Nothing improper has happened yet. Morgan reports to the firm, which may ask its adviser for a view. But if Morgan writes about Alex using what he shared in confidence as a coachee, he will have **turned coaching into an assessment** without telling Alex, and Alex's candor will have worked against him.

**What Alex can ask before sharing anything sensitive.** Alex should ask these questions at the start of any coaching arrangement, and again whenever Morgan's role changes, as it has now that the deal team wants a written view. Is this conversation purely for my development? Who will receive what I say, and in what form? Could it feed a formal assessment of me, now or later? What can the firm already see or request under the investment agreements, and what must you report, whatever we agree on? Will you tell me if the purpose changes? Without clear answers, Alex cannot know whether his candor stays in the coaching room or reaches the directors deciding on expansion.

**What Morgan must explain.** Morgan must tell Alex that the firm has asked for a view of Alex's leadership, and name who asked. Morgan must also say exactly what the firm will receive: an assessment of whether Alex's leadership capability fits the company's plan, not a transcript or summary of the coaching sessions. With that explained, Alex can decide what to say in coaching, knowing that some of it may shape the firm's view.

**What the coaching agreement already says.** Confidentiality here is not a general principle. It is whatever Alex and Morgan wrote down at the first session, and it should have been written down. In this fictional arrangement, the rule is one sentence: *what Alex says in a coaching session is not reported to the investment firm, and Morgan will raise any concern that affects the company with Alex first and then, if it is unresolved, with Ines, Rotaline's chief executive officer, the executive who leads the whole company.* So even when Morgan judges a concern serious enough to affect the company, that does not make a coaching conversation reportable. The agreed rule decides, and Morgan cannot promise more confidentiality than the rule gives. A professional coaching code can guide how such a rule is written, but it binds only an adviser who has adopted it.

**Why that rule matters now.** The coaching sessions cannot be used as material for the assessment. Morgan may report only what Morgan can establish another way: from the diligence record, from work Morgan has seen Alex do, or from interviews the company agrees to. The three months of candid conversation are out of bounds unless Alex releases them.

Only Alex can release the coaching sessions. The rule exists to protect him, so lifting it is his decision. Ines and the board can commission an assessment, but approving the work does not lift the confidentiality attached to the coaching. One limit remains: a **private agreement cannot override a disclosure the law requires**. It settles what Morgan may volunteer, not what Morgan could be compelled to produce.

If Morgan wants to use what the coaching revealed, there are three honest routes. Morgan can ask Alex to **agree in writing**. Morgan can write the assessment only from **independently obtained evidence** and say so. Or Morgan can tell the firm that the coaching relationship makes Morgan the **wrong person to assess** Alex, and let the firm appoint someone else.

**Who can authorize the changed assignment.** The firm may ask its adviser for an opinion without anyone's further consent. The investment agreements may already entitle the firm, and those it authorizes, to company information and to discussions with senior executives so it can monitor its investment. Morgan can form a view from those sources without new permission from anyone.

What the firm cannot do on its own is turn that view into an assessment it commissions. That means gaining new access to staff and records to interview Alex's managers. Under Rotaline's agreed arrangement, both require Ines to agree to three things: the **scope**, the **evidence** Morgan may use, and the **form of the report**. If the assessment will inform a decision the directors will take, the board gives that agreement instead of Ines.

Ines or the board can authorize the *work*, meaning the assessment and any extra access it needs. Only Alex can release the *coaching material* because the agreement protects what he said in the sessions, and he is the person it protects. Without his consent, Morgan cannot use that material, whatever Ines or the board approves. Morgan should therefore check what the existing information rights already cover, then ask Ines to approve the new purpose and any additional access. Morgan should put the question of the coaching sessions to Alex separately.

Suppose the firm proposes that Morgan **take over running** onboarding, the work of getting newly signed customers set up and running on the product, for three months because Alex is stretched. Directing company work requires authority that the company has actually granted. Unless the board authorizes the temporary assignment, stating its duration, its resources, and who Morgan reports to, it does not happen. Without that authorization, Morgan would be giving instructions that nobody has empowered them to give. Neither side should learn of the change from a meeting invitation.

**How employees are informed.** Ines sends the engineering managers a written note saying what Morgan is now doing and until when. For example: “Morgan is preparing a leadership assessment for the board’s expansion decision, and conversations for that purpose will be announced as such. Day-to-day priorities still come from Alex.” If Morgan is instead leading delivery on an interim basis, the note lists which decisions Morgan may make and which still go to Alex or Ines. That way, managers know whom to ask and whose instructions to follow, and no one learns of the change from a rumor or a stray conversation.

Alex does not have to like the assessment, but he is entitled to know three things: that it is **happening**, what **information** it draws on, and who **authorized** it. If Morgan's coaching continues after the assessment, treat it as a new agreement and restate the boundary between coaching and assessment.

## Build a Shared Account of Leadership Capabilities and Past Decisions

Explain the customer **commitments** you must honor, the **constraints** you work under, and why the team made its earlier **choices**. Then ask the adviser which investment assumptions depend on those capabilities and what evidence would change their view. If they cannot name any, their assumptions are untested.

**Disagreement** improves that account when both sides state what **evidence** would overturn their position. Take the example developed in the chapter [Use Diligence: Correct the Plan Before It Is Signed](#use-diligence). Morgan sampled five recent onboarding implementations, and the average onboarding time was about 80 hours. In three of the five, the setup depended on manual work by one particular specialist. Morgan and Alex both accept these observations but disagree about what they mean: Morgan reads them as a sign that growth will be limited by that specialist's time, while Alex reads them as a sign of one-off effort that doesn't bear on how well the process scales.

Morgan's concern is a **hypothesis about capacity**, not a measured rule. Three of the five sampled onboardings needed one specialist's manual work. If that dependency is built into how the work is organized, selling twice as much would require roughly twice as many hours, and the company would not have the people to supply them. That follows only if effort per customer stays at about eighty hours, which nobody has established. Alex's counter is an estimate, not a finding: he puts roughly 40% of the hours on cleaning up messy customer data, which he argues says nothing about whether the onboarding process itself can scale. The two explanations are compatible, and nobody yet knows how much of the work each accounts for. Until someone does, the company can't say whether growth would strain its people or only expose a data problem.

Neither is asked to concede. Both explanations are **written down**, and both **agree in advance** on one measurement. For the next group of customers, record how the hours are divided among three kinds of work: fixing customer data, working around what the product cannot yet do, and all other setup work, such as customer sessions and rework, which also sits inside the eighty-hour average. The split shows how much of the load each explanation accounts for, so the next investment can follow the data instead of the argument.

Filling every hour that is not data cleaning into the "product limitation" category would invent a finding rather than measure one. The measurement does not declare Morgan or Alex right. It shows how many of the eighty hours each explanation accounts for. If most of the time goes to product workarounds, the next money should go to the product. If most goes to data cleaning, it should go to data tooling. If the remainder dominates, neither explanation has earned the investment yet.

If the measurement shows the adviser's assessment was partly wrong, the adviser should be able to **revise it**. If it shows a familiar practice, such as the company's manual data cleaning, accounts for fewer than eighty hours than assumed, the company leader should be able to reconsider it. Neither should treat the change as a personal defeat, because the measurement, not either person, decides where the next money goes.

The company shouldn’t tell investors one confident story and engineers another account of the real constraints. **Preserve the same facts** for both, and change only the level of detail to suit each audience. Otherwise, whichever version is wrong will surface later as a loss of trust.

## Back to the Planning Meeting

- **Why Morgan is here.** Morgan attends as the investment firm’s technology adviser. Anything Morgan says about the plan is a recommendation, not an instruction.
- **Who decides?** Alex decides on the technical plan, within the spending already approved. Ines and the board decide on anything above that amount.
- **What happens if that changes?** If the firm wants a formal assessment of the plan or the team, it will say so openly rather than gather one informally.
- **What needs to be agreed first?** Any access to people or company time beyond the investor’s existing information rights must be agreed with Ines or the board first, and the company will tell the team about it.

Alex tells the engineers **before the meeting**, not after, and Morgan confirms it in the room: Morgan's ideas are suggestions, not instructions. From then on, the engineers can weigh each idea on its merits, accepting it if it helps and declining it if it doesn't, without worrying that refusing the investor's adviser will count against them. That is what makes the ideas useful.

The adviser is one of several people at the investment firm whose job is to **help companies in practical ways**, not to make investment decisions. The next chapter covers how that group supports companies, how it works with product and technology leaders, and what hiring and artificial intelligence (AI) add to its work: [Work With Technology Operating Partners](#technology-operating-partners).

## Questions to Consider

1. *Who from your investor is involved in your technology decisions, what is their assignment, and which decisions, if any, are they authorized to make?*
2. *Which job is the adviser doing with you now: diligence, advice, delivery, coaching, oversight or interim leadership? Would your engineers give the same answer?*
3. *Before your last candid conversation with the adviser, did you know who would receive what you said and what they were obliged to report?*
4. *If the adviser’s role changed tomorrow, what could the investor do under its existing rights, who would have to authorize anything more, and how would the team be told?*
