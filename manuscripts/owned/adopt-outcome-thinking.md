# 16. Adopt Outcome Thinking: Balance Customer and Business KPIs

![Adopt Outcome Thinking: Balance Customer and Business KPIs — logo](private-techuity/posts/15-adopt-outcome-thinking/assets/images/15-adopt-outcome-thinking/logo.jpeg)

> **IN THIS SECTION, YOU WILL:** Learn to write an outcome record. It names one change the company wants for its customers, in the customers’ own words; one business result the leadership agrees that change should feed; the measures that connect the two; who is responsible for each measure; and the date on which each is reviewed. Then use it to answer the investor’s questions, and to say no to a request with a record rather than a fight.

> **WHY INVESTORS CARE:** An investor, here an investment fund (a pool of money from many investors) that put money into the company expecting a financial return, reads the company through a handful of numbers. It wants to know which of those numbers the product work changes, when the change will show, and why a team that delivered everything on its plan can still have changed nothing that matters.

> **WHY YOU SHOULD CARE:** Without one agreed pair of outcomes, the plan becomes a negotiation among whoever asked loudest, and the product team is graded on this quarter’s sales. The record lets you build what customers value and show, on a realistic timetable, what that does for the business.

> **KEY POINTS:**
>
> * **Judge work by outcomes, not outputs.** An output is something delivered: a feature, a release. An outcome is a measurable change in what customers or the company can do. A delivered feature is a cost until an outcome moves.
> * **Pair one customer outcome with one business outcome, and write down the chain between them.** Each outcome breaks into the factors that drive it and the measures that track them. The record says which measures the product team is accountable for, which it only influences, and which belong to sales and to customer success, the team that helps existing customers get value from the software.
> * **Connect the business outcome to the investor’s numbers on an honest timetable.** Say which investor measure it feeds, when the effect can first be observed, and refuse to grade a slow effect on a fast clock.

Larkspur is the fictional scheduling-software company this book follows. It sells software that plans maintenance work for businesses whose staff work in the field. Alex leads technology as chief technology officer (CTO). Priya leads **product**, the team that decides what the software should do and for whom. Ines is the chief executive who leads the company, and Sam leads finance. Morgan is the technology adviser to Larkspur’s investor. Every figure is fictional.

It is the week after **closing**, the day the investment was completed. Within days of closing, the **board**, the group that oversees the company, met and adopted the operating plan. That meeting is **day 0**. The chapters count project days from it, so day 20 and day 90 below are days after that board meeting, not after closing.

The plan the investor agreed to at closing expects 50% more new customers in the first year without a larger setup team, and a second country only if a pilot, a small trial of the changed setup with a few customers, produces evidence that the setup can carry it. The investor’s original ambition was larger: a second country within twelve months and a third within two years. Diligence, the investigation before the deal, cut that back, and the chapter [Use Diligence: Correct the Plan Before It Is Signed](#diligence-corrects-the-plan) records why.

Priya has three requests on her list: a customer portal, a website through which customers would manage their own accounts; simpler setup for new customers; and the recovery work Alex wants, so the scheduling service can be restored after a failure. The chapter [Set Priorities: You Cannot Fund Everything at Once](#cannot-fund-everything) will choose among them against the cash and the team time the board approves. Before it can, someone has to say what the work is *for*. Larkspur could deliver a setup feature next quarter and nothing would change for its customers if the delay they suffer comes from somewhere else. Delivering is not the same as helping.

Morgan asks the question in the form investors use: “What is your north star?” A **north star** is one guiding measure the whole company steers by. It is a fair question and a trap at once. Answered with a feature list, it tells the investor nothing; answered with a single number, it tells the team nothing. This chapter answers it with an **outcome record**: one change for customers, one business result it should feed, the measures between them, who is responsible for each, and the dates on which they are reviewed. The setup facts below are the ones the chapters [Plan the First Hundred Days: Turn Expectations Into Funded Work](#first-hundred-days) and [Test Revenue Assumptions: Do Customers Respond as Expected?](#roadmap-to-revenue) also use; this chapter adds none.

## Outcomes, Not Outputs

An **output** is what the company delivered: a feature (a capability of the software), a release (a batch of changes made available to customers), a redesigned screen. An **outcome** is a measurable change in the customer’s or the company’s reality: a customer who can do something she could not, a cost that fell, a customer who renewed. A **key performance indicator (KPI)** is a number chosen to track an outcome. A **leading indicator** moves first, while the work is still going on, and gives early warning; a **lagging indicator** confirms later that the outcome happened. The same number can lead one result and lag another. A **vanity metric** is a number that rises for reasons unrelated to whether customers succeeded: logins, clicks, pages viewed. Such numbers are not useless, but they count only when someone has shown how they connect to a customer or business result.

The test for a strong customer metric is simple to state and hard to pass: it would still matter if the product changed completely, and customers confirm they would pay, renew or buy more to get it. If a redesign would make the metric meaningless, the metric was measuring the product’s mechanics, not the customer’s success. Larkspur’s customers do not want a **setup wizard**, a guided sequence of setup screens; they want to be scheduling real work soon after they signed, without waiting on anyone. That sentence survives any redesign. “Share of customers who finish the wizard” does not.

| What outcome thinking says | What it does **not** say |
| --- | --- |
| Every piece of work names, before it starts, the outcome it should move | No work starts until the measures are perfect |
| A delivered feature is a cost until an outcome moves | Delivering does not matter; delivering is how outcomes move, it is just never the finish line |
| Outcomes are checked with customers | Customers dictate the plan item by item |
| Usage counts only when it clearly connects to value | Usage data is useless |
| Product is judged over quarters and years | Product is never accountable; the lagging indicators still arrive and are still reported |
| Work that moves no outcome is stopped | Every experiment must succeed; a test that disproves a link improved the model too |

The author’s own operating model for this, written for product leaders rather than for leaders under investors, is the record [Outcomes](https://zeljkoobrenovic.github.io/spec-driven-journals/grounded-product-management/outcomes.html) in the companion journal; this chapter is its translation into the situation the rest of the book describes.

## One Customer Outcome, One Business Outcome

**The customer outcome first.** Priya writes it in the customer’s language, after asking six of them: *a new customer is scheduling real work within a few weeks of signing, without Larkspur’s specialist setting the account up by hand.* Its metric is the **median** weeks from signed contract to the first real schedule; the median is the middle value when the customers are lined up from fastest to slowest (with an even number, the average of the two middle values), so one very slow customer does not distort it. Its main driver is the **staff hours per customer setup**: everyone’s time on that customer’s setup, not the specialist’s alone. The specialist’s manual configuration, entering the customer’s settings by hand, is only part of those hours, but it is the part that holds the queue, because only one person can do it.

Both numbers are provisional in the week after closing. Morgan’s diligence sample of five recent setups gave about 80 hours of staff time each, with three of the five needing the specialist’s manual configuration, and the customers’ own accounts put the wait at about ten weeks. The day-20 baseline, built on day 20 from time records for all twelve setups of the previous two quarters, confirmed both figures, and the record carries that baseline from then on; the chapter [Plan the First Hundred Days: Turn Expectations Into Funded Work](#first-hundred-days) shows how it was built. The estimate made then also says where the hours go: entering settings took under half of them, and cleaning the customer’s data, fixing missing or inconsistent records, was the largest remaining share.

The six interviews are evidence that the delay matters, not proof of what fixing it will earn. Three of the six said the wait had delayed their own rollout, the point at which their staff start using the software; two said they had considered a competitor for that reason. That validates the outcome. It does not validate any particular feature, and it does not yet show that a faster start makes a customer renew. That link is the chapter’s working hypothesis, and the record says when it will be tested.

**Then the business outcome, and only one.** Ines asks her executive team and the investor-appointed director, the board member the investor chose, to agree on the one business result product optimizes for. Each function arrives with its own. Sales wants **bookings**, the value of new contracts signed. Finance wants **margin**: **revenue** is the money the company earns from selling its service, before any costs are taken off, and margin is the share of that revenue left after the direct cost of serving customers. The investor’s **thesis**, its explanation of how the investment is meant to pay off, says growth in customer count without proportional staff.

They agree on **retention and expansion of paying customers**. Retention means keeping the customers Larkspur already has, and the revenue they pay. **Expansion** means existing customers paying to cover more of their business: additional technicians using the software, or additional business locations. Entering a second country is a different thing, and the chapter calls it that.

The hypothesis behind the choice: a customer who starts fast is more likely to stay and to grow, and a company that cannot start customers fast cannot take on the volume the thesis needs. The other results are not abandoned; they belong to other functions and to later chapters. What the agreement removes is the treaty negotiation in which every function carries its own definition of success and the plan is whatever survives the argument.

Why only one of each? Because a record with three customer outcomes and four business outcomes lets every request find a home and decides nothing. One pair is what a team can prioritize with, defend in front of a loud customer, and recite without the slide.

## Two Pyramids and the Chain Between Them

A key outcome on a slide changes nothing. Broken into the factors that drive it and the measures that track those factors, it tells a team what to improve next and lets the leader trace every planned item to the measure it claims to move. Larkspur draws two **pyramids**, one per outcome, each with the same three levels: the outcome at the top, the contributing factors beneath it, and each factor’s measures beneath those. A **node** is one item in that diagram. The author’s [Productscape](https://zeljkoobrenovic.github.io/productscape-examples/start-packages/overview/index.html) ready-made examples, which it calls start packages, show the same shape worked out for fifty product domains: for each customer type, a customer-outcome pyramid and a business-outcome pyramid, with every team measure linked by name to a node in one of them.

**Customer-outcome pyramid.** Top: median weeks from contract to first real schedule. The same signing and first-schedule dates also give the share of customers who reach a first real schedule within eight weeks, a percentage rather than a middle value; that share is the measure the business pyramid borrows, so it sits at the top of this pyramid beside the median.

- **Setup effort.** Staff hours per customer setup (leading; about 80). Share of setups needing the specialist’s manual configuration (leading; three of five in the diligence sample).
- **Customer data readiness.** Share of customer data arriving clean enough to load, meaning complete and consistent enough for the software to use without correction (leading). Days from contract to the customer returning its data (leading).
- **Early use.** Share of customers scheduling real work in the first week after their account is activated (lagging). Setup-related **support requests** in the first month, the recorded calls and messages asking for help (lagging).

**Business-outcome pyramid.** Top: **net revenue retention** of paying customers. **Recurring revenue** is the part of revenue that repeats because customers pay a **subscription**, an ongoing paid service, rather than buying once; Larkspur’s customers pay yearly. Net revenue retention compares what the customers the company had a year ago paid then with what those same customers pay now, so it is the investor’s measure of whether existing customers’ recurring revenue grew or shrank; the worked example is in the next section. The measures beneath it are a selection of indicators the teams can act on, not a complete breakdown of the figure.

- **Renewal.** Renewal rate at contract anniversary, the share of customers whose yearly subscription came up for renewal and who renewed (lagging). It counts customers, not money: if every customer renewed but on a smaller subscription, the renewal rate would be 100% while net revenue retention fell, so the record reads the two together. Share of customers who reached a first real schedule within eight weeks (leading; the join to the customer pyramid, the same statistic that sits at its top, and the link under test).
- **Expansion.** Technicians or business locations that existing customers add to their subscription within a year (lagging). Share of customers with every technician scheduled through the product by month three (leading; a customer that uses the product for all its staff has somewhere to grow).

Two things the same measures feed sit outside this pyramid on purpose. Staff hours per setup and month-one support requests are also **cost to serve**, the direct cost of setting up and supporting customers, so they feed **gross margin**, the share of revenue left after those direct costs; lowering them does not change net revenue retention, which counts revenue, not cost. New customers signed, including in a second country if the board approves it, feed **annual recurring revenue**, the yearly value of all the subscriptions customers currently pay for, and are sales’ work; a customer that was not there a year ago does not enter the retention figure. The record lists both so the investor can read them, but neither is the business outcome.

The pyramids are joined at one shared measure, on purpose. The share of customers reaching a first real schedule within eight weeks is read from the same dates as the customer outcome’s median, so it is a customer confirmation and a renewal input, and it sits in both pyramids as the same statistic. Staff hours per setup are not shared. They are a customer driver that also feeds the cost to serve and gross margin, outside the retention pyramid. That structure is the **causal model** the investor is really asking for when it asks for a north star: the near-term things the team can influence, linked to the long-term things that propel the company, with honesty about which links are measured and which are conviction.

The first link under test runs from the hours to the wait. At Larkspur two quantities are measured today, the staff hours and the wait. That the hours cause the wait is the working explanation, not a measured link, and the first cohort is its first test: hours that fall while the wait stays put would weaken it.

The second link runs from a fast start to renewal. It is the second hypothesis, and its test is written down: at the first anniversaries, compare the renewal rate of customers who reached a first schedule within eight weeks with the rate of those who did not. The comparison is evidence to weigh, not a verdict. The groups are small and the customers differ in size and data quality, so similar rates, different rates and too few renewals to tell are all possible readings, and the record reopens the pair if the comparison shows no difference or cannot be read.

**Attach responsibility to measures, not to levels.** The record names who answers for each measure:

- **Product:** staff hours per setup, the manual share and the first-schedule weeks, including the share reaching a first schedule within eight weeks.
- **Shared, data readiness:** product owns the tools that check and load the data; the customer owns the records; the **customer success** team, which helps existing customers get value from the software and keep using it, chases the data.
- **Shared, early use and month-one support requests:** product, customer success and the support team.
- **Renewal rate:** customer success and the people who set prices.
- **Expansion:** sales and customer success.

Product still influences renewal and expansion, through the customer’s experience, and the record says so. The wording matters in both directions. A product leader who claims the renewal rate will be blamed for it. One who disclaims any effect on it will be ignored.

The pyramid and responsibility lists above give the full content.](private-techuity/posts/15-adopt-outcome-thinking/assets/images/15-adopt-outcome-thinking/two-pyramids-joined.jpeg)

**Figure 1:** *The two pyramids and the chain between them. Every branch has the same three levels: outcome, factor, measures. One measure sits in both pyramids: the share of customers reaching a first real schedule within eight weeks, read from the same dates as the customer outcome’s median, confirms the customer outcome and, if the hypothesis holds, feeds renewal. Staff hours per setup are not shared: they are the suspected main cause of the customer’s wait, which the pilot tests by lowering them, and they also feed gross margin, outside the retention pyramid, as new customers signed feed annual recurring revenue. The tag under each pair of measures says who is accountable for them; product influences the business measures through the shared one. The business measures are a selection of indicators the teams can act on: the renewal rate counts customers, while net revenue retention counts revenue.*

**The removal test.** Take any node out. If next week’s work changes, the node is useful: it is part of the model the team works with. If only a slide changes, it is decoration. Larkspur’s “share of customers scheduling real work in week one” fails the test at first: nobody would do anything differently if it vanished. Priya keeps it only after tying it to a specific action: a customer that has not scheduled real work by the end of its first week after activation gets a check-in call. The node earns its place. In the author’s experience many nodes fail this test, and the pruning is the point. The test says whether a measure is useful to the team; whether it actually causes the outcome above it is a separate question, answered by the comparisons written into the record, such as the renewal comparison above, which can also come back inconclusive.

![The removal test: a block lifted out of a pyramid either changes next week’s work, marked useful, or changes only a slide, marked decoration.](private-techuity/posts/15-adopt-outcome-thinking/assets/images/15-adopt-outcome-thinking/the-removal-test.jpeg)

**Figure 2:** *The removal test. A node earns its place only if taking it out changes what a team does next week; a node whose absence changes only a slide is decoration. Larkspur’s week-one scheduling node fails at first and is kept only once a specific action, a check-in call for any customer that has not scheduled by the end of its first week, hangs on it. The test establishes usefulness, not cause; the causal link is tested separately.*

## The Investor Bridge, on an Honest Clock

The investor reads Larkspur through a few numbers, and the outcome record has to end in them or the investor will translate it alone. Four are named here in plain words. They are the ones a company selling software by subscription is judged on, and they sit alongside the terms Parts I and II already defined: revenue, **profit** (what is left of revenue after all the company’s costs), cash (the money actually received and held) and the valuation multiple, the number of times a year’s profit a buyer would pay for the company, on whichever profit measure the buyer uses. The investor’s profit measure is **EBITDA**, earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization: profit before the cost of borrowing, taxes and the accounting charges that spread the cost of equipment and purchased assets over years. It is a profit figure, not the cash in the bank; the chapter [Understand Cash Flow: Confirm the Cash Before You Commit](#obligations-before-budget) follows the money from it to cash.

- **Annual recurring revenue (ARR)** is a snapshot: the yearly value of the subscriptions customers currently pay for, counted as if every one of them renewed unchanged. It is not the revenue earned in a past year and not the cash received. It moves when customers sign, leave, grow or shrink. Larkspur’s subscriptions are yearly.
- **Net revenue retention (NRR)** takes the customers the company had a year ago and compares their recurring revenue now with their recurring revenue then, counting the ones who bought more, the ones who cut back and the ones who left. Suppose those customers were worth 100 a year ago; since then some bought 20 more, some cut back by 10, and some left, taking 5. Their revenue now is 105, and NRR is 105%. Above 100% means the existing customers grew more than they shrank. Signing a new customer does not change the figure, and neither does lowering the cost of serving the old ones. It counts revenue, not customers, which is why the renewal rate beside it in the pyramid can stay at 100% while it falls. It is the business outcome Larkspur agreed on, expressed the way the investor will read it.
- **Gross margin** starts from revenue less the direct cost of serving customers. That remainder, as an amount, is gross profit; gross margin is the same remainder expressed as a share of revenue. The direct costs include **hosting**, the computers on which the online service runs, plus support and setup. Staff hours per setup are part of that direct cost, which is why they feed gross margin without touching net revenue retention.
- **Cash conversion** is how much of the profit becomes cash in the bank, after adjustments such as the timing of customer payments and the company’s own bills.

Now the clock, and where each measure’s clock starts. Numbered project days count from day 0, the board meeting that adopted the plan: day 20 was the baseline, and day 90 is the first cohort review. A **quarter** is three months. The customer measures run on each customer’s own dates instead: **signing**, when the contract is signed; the start of that customer’s setup, once the changed setup has **gone live**, meaning it is in use for pilot customers; and **activation**, when the customer’s account is ready to use. Those dates need not coincide with each other or with the project days, so the table gives each measure its own trigger and the window in which it can be read. It is this scenario’s expected observation schedule, not a general law.

| Measure | Its clock starts at | When it can be read |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Staff hours per setup | The start of each pilot customer’s setup | When that setup finishes, within weeks |
| First-schedule weeks | Each customer’s signing | Within a quarter of signing, since the current median wait is about ten weeks |
| Month-one support requests | Each customer’s activation | One month after activation |
| Gross margin | The company’s quarterly accounts | Every quarter regardless; what takes time is enough pilot customers to move the company-wide figure |
| Renewal rate and NRR for the pilot customers | Each customer’s signing | At one review, that customer’s first contract anniversary, about a year after signing, because subscriptions renew yearly; company-wide NRR moves later, as pilot customers become a larger share of all customers |
| Day-90 cohort review | Day 0, the board meeting that adopted the plan | Day 90, reading whichever leading measures the eight customers have reached by then |

Sales is read on this quarter’s new contracts and customer success on this year’s renewals; product’s work shows in the customer measures within a quarter and in retention at the anniversaries. Grading product on this quarter’s ARR buys feature trading, work chosen to win a contract this month, and nothing that builds on itself.

The table above gives every row.](private-techuity/posts/15-adopt-outcome-thinking/assets/images/15-adopt-outcome-thinking/investor-bridge-on-an-honest-clock.jpeg)

**Figure 3:** *The investor bridge on an honest clock. Each measure has its own trigger and its own window: the customer measures run from that customer’s setup start, signing or activation, gross margin from the quarterly accounts, and the renewal rate and net revenue retention from each signing to its first anniversary. Project days such as day 90 count from day 0, the board meeting that adopted the plan, on a separate clock, so a customer’s signing, setup and activation need not coincide with each other or with the review. Ines takes this to the investor director as a negotiation, not a principle. Her proposal: product is reported on its leading indicators now, every quarter, on the record’s definitions. The lagging indicators are reported when they arrive, at the anniversaries, and product is judged on them then.

The director’s reply is the counterargument the chapter owes. The investor’s **holding period**, the time it expects to own the company before selling, is about four years. It cannot spend a year or more of them waiting to learn whether the thesis holds.

The answer is not to refuse the clock but to date it. The first **cohort**, the group of eight customers followed together through the new setup, goes through it by day 90. If their hours and their first-schedule weeks do not move, the thesis is in doubt long before any renewal. The chapter [Plan the First Hundred Days: Turn Expectations Into Funded Work](#first-hundred-days) sets the review that decides it. The evidence that would change the judgment is written into the record: leading indicators flat at day 90 reopen the outcome pair, not just the plan.

## Balance Is an Allocation

Customer value and business value are a sequence, not a trade-off. **Customer value** is the benefit customers get; **value capture** is the revenue and profit the company takes from delivering it. Customer value creates the opportunity for value capture; charging more than the company delivers is **over-monetization**, and it shows up as complaints, customers leaving and declining trust. How soon depends on customer choices and contract terms: at Larkspur, where subscriptions renew yearly, a pricing mistake would show mostly at the anniversaries, up to a year later. So the record puts customer value first in time and business value first in the investor conversation, and the pyramids keep the two honest with each other.

Balance then has to become a budget, or it stays a slogan. Larkspur classifies every planned item as **innovation** (work that advances the vision; the second-country capability, if the board approves it), **iteration** (improvements to the existing product; the setup work) or **operation** (work needed to run, secure and maintain it; the recovery work). It decides the split between the three before ranking anything and compares items only within their category, so a vision bet, an uncertain investment in a future capability, never loses head-to-head to a bug fix, the correction of a software fault, and a loud customer’s request competes for the iteration slice rather than for the whole plan.

The outcome record decides what enters each slice. An item enters by one of three routes:

- **It moves a measure** in either pyramid, as the setup work moves staff hours.
- **It protects an outcome or meets an obligation** the company has. The recovery objective is the example: a company that cannot restore its scheduling service will not keep the customers it has.
- **It tests an assumption** the record depends on, such as an experiment that could disprove the link from a fast start to renewal.

Obligations and safeguards enter the operation slice by the second route, each with its own record. The recovery objective’s record is the chapter [Build And Test Resilience: Backups Are Not Enough](#prove-you-can-restore), owned by Alex. An item that does none of the three does not enter at all. The allocation itself is revisited when an obligation or the evidence changes.

The ranking inside the slices, against the cash and the team time the board approves, is the chapter [Set Priorities: You Cannot Fund Everything at Once](#cannot-fund-everything). The author’s record [Balanced Roadmap](https://zeljkoobrenovic.github.io/spec-driven-journals/grounded-product-management/balanced-roadmap.html) gives the allocation method in full.

## What Outcome Thinking Costs

“Be outcome-oriented” proclaimed as a mindset leaves teams feeling inadequate and changes nothing. Outcome thinking has a price list, and John Cutler’s is the honest one: a causal model, and the habits that keep it alive, in [TBM 435: 20 Unfiltered Operating Takes](https://cutlefish.substack.com/p/tbm-435-20-unfiltered-operating-takes), an issue of his newsletter *The Beautiful Mess*. Larkspur pays it in three ways.

- **Lanes, not pyramids, are where the work happens.** A **lane** is a standing area of a team’s work aimed at one result. Each team carries a few of them, each with a couple of measures, stable for a quarter or two, revisited on a rhythm and changed when they stop making sense. The onboarding team, which gets new customers set up, has two lanes for the next two quarters: *staff hours per setup and the manual share* and *customer data readiness*. The pyramids are the reporting view that connects the lanes to the investor’s numbers; nobody works inside a pyramid. The author’s record [Lanes](https://zeljkoobrenovic.github.io/spec-driven-journals/grounded-product-management/lanes.html) argues this at length, including why the North Star Framework, which links one central measure to the inputs that drive it, is a teaching tool rather than an operating system. [The North Star Playbook](https://amplitude.com/north-star)
- **Goals are mixed and habitual.** Learning goals next to delivery goals next to outcome goals; goals on leading inputs and on lagging results; set, revisited and retired on a rhythm. The habit is the asset; any single goal is expendable. The failure modes are twins: going through the motions of setting goals, where the numbers mean nothing, and the afterthought metric, bolted onto a wish list to satisfy the format.
- **Powerful ideas imperfectly measured beat perfect measures of weak ideas.** A measurement bar that only cheap, already-measured ideas can clear is a filter that selects for weakness. Larkspur holds the ambition fixed, a customer scheduling within weeks rather than ten, and lets the measurement improve: the first cohort is measured by hand from time records, and the automatic recording follows. The author’s record [Goals and Questions](https://zeljkoobrenovic.github.io/spec-driven-journals/grounded-product-management/goals-and-questions.html) is the fuller argument.

The anti-patterns are the record read backwards: the **feature factory** (long release notes, no outcome anyone can name); the **vanity dashboard** (usage up while customers leave); the **afterthought metric**; the **measurement veto** (a strong idea killed for an imperfect metric while a weak one proceeds on its dashboard); **grading product on the sales clock**; and the **deckware pyramid**, built to align everyone, admired once, consulted by nobody who does work.

One more cost belongs to the leader rather than the team. The record only holds if the executives around product treat it as a partnership rather than a queue of requests: sales bringing commitments into the process that assesses new work instead of straight onto the plan, finance reading team time as well as cash, the investor director hearing the timetable argument as a plan rather than an excuse. The author’s record [Business Collaboration](https://zeljkoobrenovic.github.io/spec-driven-journals/grounded-product-management/business-collaboration.html) treats that relationship; here it is enough to say that the record is written with those people in the room, not presented to them afterwards.

## The Outcome Record

| Element | What was agreed |
| --- | --- |
| Customer outcome and metric | A new customer is scheduling real work within a few weeks of signing, without the specialist setting up the account by hand. Metric: median weeks from contract to first real schedule (about ten). Main driver: staff hours per customer setup (about 80, all staff time). Both provisional in the week after closing, confirmed by the day-20 baseline of twelve setups |
| Business outcome and metric | Retention and expansion of paying customers. Metric: net revenue retention; supporting: renewal rate at anniversary, technicians and sites added. Hypothesis: a fast start raises renewal; tested at the first anniversaries by comparing customers who reached a first schedule within eight weeks with those who did not, read with the group sizes and the customers’ differences, and possibly inconclusive |
| The join | Share of customers reaching a first real schedule within eight weeks, read from the same dates as the median: a customer confirmation and a renewal input, the one measure in both pyramids. | Outside the pair, still reported | Cost to serve feeds gross margin. ## What to Say When the Investor Asks

**“What is your north star?”** A pair, not a number: customers scheduling real work within a few weeks of signing, and net revenue retention. The chain between them is written down, the leading measures are reported quarterly, and the first cohort reports at day 90.

**“Why is product not judged on this quarter’s ARR?”** Because the work that moves ARR durably shows first in staff hours and first-schedule weeks, then in month-one support requests, an early sign for renewal, then in renewals at the anniversaries a year after each signing. Product is judged on the leading measures now and on the lagging ones when they arrive, and the dates are in the record. Judging it on this quarter’s new contracts would buy feature trading.

**“Which of our metrics does product actually move?”** Directly, the leading measures: staff hours per setup, the manual share, data readiness, first-schedule weeks, month-one support requests. Through them, gross margin, and renewal and expansion, which product influences through the customer’s experience without being solely accountable for them; customer success and the people who set prices answer for the renewal rate. New customers signed are sales’ accountability. The setup experience and the capabilities on offer affect whether a prospect buys, but the record does not attribute this quarter’s signings to the setup work, and product is not judged on them.

With the record agreed, Larkspur can choose. Three requests, one limit of cash and engineer-weeks (one engineer’s work for one week), and the record saying which measures each request moves: that is where the chapter [Set Priorities: You Cannot Fund Everything at Once](#cannot-fund-everything) begins.

## Questions to Consider

1. *Write your key customer outcome in a customer’s words, with its metric. Would the metric still matter if your product changed completely, and have customers confirmed they would pay or renew to get it?*
2. *Which one business outcome has your leadership agreed that product optimizes for, which investor measure does it feed, and when can the effect first be observed?*
3. *Take any node out of your pyramid. Does next week’s work change, or only a slide? And which of your links are measured rather than assumed?*
4. *Which of the numbers your investor reads is product accountable for, which does it only influence, and which does it affect only indirectly? Is that boundary written down?*
