# 18. Adopt Outcome Thinking: Bridge Product Work to Investor's Numbers

![Adopt Outcome Thinking: Bridge Product Work to Investor's Numbers — logo](private-techuity/posts/15-adopt-outcome-thinking/assets/images/15-adopt-outcome-thinking/logo.jpeg)

> **IN THIS SECTION, YOU WILL:** Learn to write an outcome record, a one-page note that names one change the company wants for its customers and one business result that change should drive. You will then use the record to connect product work to the investor's numbers on a realistic timetable, so you can say when results should show and not just report that work shipped.

> **WHY INVESTORS CARE:** The investor judges the company by a few numbers, such as revenue growth, customer retention, and cash burn. It wants to know which of these numbers the product work moves and when the change will show. A team can ship every item on its plan and still change nothing that matters: the work gets done, but the numbers stay flat, and the investor's money earns no return.

> **WHY YOU SHOULD CARE:** Without one agreed pair of outcomes (a customer change and a business result), planning becomes a contest of who asks loudest, and product is judged on this quarter's sales. The outcome record helps you build what customers value and show when that work should move the investor's numbers.

> **KEY POINTS:**
>
> * **Judge work by outcomes, not outputs.** An output is something delivered: a feature, a release. An outcome is a measurable change in what customers or the company can do. A delivered feature is a cost until an outcome moves.
> * **Pair one customer outcome with one business outcome, and write down the chain between them.** Each breaks into factors and measures, with a name against every measure: what product is accountable for, what it only influences, and what belongs to sales and to customer success, the team that helps existing customers get value from the software.
> * **Bridge the business outcome to the investor's numbers on an honest clock.** Say which investor measure it feeds, when each measure can first be read, and have product judged on leading measures now and on lagging ones when they arrive. Then date the clock: a first cohort that reports within the quarter, so a thesis in doubt shows long before any renewal.

> **[DYSFUNCTIONS THIS SECTION ADDRESSES](#where-investment-goes-wrong):**
>
> * **The Nodding Room** — Everyone agrees to a goal, but no one has defined it. This section defines the customer outcome, the business outcome and the review dates, so each person can say what the goal is and when it will be checked.
> * **The Ratchet Roadmap** — Each release adds work that is never removed, and the plan only grows. This section requires proposed work to show how it contributes to an outcome before it becomes a commitment, so work that can't show this is not added.

A new investment makes more activity possible, but **being busy** is not the same as getting results. A team can deliver everything on its plan and still leave customers, the company, and the investor no better off. Investors often ask for a **north star**, the single measure the whole company steers by. Answer with a **feature list** and the investor learns nothing. Answer with one number and the team learns nothing, because a single figure doesn't show what to do to move it.

This chapter answers a harder question: what should a team show, to investors and to itself, that the new money is producing results? The answer is an **outcome record**. It names one **change for customers**, one **business result** that change should feed, the measures between them, who is responsible for each measure, and the date each is reviewed. The **product-management** literature listed at the end covers the method well, so the chapter takes only what a leader under investors needs. Most of its length goes to what that literature leaves out: the **bridge from the outcome record to the investor's numbers**.

## Setting Up the Example: Rotaline After Closing

Rotaline, the fictional scheduling-software company this book follows, sells software that plans maintenance work for businesses whose staff work in the field. Alex is chief technology officer (CTO), Priya leads **product**, the team that decides what the software should do and for whom, Ines is the chief executive, Sam leads finance, and Morgan is the technology adviser to Rotaline’s investor. Every figure is fictional.

It is the week after **closing**, the day the investment was completed. Within days the **board**, the group that oversees the company, will meet to adopt the operating plan. That meeting will be **day 0**, and the chapters count project days from it.

The plan agreed at closing expects **50% more new customers** in the first year without a larger setup team, and a second country only if a **pilot**, a small trial of the changed setup with a few customers, produces **evidence** that the setup can carry it. The investor’s original ambition of a second country within twelve months was cut back by diligence; [Use Diligence: Your Last Chance to Shape the Plan](#use-diligence) records why.

Priya has three requests: a **new customer portal**, a website through which customers manage their own accounts; **simpler setup** for new customers; and the **recovery work** Alex wants, so the service can be restored quickly after a failure. [Set Priorities: Say No With Evidence, Not Opinion](#set-priorities) will choose among them. Before it can, someone must say what the work is *for*: Rotaline could deliver a setup feature next quarter and still change nothing for its customers, if the delay they suffer comes from somewhere else.

{id: adopt-outcome-thinking--outcomes-not-outputs}
## Outcomes, Not Outputs

An **output** is what the company delivered: a feature, a release, a redesigned screen. An **outcome** is a measurable change in the customer’s or the company’s reality: a customer who can do something new, a cost that fell, a customer who renewed. A **key performance indicator (KPI)** is a number chosen to track an outcome. A **leading indicator** moves first, while the work is still going on; a **lagging indicator** confirms later that the outcome happened. A **vanity metric** (logins, clicks, pages viewed) rises for reasons unrelated to whether customers succeeded, and counts only once someone has shown how it connects to a customer or business result.

The test for a strong customer metric: it would still matter if the product changed completely, and customers confirm they would pay, renew or buy more to get it. Rotaline’s customers do not want a **setup wizard**, a guided sequence of setup screens; they want to be scheduling real work soon after they signed. That sentence survives any redesign. “Share of customers who finish the wizard” does not.

| What outcome thinking says | What it does **not** say |
| --- | --- |
| Every piece of work names, before it starts, the outcome it should move | No work starts until the measures are perfect |
| A delivered feature is a cost until an outcome moves | Delivering does not matter (delivery is how outcomes move; it is just never the finish line) |
| Usage counts only when it clearly connects to value | Usage data is useless |
| Product is judged over quarters and years | Product is never accountable (the lagging indicators still arrive and are still reported) |

{id: adopt-outcome-thinking--one-customer-outcome-one-business-outcome}
## One Customer Outcome, One Business Outcome

**The customer outcome first.** Priya writes it in the customer’s language, after asking six of them: *a new customer is scheduling real work within a few weeks of signing, without Rotaline’s specialist setting the account up by hand.* Its metric is the **median** weeks from signed contract to the first real schedule, the middle value when customers are lined up from fastest to slowest. Its main driver is the **staff hours per customer setup**: everyone’s time, not the specialist’s alone, though the specialist’s manual configuration is the part that holds the queue, because only one person can do it.

Both numbers are provisional in the week after closing. Morgan’s diligence sample of five recent setups gave about 80 hours of staff time each, three of the five needing manual configuration, and the customers’ own accounts put the wait at about ten weeks. The day-20 baseline from time records for all twelve setups of the previous two quarters later confirms both; [Plan the First Hundred Days: Where Expectations Become Commitments](#first-hundred-days) shows how it is built. Three of the six customers said the wait had delayed their own rollout and two had considered a competitor. That validates the outcome, not any particular feature, and it does not yet show that a faster start makes a customer renew. That link is the chapter’s working hypothesis, and the record says when it will be tested.

**Then the business outcome, and only one.** Ines asks her executive team and the investor-appointed director, the board member the investor chose, to agree the one business result product should optimize for. Sales wants **bookings**, the value of new contracts signed. Finance wants **margin**, the share of **revenue** (the money earned from selling the service) left after the direct cost of serving customers. The investor’s **thesis**, its explanation of how the investment is meant to pay off, calls for more customers without a proportional increase in staff.

They agree on **retention and expansion of paying customers**: keeping the customers Rotaline already has and the revenue they pay, and existing customers paying to cover more technicians or locations. Entering a second country is a different thing. The hypothesis behind the choice is that a customer who starts fast is more likely to stay and to grow, and a company that cannot start customers fast cannot take on the volume the thesis needs. The other results belong to other functions. Why only one of each? A record with three customer outcomes and four business outcomes lets every request find a home and decides nothing.

## Two Pyramids and the Chain Between Them

Broken into the factors that drive it and the measures that track them, an outcome lets the leader trace every planned item to the measure it claims to move. Rotaline draws two **pyramids**, one per outcome, each with three levels: outcome, factors, measures. My [Productscape](https://zeljkoobrenovic.github.io/productscape-examples/start-packages/overview/index.html) site works the same shape out for fifty product domains, for readers who want ready-made examples.

**Customer-outcome pyramid.** Top: median weeks from contract to first real schedule, and beside it the share of customers who reach a first real schedule within eight weeks, read from the same dates.

- **Setup effort.** Staff hours per customer setup (leading; about 80). Share of setups needing the specialist’s manual configuration (leading; three of five in the diligence sample).
- **Customer data readiness.** Share of customer data arriving clean enough to load (leading). Days from contract to the customer returning its data (leading).
- **Early use.** Share of customers scheduling real work in the first week after activation (lagging). Setup-related **support requests** in the first month (lagging).

**Business-outcome pyramid.** Top: **net revenue retention** of paying customers, the investor’s measure of whether existing customers’ **recurring revenue**, the subscription payments that repeat each year, grew or shrank; the next section defines it. The measures beneath are a selection the teams can act on, not a breakdown of the figure.

- **Renewal.** Renewal rate at contract anniversary (lagging). It counts customers, not money: every customer could renew on a smaller subscription, leaving the rate at 100% while net revenue retention fell, so the record reads the two together. Share of customers who reached a first real schedule within eight weeks (leading; the join to the customer pyramid, and the link under test).
- **Expansion.** Technicians or locations that existing customers add within a year (lagging). Share of customers with every technician scheduled through the product by month three (leading).

Two things sit outside this pyramid on purpose. Staff hours per setup and month-one support requests are also **cost to serve**, so they feed **gross margin**, not net revenue retention, which counts revenue, not cost. New customers signed, including in a second country if the board approves it, feed **annual recurring revenue** and are sales’ work. The record lists both, but neither is the business outcome.

The pyramids are joined at one shared measure: the share of customers reaching a first real schedule within eight weeks, a customer confirmation and a renewal input in the same statistic. Two links are under test. That the hours cause the wait is the working explanation, and the first cohort is its first test. That a fast start raises renewal is tested at the first anniversaries by comparing the renewal rate of customers who reached a first schedule within eight weeks with the rate of those who did not; the groups are small, so the comparison may be inconclusive, and the record reopens the pair if it shows no difference or cannot be read.

**Attach responsibility to measures, not to levels.** The record names who answers for each measure:

- **Product:** staff hours per setup, the manual share and the first-schedule weeks.
- **Shared:** data readiness (product owns the tools, the customer owns the records, **customer success** chases the data); early use and month-one support requests (product, customer success, support).
- **Customer success and the people who set prices:** renewal rate. **Sales and customer success:** expansion.

Product still influences renewal and expansion through the customer’s experience, and the record says so. A product leader who claims the renewal rate will be blamed for it; one who disclaims any effect on it will be ignored.

![Two pyramids, customer outcome and business outcome, each with outcome, factors and measures, joined at one shared measure: the share of customers reaching a first real schedule within eight weeks. The pyramid and responsibility lists above give the full content.](private-techuity/posts/15-adopt-outcome-thinking/assets/images/15-adopt-outcome-thinking/two-pyramids-joined.jpeg)

**Figure 1:** *The two pyramids and the chain between them, each with outcome, factors and measures. The share of customers reaching a first real schedule within eight weeks sits in both: it confirms the customer outcome and, if the hypothesis holds, feeds renewal. Staff hours per setup are the suspected cause of the wait and also feed gross margin, outside the retention pyramid. The tag under each pair of measures says who is accountable.*

One pruning rule keeps the pyramids honest. Take any measure out: if next week’s work changes, it is useful; if only a slide changes, it is decoration. Rotaline’s week-one scheduling measure survives only once Priya ties an action to it: a customer that has not scheduled by the end of its first week gets a check-in call. In my experience many measures fail this test, and the pruning is the point.

## The Investor Bridge: Turning Outcomes into Numbers the Investor Trusts

The investor reads Rotaline through a few numbers, and the outcome record must end in them or the investor will translate it on its own. They are the ones a company selling software by subscription is judged on, alongside the terms Parts I and II already defined: revenue, **profit** (what is left of revenue after all costs), cash, and the investor’s profit measure **EBITDA**, earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, which is a profit figure, not the cash in the bank; [Understand Cash Flow: Confirm the Cash Before You Commit](#understand-cash-flow) follows the money from it to cash.

- **Annual recurring revenue (ARR)** is a snapshot: the yearly value of the subscriptions customers currently pay for, counted as if every one renewed unchanged. It moves when customers sign, leave, grow or shrink. Rotaline’s subscriptions are yearly.
- **Net revenue retention (NRR)** takes the customers the company had a year ago and compares their recurring revenue now with their recurring revenue then, counting the ones who bought more, the ones who cut back and the ones who left. Suppose those customers were worth 100 a year ago; since then some bought 20 more, some cut back by 10, and some left, taking 5. Their revenue now is 105, and NRR is 105%. Signing a new customer does not change the figure, and neither does lowering the cost of serving the old ones. It counts revenue, not customers. It is the business outcome Rotaline agreed on, expressed the way the investor will read it.
- **Gross margin** is revenue less the direct cost of serving customers, as a share of revenue. The direct costs include **hosting**, the computers on which the online service runs, plus support and setup, which is why staff hours per setup feed gross margin without touching net revenue retention.
- **Cash conversion** is how much of the profit becomes cash in the bank, after adjustments such as the timing of customer payments and the company’s own bills.

Each measure then needs its own clock. Project days count from day 0: day 20 brings the baseline, and day 90 is the first cohort review. The customer measures run on each customer’s own dates instead: **signing**, the start of that customer’s setup once the changed setup has **gone live** for pilot customers, and **activation**, when the account is ready to use. Those dates need not coincide with each other or with the project days, so the table gives each measure its own trigger and reading window. It is this scenario’s expected schedule, not a general law.

| Measure | Its clock starts at | When it can be read |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Staff hours per setup | The start of each pilot customer’s setup | When that setup finishes, within weeks |
| First-schedule weeks | Each customer’s signing | Within a quarter of signing, since the current median wait is about ten weeks |
| Month-one support requests | Each customer’s activation | One month after activation |
| Gross margin | The company’s quarterly accounts | Every quarter regardless; what takes time is enough pilot customers to move the company-wide figure |
| Renewal rate and NRR for the pilot customers | Each customer’s signing | At one review, that customer’s first contract anniversary, about a year after signing, because subscriptions renew yearly; company-wide NRR moves later, as pilot customers become a larger share of all customers |
| Day-90 cohort review | Day 0, the board meeting that adopts the plan | Day 90, reading whichever leading measures the eight customers have reached by then |

Sales is read on this quarter’s new contracts and customer success on this year’s renewals; product’s work shows in the customer measures within a quarter and in retention at the anniversaries. Grading product on this quarter’s ARR buys feature trading, work chosen to win a contract this month, and nothing that builds on itself.

![A chart of six measures, each with the date its clock starts and the window in which it can be read, and the day-90 cohort review on a separate project-day clock that starts at the board meeting, day 0. The table above gives every row.](private-techuity/posts/15-adopt-outcome-thinking/assets/images/15-adopt-outcome-thinking/investor-bridge-on-an-honest-clock.jpeg)

**Figure 2:** *The investor bridge on an honest clock. Each measure has its own trigger and window: the customer measures run from that customer’s setup start, signing or activation, gross margin from the quarterly accounts, and the renewal rate and net revenue retention from each signing to its first anniversary. Project days such as day 90 run on a separate clock from day 0, the board meeting that adopts the plan.*

Ines takes this to the investor director as a negotiation, not a principle: product is reported on its leading indicators every quarter, on the record’s definitions, and judged on the lagging indicators when they arrive at the anniversaries. The director has a reason to push back. The investor’s **holding period**, the time it expects to own the company before selling, is about four years, and it cannot spend a year or more of it waiting to learn whether the thesis holds.

The answer is not to refuse the clock but to date it. The first **cohort**, the group of eight customers followed together through the new setup, goes through it by day 90. If their hours and their first-schedule weeks do not move, the thesis is in doubt long before any renewal. [Plan the First Hundred Days: Where Expectations Become Commitments](#first-hundred-days) sets the review that decides it. The evidence that would change the judgment is written into the record: leading indicators flat at day 90 reopen the outcome pair, not just the plan. That is the bridge: the investor gets an early, dated reading on the leading measures, and product gets a timetable on which the lagging ones are judged.

{id: adopt-outcome-thinking--balance-is-an-allocation}
## Balance Is an Allocation

Customer value and business value are a sequence, not a trade-off: the benefit customers get creates the opportunity for the revenue the company takes, and charging more than it delivers shows up as complaints and customers leaving, at Rotaline mostly at the yearly anniversaries. So the record puts customer value first in time and business value first in the investor conversation.

Balance must then become a budget, or it stays a slogan. Rotaline classifies every planned item as **innovation** (work that advances the vision; the second-country capability), **iteration** (improvements to the existing product; the setup work) or **operation** (running, securing and maintaining it; the recovery work), decides the split before ranking anything, and compares items only within their category, so a vision bet never loses head-to-head to a bug fix and a loud customer’s request competes for the iteration slice, not the whole plan.

An item enters a slice by one of three routes: it **moves a measure** in either pyramid, as the setup work moves staff hours; it **protects an outcome or meets an obligation**, as the recovery work does, with its own record in [Build and Test Resilience: Backups Are Not Enough](#build-test-resilience); or it **tests an assumption** the record depends on. An item that does none of the three does not enter at all.

{id: adopt-outcome-thinking--what-outcome-thinking-costs}
## What Outcome Thinking Costs

“Be outcome-oriented” proclaimed as a mindset leaves teams feeling inadequate and changes nothing. Outcome thinking has a price, and John Cutler lists it honestly in [TBM 435: 20 Unfiltered Operating Takes](https://cutlefish.substack.com/p/tbm-435-20-unfiltered-operating-takes), an issue of his newsletter *The Beautiful Mess*: a causal model, and the habits that keep it alive. Rotaline pays it in three ways.

- **Lanes, not pyramids, are where the work happens.** A **lane** is a standing area of a team’s work aimed at one result, with a couple of measures, stable for a quarter or two. The onboarding team has two for the next two quarters: *staff hours per setup and the manual share* and *customer data readiness*. The pyramids are the reporting view that connects the lanes to the investor’s numbers; the North Star Framework is a teaching tool rather than an operating system. [The North Star Playbook](https://amplitude.com/north-star)
- **Goals are mixed and habitual.** Learning, delivery and outcome goals, set, revisited and retired on a rhythm; the habit is the asset.
- **Powerful ideas imperfectly measured beat perfect measures of weak ideas.** Rotaline holds the ambition fixed, a customer scheduling within weeks rather than ten, and lets the measurement improve: the first cohort is measured by hand from time records, and automatic recording follows.

The anti-patterns are the record read backwards: the **feature factory** (long release notes, no outcome anyone can name); the **vanity dashboard** (usage up while customers leave); the **afterthought metric**; the **measurement veto** (a strong idea killed for an imperfect metric); **grading product on the sales clock**; and the **deckware pyramid**, admired once, consulted by nobody who does work. The record only holds if the executives around product treat it as a partnership rather than a queue of requests, and the investor director hears the timetable argument as a plan rather than an excuse.

## What to Say When the Investor Asks

**“What is your north star?”** A pair, not a number: customers scheduling real work within a few weeks of signing, and net revenue retention. The chain between them is written down, the leading measures are reported quarterly, and the first cohort reports at day 90.

**“Why is product not judged on this quarter’s ARR?”** Because the work that moves ARR durably shows first in staff hours and first-schedule weeks, then in month-one support requests, an early sign for renewal, then in renewals at the anniversaries a year after each signing. Product is judged on the leading measures now and on the lagging ones when they arrive, and the dates are in the record. Judging it on this quarter’s new contracts would buy feature trading.

**“Which of our metrics does product actually move?”** Directly, the leading measures: staff hours per setup, the manual share, data readiness, first-schedule weeks, month-one support requests. Through them, gross margin, and renewal and expansion, which product influences through the customer’s experience without being solely accountable for them. Sales is accountable for new customers signed; the setup experience affects whether a prospect buys, but the record does not attribute this quarter’s signings to the setup work.

With the record agreed, Rotaline gets its numbers ready, each measure defined, owned and labelled before it reaches the investor, in [Have Your Numbers Ready: One Set for Investors, Goals and Teams](#have-your-numbers-ready). Then it chooses among the three requests against cash and team time, with the record saying which measures each request moves: [Set Priorities: Say No With Evidence, Not Opinion](#set-priorities).

{id: adopt-outcome-thinking--questions-to-consider}
## Questions to Consider

1. *Write your key customer outcome in a customer’s words, with its metric. Would the metric still matter if your product changed completely, and have customers confirmed they would pay or renew to get it?*
2. *Which one business outcome has your leadership agreed that product optimizes for, which investor measure does it feed, and when can the effect first be observed?*
3. *For each measure your investor reads, what starts its clock and when can it first be read? Is there a dated early reading, like the day-90 cohort, that could put the thesis in doubt before the lagging measures arrive?*
4. *Which of the numbers your investor reads is product accountable for, which does it only influence, and which does it affect only indirectly? Is that boundary written down?*
