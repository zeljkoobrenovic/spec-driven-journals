# 9. Management Equity, Fund Carry and Employee Jobs Are Different Bets

![Management Equity, Fund Carry and Employee Jobs Are Different Bets — logo](private-techuity/posts/06-different-bets/assets/images/06-different-bets/logo.jpeg)

> **IN THIS SECTION, YOU WILL:** Learn how management equity, fund carry and employee jobs are exposed differently to one decision, and how to pair a target with the measure it could damage.

> **WHY INVESTORS CARE:** Management equity is the investor’s tool for aligning company leaders, and carried interest is what aligns a fund’s manager with the fund’s own investors; if a payout rule rewards a damaging choice, the investor pays for it twice, in the outcome and in the price of the incentive.

> **WHY YOU SHOULD CARE:** “We are all shareholders now” hides the fact that executives, the fund, employees and customers are exposed differently to the same decision; incentives you misread will steer choices you did not intend.

> **KEY POINTS:**
>
> * Shared ownership **does not create identical interests**. Executives, the fund, employees and customers have different exposure, rights and time horizons; one proposed consolidation makes the four bets visible.
> * A **share percentage is only the beginning**. Payment priority, vesting, dilution and departure terms determine what an award can pay, and the company’s payout waterfall is not the fund’s.
> * Pair a target with **the measure it could damage**, and change the payout rule if it still rewards the damaging choice. Measurement alone cannot correct an incentive.

“We are all shareholders now” can describe a useful common interest. It can also conceal very different risks, rights and time horizons.

[Decide Who Decides, Before You Disagree](#decide-who-decides) established who may decide. **Incentives** are the rewards and consequences that encourage a particular choice. An investment may add new equity terms, exit expectations and fund-level incentives to the rewards and risks already shaping company decisions. Product and engineering leaders need to read that added layer because it shapes which proposals get approved and which behavior their own targets reward.

An **executive** is a senior leader responsible for managing the business, and may have most of their wealth and future employment tied to one company. A fund holds several investments, and its capital in this company is one of them. The fund’s manager earns fees and may receive **carried interest**, a contractual share of the fund’s profits that is normally worked out across all of the fund’s investments rather than on this company alone. Employees without equity can bear reorganization costs without sharing in the proceeds when the investor sells its holding, an **exit**. Customers can depend on a product long after the investor has sold.

Alignment is therefore a design problem, not something ownership confers automatically. This chapter reads the payout terms first, separates the company’s equity from the fund’s carried interest, follows one proposed consolidation through the four different bets, and ends with how to review a decision that incentives may have shaped.

{id: different-bets--three-different-equity-stories}
## Three Different Equity Stories

A founder might **roll over** part of their investment: keep shares or reinvest some of the sale proceeds into the new ownership arrangement. A manager might buy shares with personal money. Another executive might receive **options**, rights to buy shares on stated terms, or a share-based incentive whose payout depends on earning the rights over time or meeting conditions, known as **vesting**, and on specified value thresholds. These arrangements can produce very different results even when everyone is described as having management equity.

| Arrangement | What was paid in | What is at risk | What it can pay |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Rollover | Part of the founder’s sale proceeds, reinvested | The reinvested amount, ranked behind any shares with priority | A share of the proceeds at the next sale, under the company’s payout rules |
| Purchased shares | The manager’s own cash | The cash paid, and the departure terms if the manager leaves | That share class’s portion of proceeds, after priorities |
| Options | Nothing until exercise | The exercise cost, unvested rights on departure, dilution from later rounds | Proceeds per share above the exercise price, after priorities |
| Share awards | Nothing, or a nominal price | Unvested awards on departure, dilution from later rounds, and tax that in some jurisdictions falls due before any sale | That share class’s portion of proceeds once vested, after priorities |

The documents determine which shares receive money first (**preferences**), how issuing more equity changes existing ownership or payouts (**dilution**), and what happens to a participant’s rights when they leave (**leaver provisions**). Different share classes can carry different payment and decision rights. Legal and tax treatment depends on jurisdiction and the actual instrument; the options row describes the illustrative option used later in this chapter, not every share plan. The practical task is to request worked outcomes and understand the exposure, not infer rights from a headline percentage.

In a fictional example, a manager is entitled to 5% of qualifying equity proceeds above a €100 million threshold. At €140 million of qualifying equity proceeds, 5% of the €40 million excess is €2 million. That is not 5% of €140 million. The real plan may calculate the pool differently; the example shows why the denominator, threshold and payment order matter.

Ask for scenarios covering a weak exit, a good exit, a refinancing, an additional acquisition funded with equity, and departure before exit. Ask which assumptions change the answer. A percentage without the **company payout waterfall**, the order and formula for allocating the company’s sale proceeds among its share classes, and a dilution explanation is incomplete information. The fund has a separate waterfall for dividing its proceeds between its investors and its manager; that side is covered in [Fund Economics: Fees, Distributions and Performance Reports](#fund-economics).

## Ask What Happens Before a Sale as Well as At One

A manager joining a venture-funded company needs scenarios for another round, a lower-priced round and no near-term chance to sell. A leader in a buyout may need to understand the value threshold above which management participates and the treatment of money rolled into the deal. Under a corporate parent, rewards may depend on parent shares, business-unit targets or integration milestones. These are possible arrangements to investigate, not standard packages guaranteed by investor type.

Take a fictional option example. Alex has vested rights to buy 10,000 shares at €2 each. A hypothetical sale pays €3 per underlying ordinary share, after the relevant priorities, and the options can be exercised or settled on those terms. Gross value after the €20,000 exercise cost is €10,000, before tax or fees. Quoting “10,000 shares worth €30,000” would omit the cost of obtaining them. A future round can also change the fully diluted ownership percentage, the percentage after including the specified options and other rights to shares.

Ask finance and the relevant advisers to show which assumptions determine payment and whether cash is required before any proceeds are available. For team communication, distinguish an illustrative value from money employees can receive. A funding announcement can change a valuation while leaving employees without a sale opportunity.

{id: different-bets--your-shares-and-the-funds-carry-are-two-separate-bets}
## Your Shares and the Fund’s Carry Are Two Separate Bets

Company management equity is usually a stake in one company, held under that company’s own payout rules and leaver terms, and it rewards an outcome in that company. Fund carry is a share of the fund’s profit, allocated under the fund’s own waterfall and normally computed on the fund’s whole result rather than on any one company, unless the fund agreement pays carry deal by deal. A successful company can therefore sit within a disappointing fund and earn its manager no carry at all, and fund economics can differ from what an executive experiences.

The Institutional Limited Partners Association (ILPA), which represents investors in private funds, addresses alignment through matters including the general partner’s own capital commitment, fee economics, carry and governance. These principles represent the perspective of investors in the fund; they don’t cover every employee or customer interest. [S02: ILPA principles](https://ilpa.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/06/ILPA-Principles-3.0_2019.pdf) The product leader’s task is to add the company and customer consequences to the discussion, not assume a fund-level alignment mechanism covers them.

Timing creates further differences. An investment partner may want a sale that produces a realized result. A founder may prefer continued independence. A CTO may need a transition to run through several product releases. None of these preferences is illegitimate. They become dangerous when one is presented as everyone’s objective.

## Measures Can Reward the Wrong Work

Suppose a company rewards executives solely for this year’s adjusted EBITDA: earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, with further stated exclusions. This is a measure of earnings, not cash after every obligation. A product transition will reduce earnings this year and protect a major customer segment next year. The incentive encourages delay even if delay reduces the business’s long-term value.

Adding a revenue target isn’t enough on its own, because growth can be bought. Here is one worked incentive choice, fictional, for Larkspur. The executive bonus pays on 25% annual revenue growth. Discounts, unprofitable implementation commitments and contracts whose onboarding costs more than the first year’s fees would all hit the target while damaging the company. So the rule pairs the target with what it could damage:

- **The target as counted.** Revenue growth counts only on contracts whose first-year **contribution**, revenue less the specified costs of serving the customer including implementation effort, is positive.
- **The paired guardrail.** Two measures, defined before the plan year starts. **Gross revenue retention** is this year’s revenue from the customers who were paying twelve months earlier, after their departures and reductions but before any additional sales to them, divided by what those same customers paid a year ago; new customers are excluded, the ratio cannot exceed 100%, and the guardrail is that it stays at or above 90%. **Net revenue retention** is the same ratio with additional sales to those customers included. It is reported beside the gross figure, not used as the guardrail, because expansion by some customers can hide the loss of others: if last year’s customers paid €10 million, then lost €1.2 million through departures and reductions and added €1.5 million of new purchases, net retention is 103% and gross retention is 88%. The net figure passes, the gross figure fails, and the gross figure is the one that describes the harm. Public software companies define these ratios in their own ways, which is why the plan writes its definition down: [S75: Fastly’s fourth-quarter 2020 shareholder letter](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1517413/000151741321000007/ex991-shareholderletter123.htm), for example, reports a net expansion rate that counts only customers who are still customers at the period end, beside a net retention rate that includes those who left. Each quarter Sam measures both on the customers who were paying in the same quarter a year earlier. The third measure is customer waiting time from contract to go-live, which stays within the agreed onboarding target.
- **Who reviews.** Sam prepares the growth, retention and waiting-time figures each quarter from the definitions used in board reporting; Ines and the board committee that sets executive pay review them; Priya is accountable for the service measure.
- **What changes when the guardrail fails.** The growth component is withheld for that quarter and re-examined at year end. Sales commissions follow the same logic, because the executive bonus is not the only payout a discounted contract triggers: from the start of the plan year, commission on a contract sold with a discount or implementation commitment that makes its first-year contribution negative is paid half at signature and half at first renewal. That rule is written into the commission plan before the year begins; it is not applied afterwards to contracts already sold.

Measurement cannot fix a payout rule that rewards the damaging choice. If the bonus still paid on gross revenue, tracking retention would only record what the growth had cost. The rule itself, what counts and when it pays, has to change. **Guardrails**, limits intended to reveal or prevent harmful side effects, narrow the range of misleading stories; they don’t remove judgment.

A compensation plan also needs to distinguish outcomes people can influence from **conditions they cannot control**. It is reasonable for shareholders to care about exit value. It is much less useful to treat a general fall in market multiples as proof that the engineering organization failed.

## One Consolidation, Four Different Bets

The payout rules above only matter once a decision sets them against each other. In a fictional scenario with its own assumptions, Larkspur bought an add-on scheduling product last year. It came with a second support location of fourteen people and thirty customers who use the add-on. The board, encouraged by the investor, proposes closing the second location within nine months and merging support onto the main team. The saving is about €600,000 a year in payroll and premises once the location is closed; one-off severance is about €350,000. In this scenario the fund holds 70% of Larkspur’s ordinary equity.

| Bet | What the consolidation offers | What it puts at risk | Clock |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Executives’ management equity | If the full €600,000 saving is still in earnings at a sale and a buyer pays an assumed ten-times earnings multiple, equity value rises by about €6 million; for the executive on the 5%-above-€100 million plan from the earlier example, with the threshold already passed, about €300,000 more | The saving evaporating in lost renewals, and the executive’s own position if the plan fails | The exit, two to four years away |
| The fund’s stake and its manager’s carry | The fund’s share of the same €6 million of company equity value: about €4 million at its 70% holding, after the executive plan’s €300,000. The fund’s manager’s carry is a share of fund profit, not €6 million and not €4 million: at an illustrative 20% rate, about €800,000 of the €4 million, and less or nothing if the fund as a whole has not returned its capital and cleared any preferred return, because carry is normally computed on the fund’s whole result rather than on Larkspur alone unless the agreement pays it deal by deal ([Fund Economics: Fees, Distributions and Performance Reports](#fund-economics)) | The fund’s capital invested in Larkspur: if lost renewals cost more than the consolidation saves, the stake is worth less than before the decision, and the manager’s prospective carry on it falls with it. The fund’s other holdings spread its exposure across several companies; they do not make a loss on Larkspur smaller | The fund’s exit window: the saving has to sit in reported earnings for a period before a sale process starts |
| Employees at the second location | Nothing for the eleven who leave; relocation offers for three | Their jobs, and for those who stay, a merged team with a heavier load and a product to learn | Now: notice periods and severance in the coming quarter |
| Add-on customers | A larger support team, eventually | Losing the people who know their configurations during the two quarters in which thirty renewals fall | The renewal dates in the next two quarters, not the exit |

The €6 million is incremental company equity value, not anyone’s payout: it assumes a multiple nobody controls, an unchanged bridge from enterprise value to equity and a saving that has survived the renewals; [A Valuation Is an Estimate, Not a Fact](#valuation-is-an-estimate) explains why it is an estimate. The four rows are not four views of one gain. The executives and the fund are betting on a sale price, with different money at risk and different rules for what reaches them. The employees are betting their next job. The customers are betting that the people who understand their setup are still there at renewal.

Ines does not take the proposal as it stands. The company’s decision, recorded: the chosen option is a staged consolidation over twelve months: six of the eleven leave at month six, when the smaller floor is given up, and the rest leave when the location closes at month twelve, so roughly half of the run-rate saving starts at month seven and all of it at month thirteen. Three add-on specialists are kept on twelve-month retention payments of about €120,000 in total, with knowledge transfer scheduled before each renewal. Alternatives rejected: the nine-month closure, because it concentrates the customer risk in the renewal window, and no consolidation, because the second location’s costs are real and the board is entitled to expect them addressed. Funding: severance, retention and about €80,000 of contractor help to merge the two configuration systems, about €550,000 in all from operating cash, approved by the board because the total exceeds the €500,000 Ines is authorized to approve. Scarce capacity: the support lead’s time and six engineer-weeks. Authorized: the board, on Ines’s recommendation. Evidence that would change the decision: the add-on customers’ renewal rate falling below last year’s, or the waiting-time measure breached for two consecutive months, either of which pauses the next stage.

Annual run rate and cash saved in year one are different numbers. The €600,000 is what the closed location saves in a full year; nothing is saved until people have left and premises have been given up. Under the nine-month closure the saving would run for the last three months of year one, about €150,000, against €350,000 of severance: a net €200,000 out in year one and the full €600,000 from year two. Under the staged plan, half the run rate runs from month seven and all of it from month thirteen, so year one also saves about €150,000, against €550,000 of one-off cost: a net €400,000 out. The staging therefore costs €200,000 more than the closure it replaces, all of it retention and contractor help, and moves the month from which the full saving is reliable from the tenth to the thirteenth. From year two both plans save the same €600,000, if the renewals hold. That three-month delay is what keeps the fund waiting: a sale process wants the full saving visible in reported earnings for a period before it starts, and the pressure does not disappear because the record is tidy.

{id: different-bets--who-carries-the-unresolved-burden}
## Who Carries the Unresolved Burden

The record is honest about what it does not solve. Eleven people lose their jobs. Severance and a reference are obligations, not compensation for an interrupted career; no company measure offsets that cost, and the sale price that rewards the executives and the fund does not reach them. The add-on customers’ risk is reduced by staging, not removed. A decision record should say so, with the expected distribution of benefits and burdens, rather than claim that enterprise value makes everyone better off. What the eleven are owed in notice and severance, what work stops in the second location and what the merged team is and is not expected to absorb are operating decisions with their own dates; [Anatomy of a Layoff](#anatomy-of-a-layoff) works a reduction through them.

Employees may value stable work, development, fair treatment and a viable company after exit. Customers may value continuity, reasonable prices, data portability and trustworthy service. Lenders prioritize contractual repayment and protections. These interests can conflict even when management and the financial sponsor share equity upside. A price increase can improve margin while reducing affordability; a platform consolidation can cut costs while forcing customers into an inferior migration. Such decisions need an explicit justification and mitigation.

Read an incentive plan as a set of possible outcomes: who receives a reward, what must happen first, and who bears the costs. Holding shares or sharing targets doesn’t settle those questions.

{id: different-bets--how-to-review-a-decision-that-incentives-may-have-shaped}
## How to Review a Decision That Incentives May Have Shaped

A useful review separates three judgments: the quality of the original decision given the available evidence, the quality of execution, and the result. An intelligent experiment can fail. A poorly justified decision can get lucky. Treating both simply as green or red teaches the wrong lesson, and if every admission leads straight to blame, people learn to report activity and defer interpretation. Reward the early report of a failed assumption, and **use the same facts** with the board and with the team.

The same scrutiny applies when the conclusion is about a person. When an executive’s payout or the fund’s exit timetable depends on a plan being achievable, the judgment that a leader is not up to it can be a convenient explanation for an unrealistic plan; [Headcount Is Not Capacity: Trace the Work Before You Hire](#fix-decisions-before-hiring) shows how to state and test the hypothesis behind a leadership change before recruiting.

Decision rights and incentives describe the arrangement on paper. The next chapter asks how to judge whether a particular investor will honor them when a plan misses, and how Ines compares two term sheets on evidence rather than promises: [Judge an Investor by Their Behavior Under Pressure](#investor-under-pressure).

{id: different-bets--questions-to-consider}
## Questions to Consider

1. *For your own equity or incentive arrangement, have you seen worked outcomes for a weak exit, a good exit, a refinancing, an equity-funded acquisition and your departure before exit?*
2. *Which measures does your compensation reward this year, what could they encourage you to delay or overdo, and would a paired measure change the payout or only record the damage?*
3. *Whose time horizon is driving the current plan: the fund’s, the founder’s, the executives’ or the customers’? Which of these is being presented as everyone’s objective?*
4. *Who bears costs from the current plan without sharing in its rewards: employees, customers, suppliers or lenders? Is that trade-off recorded anywhere?*

## To Probe Further

- **[The Holloway Guide to Equity Compensation](https://www.holloway.com/g/equity-compensation)** — Joshua Levy and Joe Wallin, Holloway, 2022 edition.  
  *Works through the mechanics behind Alex's option example in far more detail, from vesting and dilution to exercise cost and fully diluted percentages.*
- **[Managerial Incentives and Value Creation: Evidence from Private Equity](https://www.nber.org/papers/w14331)** — Phillip Leslie and Paul Oyer, NBER Working Paper 14331, 2008.  
  *Compares US private-equity-owned companies with public companies over 1996–2006, using 144 PE-owned firms that later went public: much stronger executive incentives and higher debt, but little evidence of better profitability or operating efficiency in that sample, useful when someone argues equity alone explains results.*
- **[The Economics of Private Equity Funds](https://academic.oup.com/rfs/article-abstract/23/6/2303/1569783)** — Andrew Metrick and Ayako Yasuda, Review of Financial Studies, 2010.  
  *Puts numbers on the fund side of "your shares and the fund's carry are two separate bets", showing that fees rather than carry are the larger share.*
- **[On the folly of rewarding A, while hoping for B](https://journals.aom.org/doi/10.5465/AME.1995.9503133466)** — Steven Kerr, Academy of Management Executive, 1995 reprint of the 1975 article.  
  *The classic short paper on reward systems that pay for one behavior while hoping for another, a vocabulary for the "measures can reward the wrong work" section.*
- **[Thinking in Bets: Making Smarter Decisions When You Don't Have All the Facts](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/552885/thinking-in-bets-by-annie-duke/)** — Annie Duke, Portfolio, 2018.  
  *A book on separating the quality of a decision from the quality of its outcome, which is the distinction this chapter asks a review to make.*
