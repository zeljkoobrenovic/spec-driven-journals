# 14. Choose the Right Help: Compare Investor Support With Other Options

![Choose the Right Help: Compare Investor Support With Other Options — logo](private-techuity/posts/11-choose-right-help/assets/images/11-choose-right-help/logo.jpeg)

> **IN THIS SECTION, YOU WILL:** Learn to name a capability gap, compare the investor’s people and networks with the help available elsewhere, and write the request for a first step.

> **WHY INVESTORS CARE:** A **fund manager**, the firm that pools other people’s money into a fund and decides where to invest it, often promises practical help to the companies it invests in. It makes that promise both to the people whose money it manages and to the founders it hopes to back. That help earns its cost only when it changes what the company can do.

> **WHY YOU SHOULD CARE:** Accepting whatever help the investor offers, rather than the help you actually need, costs your team scarce hours on work that does not close the gap, and can leave you relying on an investor-provided specialist or contact that nobody planned to keep. Comparing the investor's people and networks with outside options, such as a hire, a contractor, or a peer, first means the help you accept closes the gap you named.

> **KEY POINTS:**
>
> * Ask for the help a **specific company problem** requires. Describe the missing capability through the work that fails or takes too long, and say which kind of request you are making.
> * **Compare the investor’s access with the alternatives.** An investor may reach relevant people faster. A peer, an independent specialist or a hire may fit better. Judge each route by availability, company effort, cost and relevant experience.
> * **Borrow experience with its context attached**, and make any continuing dependence explicit. Finish with a written request that names the result, the source and the accountable company leader.

> **[DYSFUNCTIONS THIS SECTION ADDRESSES](#where-investment-goes-wrong):**
>
> * **The Dusty Address Book** — Starts with a capability gap and compares useful introductions with other sources of help.
> * **Borrowed Brains** — Makes the capability sought and any continuing dependence explicit in the request.

Sooner or later, most companies hit a **capability gap**: a skill or kind of experience the team lacks, so work in that area fails or drags on because nobody inside has done it well before. Typical examples are a **first security audit**, a **pricing change**, or a **migration** to a new platform. Many investors offer help with such gaps through their own specialists, the other companies they have invested in, and their wider contacts. That help can be fast and relevant. It can also be simply the help on hand rather than the help the gap needs, and accepting it still costs the team hours of briefing, meetings, and follow-up. So the useful question is not whether to accept the investor's help, but which source, inside or outside the investor's network, will leave the company able to do the work itself next time.

The investor gains when you get stronger: a **stronger company is worth more**, which raises the value of the investor's holding, the ownership stake it bought and will one day sell. But an investment firm that employs its own team of advisers, such as recruiters, security specialists, or go-to-market experts shared across its companies, also wants three other things: enough requests to keep that team busy and **justify its cost**, **goodwill** from the other companies it has invested in, and a **reputation** for hands-on support that attracts the next founder. So its offer of help is genuine but not neutral: the firm benefits whether or not its adviser is the best fit for your gap, and that is why you still need to compare it with other options.

The earlier chapter [Plan Investor Support: Match the Help to Company Priorities](#plan-investor-support) connected the investor–company working arrangement with the company’s own organization, authority and resources. The previous chapter, [Learn Through Your Investor’s Network: Knowledge, Peers and New Perspectives](#learn-through-investors-network), explored learning that can reveal new possibilities and needs. This chapter is about sourcing help. It starts with the company’s need, sets out four kinds of request, then compares credible sources for the same need, including sources outside the investor’s network. It ends with a written request, which the chapter [Set the Terms of Help: Agree the Work, Authority and Handover](#set-terms-of-help) turns into a **charter**: the short written agreement that fixes what the helper will do, for how long, at what cost and under whose direction.

## Setting Up the Example: Rotaline’s Onboarding Gap

Rotaline, the fictional scheduling-software company this book follows, has money to improve customer onboarding: the setup work, mostly configuration and cleaning the customer's data, before a new customer can schedule real work in the product. Today each setup takes about 80 hours of staff time and leans on one in-house specialist's manual work. **Nobody at Rotaline has designed a repeatable setup process before**, so the team does not know which steps to standardize or automate first. Hiring a permanent leader who has done it would take months. Asking the same engineers to work it out on top of their existing work would delay it further. Two later chapters, [Assess Capability: Can the Team Deliver?](#assess-capability) and [Scale the Team Up: Headcount Is Not Capacity](#scale-the-team-up), show how to find and confirm a gap like this by tracing the setup work through the company. This chapter skips that step: it assumes the gap is already known and focuses on choosing who should help close it.

{id: choose-right-help--name-the-capability-you-need}
## Name the Capability You Need

A **capability** is something the company can **do reliably and repeatedly**, without depending on luck or one heroic person: for example, understanding what a customer needs, releasing software without breaking it, or hiring for a specialist role. Describe the gap through the work that currently **fails or takes too long**, not through the help you think you want. "Each customer setup takes about 80 hours and depends on one specialist" names a gap; "we need an onboarding consultant" names a solution and skips the comparison.

“We need help with onboarding” is **too vague to act on**. The **due diligence** finding is not. Due diligence is the investigation the investor ran on Rotaline before deciding to invest, described in the chapter [Use Diligence: Correct the Plan Before It Is Signed](#use-diligence). It looked at five sampled **implementations**, meaning the setup of five customers, and recorded two things:

- In three of the five implementations, configuring the customer's setup needed the same implementation specialist's hands-on work. That person was a **bottleneck**: if they were unavailable, those setups stalled.
- Across all five, the average total team effort was about 80 hours per customer. That figure **covers everything** from signed contract to the customer going live (configuration, cleaning up the customer's data, sessions with the customer and rework), not the specialist's configuration alone.

The useful help might teach the customer team to run one standard setup path, remove a repeated manual step or change how customer data is taken in. **Any of these** would reduce the load on the one specialist and cut the roughly 80 hours each setup takes. An introduction to a large outsourcing provider does not show that the provider can do any of them. It might, but the only way to know is to **compare it with every other route** on the same terms.

Separate four kinds of requests: help to **understand the problem,** help to **make a decision**, people to **carry out the work**, and help **building the company's own skills**. One engagement can involve several. Agreeing up front on which ones are needed prevents advice from being mistaken for delivery capacity, which would leave the work undone while everyone believes it is covered. Rotaline mostly needs the third and fourth: someone to work alongside its engineer on one setup path and leave the team able to repeat it without them.

## Map the Possible Sources of Help

An investment firm’s **operating team**, where it has one, is a group of **in-house specialists** who help the companies the firm owns improve how they work. The firm’s **portfolio** is the set of businesses it has invested in, and those are its **portfolio companies**. A **peer network** links leaders in similar roles, such as two CEOs or two heads of operations, so they can share what has worked for them. The firm may also bring in outside specialists, suppliers, or people from its own contacts. A **corporate investor** is an operating company, not a fund, that has invested in another business. It may offer a service its own group already runs, or a **sales channel**, meaning an existing route to reach customers. Either offer comes with commercial conditions, such as pricing, exclusivity, or obligations to buy or sell through the investor, so check those terms before accepting.

KKR’s public description of its **operating arm**, KKR Capstone, shows what such a team can look like. It says Capstone works with company management, operating specialists, and external partners, and offers services in technology, growth, and people. That is only what the firm claims to offer. It does not show that the help works or that any particular company can get it. [S22: KKR Capstone description](https://www.kkr.com/approach/capstone)

Treat the table as a list of possible help to investigate, not a menu of services the firm will deliver. Each row names a company need and the kind of help that might meet it. Before you rely on any row, confirm that the firm or investor offers that help to your company, and on what terms.

| Company need | Help to explore | What to establish first |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Understand a new customer problem | Introductions to potential customers, experienced product leaders or research specialists | Are these people relevant to the market you intend to serve, or to the investor’s existing relationships? |
| Start a capability the company lacks | A temporary specialist, a peer example and practical training | Who in the company will learn the work and be accountable for it afterward? |
| Improve engineering delivery | Experienced engineering leaders, focused assessments or reusable tools | Which constraint should change, and what company effort is needed? |
| Hire for a critical role | Recruiting support, candidate introductions and help defining the job | Who selects the candidate, pays the costs and manages the person? |
| Develop leaders and teams | Coaching, training, mentoring and peer learning | Which capability should improve, and how are coaching and assessment kept apart ([Clarify the Adviser’s Role: Are They Helping, Assessing or Deciding?](#clarify-adviser-role))? |
| Make a supplier or expansion decision | **Procurement** expertise (finding, checking and contracting with suppliers), introductions to potential customers or partners, or experience in another market | Are the terms and experience relevant to this company’s actual needs? |
| Use a group shared service or channel (corporate investor) | Technical, security or sales capability the **parent** — the company that controls this one — already runs and shares across its businesses | Who else sees the data, what the service costs to leave, and what continues after an ownership change |
| Combine or separate businesses | People with experience of the particular transition | Can they help the company operate through the change, within agreed responsibilities? |

## Peer Communities as a Source of Help

[Learn Through Your Investor’s Network: Knowledge, Peers and New Perspectives](#learn-through-investors-network) covers continuing peer relationships and exploratory learning in depth. This section asks a narrower question: **can a peer help close the specific capability gap** you have already identified? If so, the peer's direct experience can shorten the path. If not, the contact is useful for other reasons but does not solve this problem.

A community of leaders across the investor's portfolio can give a company **experience it would struggle to assemble alone**. It can also become an event series that exists mainly to show the manager's activity. Judge a peer session by whether it **starts from decisions the participants actually face**. An hour is well spent on a conversation comparing how two companies made customer setup repeatable, what went wrong, and what had to exist first. It is wasted on a session where every **chief technology officer** (CTO), the senior leader responsible for technology, presents the same performance charts, because no one leaves with a decision they can act on.

**Information sharing needs a boundary**. In a small portfolio, even an anonymized case can identify a company. Customer data, employees' pay, and plans for a sale or acquisition don't become fair game because the audience shares an investor. A practice that worked elsewhere should also arrive with its context: the customers, scale, architecture, and leadership that made it work. Without those conditions, a peer's practice can fail when copied. Record them with astice rather than adopting it as their playbook.

## Compare Credible Alternatives for the Same Need

About a week after the board adopted the plan, Priya, accountable for the onboarding pilot, and Alex, who must find the engineering time, compare four routes to the same result: one setup path made repeatable by the end of a six-week assignment.

**Closing** is the day the investment legally completes, and the money changes hands. A few days later, the Rotaline board met and adopted the **operating plan**. In this example, that meeting is day 0, the start of the first hundred days, and every date below counts from it. The plan **committed** €180,000 and 12 engineer-weeks to the onboarding **pilot**, ONB-1, a limited trial run before any wider commitment. The code name lets later reviews refer to it. "Committed" means agreed to spend, not yet spent, so the money and time are reserved but can still be changed if the pilot goes badly. An **engineer-week** is one engineer's working time for one week, so 12 engineer-weeks equal three months of one engineer, or six weeks of two.

Two checkpoints remain. At day 90, Priya measures the **cohort**: the eight pilot customers whose setups are measured together. At day 100, the **board**, the directors who oversee the company and approve its major decisions, reviews those results and decides whether the pilot justifies a wider commitment. The comparison is fictional and uses figures from the shared Rotaline chain. The categories show what to compare; they make no claim about what any investor offers.

| Route | Availability | Company effort | Relevant experience | Verdict |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Peer conversation with a technology leader at another portfolio company who made setup repeatable | One hour, within two weeks | Half a day of preparation | Similar problem, larger customers; context needs checking | Take it first, as a check on the approach |
| Investor’s specialist, introduced by Morgan, the investor’s technology adviser | Ten days over six weeks, from day 21 to about day 63 | Six days of one engineer, plus two customer-team sessions | Said to have done this at two comparable companies; **references** — conversations with people who have seen the specialist’s previous work — not yet checked | Chosen first step, provisional on the start date and those reference checks |
| Independent specialist found by the company | Finding and contracting one takes seven to nine weeks, so a start about day 56 to 70 and an end about day 98 to 112 — five to seven weeks behind the investor’s specialist | The same engineering effort, plus the time to run the selection | Unknown until references are checked | Fallback, not started yet; see below |
| Permanent implementation specialist | Three to four months to start | Onboarding and management | Depends on the candidate | Deferred, pending pilot evidence |

**Chosen.** The investor’s specialist, working for six weeks alongside a Rotaline engineer on a fixed assignment with a defined end, after a peer conversation. The company pays no more than the €15,000 cap, and the six engineer-days are time it already pays for.

**Not chosen, and why.** The independent specialist is slower, because we must first get a quote, confirm availability and select someone. That delay matters at day 100, when the pilot is judged; the three paragraphs headed “If the fallback is needed” work through what it costs. The permanent hire waits because the board deferred hiring until the pilot shows how much effort the setup step actually removes. Until then, the company avoids a commitment of about €150,000 a year per specialist.

**Funding.** Up to €15,000 of the €180,000 committed to ONB-1 in the chapter [Plan the First Hundred Days: Turn Expectations Into Funded Work](#first-hundred-days). Sam, Rotaline's finance leader, confirms the cap fits within what the pilot may already spend, so it needs no draw on the **reserve**. The board's **envelope** is the total money and engineering time it has authorized for the whole plan. The reserve is the part of that envelope held back for problems nobody has yet found. Only the board can release it, so staying within the cap means no board approval is needed.

**Scarce capacity.** Six engineer-days, which Alex takes from the customer-portal research. That work was research-only, so pausing it delays an answer to an open question but breaks no commitment to anyone. The engineer is unavailable for that research for those six days, and the research finishes that much later.

**Who approves what.** Ines, the chief executive, holds the board's delegated authority to approve spending inside the plan it adopted. She has delegated approval of a capped engagement of this size, up to €15,000, to Priya, who is accountable for the pilot's result whether it succeeds or fails. Alex commits the engineer's six days. Because the money comes from ONB-1's existing €180,000 commitment and draws nothing from the reserve, the board does not need to make a new decision.

**Evidence that would change the plan.** Any one of three findings would make us drop the investor's specialist and trigger the independent fallback: the specialist's start slipping past day 21; references showing experience only with much larger customers; or the peer conversation showing that data intake, not the setup step, decided the outcome at another organization.

**If the fallback is needed: what triggers it, and when it would run.** The independent route (hiring a specialist outside the investor's network) is not being run in parallel, and no procurement is under way, because two selections at once would spend the selection time twice. It starts only if one of two triggers occurs: the investor's specialist becomes unavailable, or fails the reference check. If triggered on day 21, a seven-to-nine-week search and contracting would put the start at about day 70 to 84. The six-week assignment would then end at about day 112 to 126, after the day-100 board review. Even a request made on day 7, before any trigger existed, would end only at about day 98 to 112, so the fallback cannot deliver a result by day 100.

**What the day-100 review would show instead.** The engineer could still test parts of a new path earlier, so the problem is not that nothing could be tested by day 100. The problem is what the board agreed to read at the review: the measured effort of a cohort of eight customers set up on the finished path. On the fallback, that measurement would not exist. Priya would arrive at the review with an unfinished assignment, no cohort figure and a revised measurement date instead of a result.

**What the engineer does while waiting.** Between the trigger and the new start, Alex's six engineer-days go back to the customer-portal research they came from, so the fallback's delay costs no other work. The **baseline**, the starting measurement that later results are compared against, is still taken at day 20 either way. It is rebuilt from Rotaline's own records and needs no specialist, so a late start does not delay it.

## Use Connections With Care

An introduction can give a product team access that it would otherwise struggle to get. It can help test a problem, show how a company buys, or find a partner. But it **doesn't prove demand**: one warm contact says little about whether the wider market wants the product, so a team that treats it as proof risks building for the wrong customers.

Investors tend to introduce people from companies they already know, and those** companies may not resemble the customers Rotaline serves**. Larger portfolio companies might describe scheduling needs that don't apply to Rotaline's smaller maintenance businesses. Before changing the roadmap, Priya should check what she learns from them against evidence from the intended market. A commercial introduction also needs a named owner for the relationship and an estimate of the delivery work a sale would create. Without both, more opportunities could worsen the onboarding backlog the company is trying to clear.

An investor's network can help the company find candidates or someone to assess a role. But a prestigious introduction doesn't show that a person fits, so **start with the work the company needs done**. Hiring is also only one way to build a capability. Coaching an existing leader or helping several teams practice a skill can work better than adding a senior role, and it avoids the cost of a hire who doesn't fit.

When the investor helps with recruiting, the company keeps three responsibilities:

- **Assessment.** Candidates, from whatever network, are measured against the company’s own written description of the job and its requirements, and the company takes its own references — the conversations with former colleagues that check how someone actually worked.
- **Disclosed interests.** Anything that could pull the recommendation — a candidate the investor has worked with before, or a search firm the investor pays — is declared and written down, then assessed. A prior relationship is a potential conflict to weigh, not automatically a disqualification.
- **Accountability.** The company appoints the person, manages them and answers for the result. An introduction does not transfer that.

The chapter [Scale the Team Up: Headcount Is Not Capacity](#scale-the-team-up) applies this test to a **chief product officer** proposed by an investor. This senior leader decides what the product should become. The company must assess the candidate against its own job description, take its own references, and record any link between the investor and the candidate. Only then does the company decide, and it stays accountable for the hire.

## Reuse a Practice Only After Checking What Comes With It

Ask what **assumptions come with a practice** you want to reuse. A tool built for a much larger business may need a dedicated administrator your team can't staff, so it sits half-configured and unused. Shared purchasing terms may offer a lower price, but if switching suppliers later would cost more than the discount saves, you are locked in, and the savings never arrive.

Starting a new capability can pair external experts with internal owners who remain accountable after the experts leave. Judge the result by whether it **works in real customer setups** and whether your own team can repeat the work without help. A presentation of what an improved process could look like is a different deliverable, and it leaves the team no better able to do the work.

A company may sensibly keep buying a scarce specialist service, but it should know **what that costs**, how **available** the specialist is, and **who is responsible** for what. Otherwise, it will only discover its dependence when the first assignment ends, leaving no one able to continue the work. The engagement review in the chapter [Set the Terms of Help: Agree the Work, Authority and Handover](#set-terms-of-help) makes that choice explicit.

## Write the Request

The search ends with a short request that people can agree on in one conversation. Here is the one Priya wrote for Rotaline:

> Ask for a specialist to work alongside our engineer for six weeks and help our customer team turn one setup path into a repeatable process, one that works on actual customer setups. Priya will judge success by customer outcomes.

Behind the sentence sit the decisions made above. **Result:** one repeatable setup path, tested on real customer setups. **Source:** the investor's specialist, starting on day 21, with an independent specialist as the fallback. **Company effort:** six engineer-days and two customer-team sessions. **Funding:** up to €15,000 from the money already set aside for the ONB-1 pilot. **Accountable leader:** Priya. The sentence deliberately leaves four things open: which days the specialist works, what information they may see, who directs their work, and how the engagement ends. Until those are settled, the specialist cannot start. The next chapter negotiates them: [Set the Terms of Help: Agree the Work, Authority and Handover](#set-terms-of-help).

## Questions to Consider

1. *What capability does your company lack, described through the work that fails or takes too long, and which of the four requests are you making?*
2. *For that need, what would a peer conversation, an investor’s specialist, an independent provider and a hire each cost in time, money and company effort, and which would you choose first?*
3. *Which practices borrowed from another portfolio company arrived with their context attached, and which did not?*
4. *Where is your company already dependent on a specialist service that no one planned to keep funding?*

## To Probe Further

The first three sources concern **venture capital**: investment in young companies with growth potential, in exchange for a share of the ownership. A company that has taken such investment is **venture-backed**.

- **[Venture Capital and the Professionalization of Start-Up Firms: Empirical Evidence](https://doi.org/10.1111/1540-6261.00419)** — Thomas Hellmann and Manju Puri, The Journal of Finance, 2002.  
  *Evidence from a sample of Silicon Valley start-ups — young companies still establishing themselves — that venture-backed companies adopt formal policies sooner and more often replace the founder in the chief executive role, showing the hiring and leadership support this chapter describes as a pattern with a less comfortable side.*
- **[The Impact of Venture Capital Monitoring](https://doi.org/10.1111/jofi.12370)** — Shai Bernstein, Xavier Giroud and Richard Townsend, The Journal of Finance, 2016.  
  *Uses changes in travel time between venture investors and their companies to separate the effect of investor involvement from the effect of choosing good companies; a population-level estimate for venture-backed firms, not a way to judge whether one engagement like Rotaline’s worked.*
- **[How Smart Is Smart Money? A Two-Sided Matching Model of Venture Capital](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.2007.01291.x)** — Morten Sørensen, The Journal of Finance, 2007.  
  *A model of how venture investors and young companies choose each other. Its measured outcome is narrow and specific: whether a company eventually becomes publicly traded, its shares bought and sold on a stock market. Companies backed by more experienced investors reach that outcome more often, but the paper separates two causes and finds that selecting stronger companies matters almost twice as much as anything the investor does afterwards — a caution against attributing a company’s results to its investor’s network.*
- **[Eleventh Annual Private Equity Leadership Survey](https://www.alixpartners.com/insights/private-equity-leadership-survey-2026/)** — AlixPartners, 2026.  
  *This one is not about venture capital. It surveys **private equity**: buying ownership in businesses that are not listed on a stock market. The businesses involved are usually established and often substantial, not young companies. A consulting firm asked 174 leaders at investment firms and 253 senior executives of the companies those firms own, and reports the two groups’ views side by side — including how the support investors say they provide compares with what company leaders say they want. Survey responses about perceptions, not measurement of what the support achieved.*
