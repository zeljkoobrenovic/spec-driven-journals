# 17. Work With Technology Operating Partners: Build a Partnership That Serves Your Plan

![Work With Technology Operating Partners: Build a Partnership That Serves Your Plan — logo](private-techuity/posts/14-technology-operating-partners/assets/images/14-technology-operating-partners/logo.png)

> **IN THIS SECTION, YOU WILL:** Learn what a technology operating partner is, why investment firms rely on them, how the role differs from your own, and how to build a working partnership with the partner on your plan, your hiring and your use of AI.

> **WHY INVESTORS CARE:** Investors now earn more of their expected gain from software, data and delivery: growing the product, merging acquired businesses onto one platform, and judging whether AI helps or threatens the plan. If those assumptions are wrong, the investor loses money, so someone with technology judgment must test them before the investment and track them afterward. Many investment firms use technology operating partners to provide that judgment across the companies they own.

> **WHY YOU SHOULD CARE:** A technology operating partner, the investor's in-house technology adviser to the companies it owns, can bring you experience, contacts and a second opinion your team lacks. Handled badly, the same role adds requests and expectations nobody has agreed on. If you understand what the partner is for and which decisions stay yours, you can work with them as a partner rather than manage them as an inspector.

> **KEY POINTS:**
>
> * **The partner exists because the investor's plan contains technical assumptions.** Someone qualified must test them before the investment and track them afterward. Knowing that purpose tells you what the partner will ask about and where their help is strongest.
> * **The partnership can cover the whole investment, but each piece of help is an agreement.** For each assignment, agree in writing who decides, how much of the partner's time you get, who pays and what evidence will show it worked.
> * **The partner advises, assesses and supports; you and your fellow executives keep the continuing responsibility.** Keep those roles distinct and the partnership gives you experience and access without taking your decisions. Judge it by better decisions and results your company keeps, not by activity.

> **[DYSFUNCTIONS THIS SECTION ADDRESSES](#where-investment-goes-wrong):**
>
> * **The Dusty Address Book** — Your company never uses the operating partner's network. This section shows where the partner can introduce you to peers, specialists and people with hiring experience.
> * **The Accidental Gatekeeper** — Portfolio support turns into a layer of approval over your leadership. This section separates the two and sets out who decides what for each assignment.
> * **All Bulk, No Muscle** — Your company adds headcount and AI projects that look busy but build no real capability. This section tests each hiring and AI proposal against the work and skills your company needs.

Imagine that your investor's plan assumes your company can serve twice as many customers, absorb an acquired business, and launch a product built on **artificial intelligence (AI)**, software that can, for example, draft text or find patterns in data. Each ambition feeds the **financial forecast**, the investor's estimate of future sales, costs and profit. If no one checks whether your systems and staff can handle that growth, the forecast rests on guesses. Someone must work out what your systems and people can actually support, what needs funding, and what to change when the evidence contradicts the forecast.

Often that someone works for the investor. The **technology operating partner**, sometimes called a **technical operating partner**, is a senior practitioner who works with an investment firm and its **portfolio companies**, the businesses it has invested in. In practice, the partner checks whether your systems and people can support the plan, and flags early when they can't. EE Solutions describes this link between assessing and delivering as central to the role. [S80: EE Solutions, technology operating partner](https://www.eesolutions.io/insights/technology-operating-partner-private-capital/) A firm may employ one partner, build a specialist team or bring in outside support.

Two documents shape the partner's work. An **investment thesis** is the investor's written case for why an investment should earn a return, such as "this company can double its customers." A **value creation plan** turns that case into specific improvements with owners and deadlines, such as upgrading systems or launching an AI product. When those improvements depend on your technology and your teams, you will be working with the operating partner, who tests whether the plan is realistic and helps you carry it out.

The preceding chapter, [Clarify the Adviser’s Role: Are They Helping, Assessing or Deciding?](#clarify-adviser-role), explains how to work with one adviser, such as Morgan, the role this book calls the Technology Principal. This chapter steps back to the function that supplies such advisers, so you understand what the partner's own firm expects of them, how the role differs from yours, and how the two of you can work together on your plan, your hiring and your AI decisions. The practical agreements for any piece of help are in [Choose the Right Help: Compare Investor Support With Other Options](#choose-right-help) and [Set the Terms of Help: Agree the Work, Authority and Handover](#set-terms-of-help).

## Setting Up the Example: One AI Tool, Three Companies

One fictional case runs through the chapter. An investment firm wants three of its software companies to adopt the same **AI support tool**, which drafts replies to customer questions from past support records for staff to review before sending. Put yourself in the seat of the technology leader at each company in turn. Each company's readiness, not the tool, decides what help you would need from the partner:

- **The first** has reliable support records, so it can compare the tool's drafts with past answers and measure whether the tool actually helps.
- **The second** has the records but cannot yet show that its customers' permissions allow them to be used this way. Being able to open a record is not the same as being allowed to feed it into the tool.
- **The third** receives most of its complaints because its core service keeps failing. Faster replies would not fix the cause.

## Why the Function Is Becoming More Important

These pressures are what your partner answers for at their own firm, so they are what the partner will ask you about. Korn Ferry describes a progression in some firms from **external advisers** to **internal technology leaders** to **teams with specialist capabilities**; Redgrave's June 2024 article describes a role spanning investment assessment, company strategy, operational improvement and technology leadership. Both are executive search firms that profit from demand for such roles, so their accounts show how the role is described, not how widespread it is. [S79: Korn Ferry, Tech Titans in Private Equity](https://www.kornferry.com/institute/tech-titans-in-private-equity) [S81: Redgrave, technology operating partners](https://redgravesearch.com/insights/technology-operating-partner-in-private-equity/)

**The investment case contains technical assumptions.** A revenue or cost forecast often rests on a technical change: onboarding customers faster, launching a new product or cutting delivery costs. The plan is then only as reliable as the engineering behind it. The partner's first job is to test whether the change is feasible, and you are the main source of evidence for that test. See [Assess Capability: Can the Team Deliver?](#assess-capability) and [Test Revenue Assumptions: Do Customers Respond as Expected?](#test-revenue-assumptions).

**Diligence has a short shelf life.** The people, products and dependencies examined before an investment keep changing. Vaultinum argues for revisiting technology findings during ownership; its monitoring service is one commercial response. The useful principle for you is that each diligence finding about your company should be recorded with its business consequence and the evidence needed to close or revise it. Ask the partner for that record and help keep it current, or the investor may act on a risk you have already fixed, or miss one that has grown. [S84: Vaultinum, operating partners during ownership](https://vaultinum.com/blog/operating-partners-tech-due-diligence)

**Acquisitions make technical decisions interdependent.** A partner working across both businesses can find dependencies that neither company's plan shows, such as a plan to retire a system the other company's service relies on, so that each choice to integrate, share or leave a product independent becomes an explicit decision with its cost visible before anyone commits. [Plan Acquisitions and Separations: Account for Extra Work, Not Just Expected Value](#plan-acquisitions-separations) examines the work behind that choice.

**Repeated problems create a reason to share learning.** EE Solutions lists shared methods, expertise and supplier knowledge as part of the portfolio function. My inference is that the benefit depends on how often the problem repeats: if another portfolio company has faced your supplier question, the partner can bring you its findings, and a sound evaluation method can be reused even where the tools differ. [S80: EE Solutions](https://www.eesolutions.io/insights/technology-operating-partner-private-capital/)

**AI puts more assumptions up for review.** You and the partner both have to decide which work to change, which AI claims to test and which capabilities to keep in-house, before targets become commitments. Not every proposed AI investment is worth making.

## What Investment Firms Say Their Technology Operating Teams Do

KKR Capstone is KKR's **in-house operating team**. It says it helps assess investments, design and carry out improvement plans, and run programs shared across companies. Its technology remit covers AI, data, cybersecurity (protecting systems and information), infrastructure (the computing and network foundation) and new products. [S85: KKR Capstone](https://www.kkr.com/approach/capstone)

In a May 2026 interview, Blackstone's chief technology officer and the global head of its operating team describe a dedicated team that helps portfolio companies adopt AI, sorting companies by how central AI is to their business. The leaders stress that **executives are accountable** for AI results, that projects must show a **measurable economic return**, and that the work must reach **actual implementation**. Again, the AI capability works inside a wider operating team. [S86: Blackstone, AI at Scale](https://www.blackstone.com/insights/article/ai-at-scale-a-conversation-with-blackstones-cto-and-global-head-of-the-operating-team/)

Both descriptions come from the firms themselves and show what the function can look like, not measured results. A smaller firm may offer you one coordinator who can call on specialists. Find out early which arrangement your investor runs, because it decides whom you will be dealing with and how much of their time you can expect.

## What the Partner Does: From Acquisition to Exit

The partner's recurring task is to turn an **investor's assumption** about your company into work your company **can realistically do**, then show whether that work produced the **expected result**. The table below reads this across the life of an investment. It combines the lifecycle coverage described by EE Solutions and Vertex CIO with the continuing review discussed by Vaultinum; the outputs and the limits on the partner's role inside your company are the book's recommendations. [S80: EE Solutions](https://www.eesolutions.io/insights/technology-operating-partner-private-capital/) [S82: Vertex CIO Advisory](https://vertexcio.com/pe-technology-operating-partner/) [S84: Vaultinum](https://vaultinum.com/blog/operating-partners-tech-due-diligence)

| Work | What the partner can contribute | What you should be able to see |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Before investment | Tests the important assumptions about the product, its systems, security, cost and the team; brings specialists into uncertain areas. | Findings linked to the proposed plan, with the work needed to fix problems and the consequences for timing or funding. |
| Early ownership | Helps translate findings into a feasible sequence of work. | An agreed starting measurement (baseline), funded priorities, named company leaders and a review date. |
| Product and engineering improvement | Helps remove constraints on delivery, customer adoption, reliability or operating cost. | A specific change to the work and a measure tied to its intended customer or business result. |
| Leadership, hiring and organization | Helps define roles, assess candidates, support appointments and settle new leaders in; supports development and succession. | A funded need, clear selection criteria and appointment authority; an explicit distinction between advising, assessing and managing a leader. |
| Acquisitions and separations | Examines systems, data, contracts and service dependencies when a business is bought or split off. | A transition plan with its costs, continuing service obligations and an accountable company executive. |
| Learning across companies | Organizes peer learning, reusable methods and access to specialists or suppliers; [Learn Through Your Investor’s Network: Knowledge, Peers and New Perspectives](#learn-through-investors-network) develops the formats and follow-through. | Relevant access, company time and scope to explore, with assumptions and later outcomes kept distinct. |
| Preparation for a sale | Helps assemble a defensible account of the technology and remaining work. | Current evidence, known limitations and obligations the next owner must understand. |

Make sure the partner's written findings, plans and owners stay with your company if the partner leaves: a concern raised only in a presentation is carried forward by nobody. What matters is that a **successor can pick up** the record, the budget and the named owner.

Three groups hold different roles. The **investment team** decides whether to invest and on what terms. **Your company's executives**, including you, run the business within their own authority. The **operating function** provides assessment and support, and takes on oversight or delivery only when that duty is explicitly assigned. One partner may hold more than one role, but everyone must know which role applies to each decision, or your team may take a recommendation as a decision. In the support-tool case, the investment team proposed the rollout, each company's executives decide whether and when to adopt the tool, and the partner assesses the three situations and supports those decisions without making them.

## How Firms Organize the Function, and What It Means for You

You do not choose how your investor organizes its operating function, but the choice shapes what to settle at the start. The options below are **design choices**, not stages of growth.

An **internal operating partner** keeps context because one person sees both the investment discussions and the company reviews. The limit is **personal capacity**: time spent on a new transaction is time taken from a portfolio company in crisis. Agree when the partner will be available, and find out who covers when they are not.

A **team of specialists** supplies deeper skills, but if each specialist sets tasks independently, your engineers receive competing assignments. Ask for one named person who owns the combined priority list, and make that person your main contact.

An **external operating capability** is an outside firm that supplies technology expertise on contract. Vertex CIO describes such a model and distinguishes it from a **fractional executive**, a part-time executive holding a defined role inside one company. This shows the arrangement exists, not that outsourcing is better. If your investor uses an outside firm, ask who exactly will do the work, how knowledge carries over between assignments, and how you can challenge recommendations. [S82: Vertex CIO Advisory](https://vertexcio.com/pe-technology-operating-partner/)

A **hybrid** model keeps the relationship and the priorities inside the investment firm and brings in external specialists **only for particular problems**. The test from your side is whether a named person at the firm remains accountable until the recommendations are implemented.

A title such as "CTO" or "technology operating partner" says little about whether the person has delivered the specific change your company needs, such as a cloud migration or a post-acquisition systems merger. When the firm proposes a partner for an assignment, ask what comparable change they have delivered, and where. [S81: Redgrave](https://redgravesearch.com/insights/technology-operating-partner-in-private-equity/)

Korn Ferry also notes that firms **allocate the cost of operating support** differently. Find out who pays for the partner's time in your case: the investment firm, the fund (and so its investors' pooled money), or your company from its own budget. Then add the company time and spending needed to act on the advice, without which it stays on paper. [S79: Korn Ferry](https://www.kornferry.com/institute/tech-titans-in-private-equity)

## How the Partner's Role Differs From Yours

Companies usually spread technology and product leadership across a **chief information officer (CIO)**, a **chief technology officer (CTO)**, a **chief product officer (CPO)**, a **chief architect** and a **vice president (VP) or head of engineering**. You probably hold one of these roles. The main distinction between any of them and the partner is **who stays accountable over time**. You own a function day to day and keep that ownership through changes of management and of company ownership. The partner works for the investment firm, often across several companies, and steps in only for specific assessments and interventions. When the engagement ends, the partner leaves, and you still own the results. Both roles need commercial judgment, technical understanding and the ability to influence others, which is why the partnership works when each respects the other's ground.

IBM’s 2021 CIO study, GitLab’s CPO description and the UK government’s digital standard each describe one workable split of these duties, and none sets a universal hierarchy, so your company's titles and reporting lines may differ without being wrong. [S88: IBM, The CIO Revolution](https://www.ibm.com/thought-leadership/institute-business-value/en-us/report/cio) [S87: GitLab, Chief Product Officer](https://handbook.gitlab.com/job-description-library/product/chief-product-officer/) [S89: GovS 005, section 4.5.6](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/government-functional-standard-govs-005-digital/government-functional-standard-govs-005-digital-html#governance)

Use the comparison below as a starting point; its success measures are examples, not a scorecard. The last column shows where the partnership is most useful for each role.

| Role and usual scope | Continuing accountability and evidence of success | Authority to establish | How the role works with the partner |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Technology operating partner:** investment firm and selected portfolio assignments | Quality of technical investment judgments; improvements and capabilities left with companies. | Rights to recommend, assess, approve or direct must be specified for each assignment. | Coordinates investor expectations, expertise and company needs; helps remove obstacles that span several assignments. |
| **CIO — Chief Information Officer:** internal business systems, such as finance, payroll and customer records | Business processes that work, dependable services, access to data and sustainable running costs. | Delegated authority over the information technology (IT) team, service priorities, suppliers and budget. | Has the partner test plans to replace or connect business systems; keeps responsibility for running them afterward. |
| **CTO — Chief Technology Officer:** technology strategy and, in many software businesses, engineering and the shared technical base | Technical capability to deliver the business plan; quality, reliability, the ability to keep changing the software. | Delegated technology and engineering decisions; staffing and spending within the agreed remit. | Invites challenge to technical assumptions, draws on the partner's peers and specialists, and agrees where investor support helps. |
| **CPO — Chief Product Officer:** product direction, customer problems and product investment choices | Products customers adopt and continue to value, with credible sales and retention results. | Choices about which product improvements to deliver first, within strategy and funding limits. | Works through the product assumptions in the investment thesis with the partner and uses the partner to secure resources for justified opportunities. |
| **Chief Architect:** how the company’s systems fit together, their data and major technical choices | Designs that meet business constraints and can still be changed; known dependencies and manageable technical risk. | Advisory or design-approval authority as assigned; the title alone gives no budget or staff. | Examines design options and dependencies with the partner, especially in an acquisition; the company’s own design review governs implementation. |
| **VP (Vice President) or Head of Engineering:** the engineering teams and how they deliver | A capable team building and running software sustainably, with credible estimates and learning from failures. | Delegated team, staffing and delivery decisions, often within the CTO’s remit. | Makes delivery constraints visible to the partner and turns agreed support into work the teams can absorb. |

Titles do not reliably show responsibility: a CIO may lead customer-facing innovation, one person may hold both the CPO and CTO roles, and a fund's own CTO runs the investment firm's internal systems. Before you and the partner divide work, confirm what each person actually owns, decides and answers for.

In an acquisition, each executive assesses one part of the combination: the CPO which **customer needs** the combined products should serve, the CTO the required **engineering work**, the CIO the move to **shared business systems**, the Chief Architect the dependencies and **design options**. The partner helps the investor understand the resulting cost, timing and feasibility. The **chief executive officer (CEO)** and the **board**, the group that oversees the company on behalf of its owners, decide anything beyond these delegated powers. Agree this division with the partner for each deal rather than inferring it from the organization chart.

If the partner steps in as **interim CTO**, whether in your place or above you, insist that four things are recorded in writing: the **appointment**, the **reporting** line, the **delegated budget** and the **end condition** for the role. Without this record, an advisory role can quietly become a second management hierarchy.

## Working With the Partner on Hiring and Leadership

A technology operating partner can help you **hire and develop the senior leaders** and specialists your plan requires: **defining roles**, finding and assessing **candidates**, developing **existing staff** and shaping the **team structure**. Redgrave and KKR Capstone both list talent work within the role; the division below is the book's own proposal, so who does what in a given hire is something you agree case by case. [S81: Redgrave](https://redgravesearch.com/insights/technology-operating-partner-in-private-equity/) [S85: KKR Capstone](https://www.kkr.com/approach/capstone)

**Define the need together.** Before anyone recommends a new executive or a replacement, work with the partner and your CEO to pin down what is missing: technical direction, product judgment, execution capacity, specialist knowledge or clearer decisions. The partner brings a view from outside; you bring the operating context. Consider the alternatives together: internal promotion, coaching, a narrower hire or temporary support. For a permanent role, agree the outcomes, reporting line, authority, team and budget before the search starts. In the support-tool case, one proposal is to hire an AI leader in each of the three companies. The first may need help running a **pilot**, a small, time-limited trial before wider use; the second, advice on data permissions; the third, reliability engineering, not AI expertise. Hiring an AI leader for all three before diagnosing these gaps risks paying for a senior role that solves none of them. [Scale the Team Up: Headcount Is Not Capacity](#scale-the-team-up) works through that choice.

**Use the partner's network to reach candidates.** The partner can introduce leaders from other portfolio companies, identify relevant **executive-search firms** (agencies that recruit senior leaders), and help you explain the opportunity. Agree how the partner coordinates with your hiring manager, your human resources (HR) team and any investor talent team, so that candidates hear one message. An introduction from the investor does not replace your own assessment of fit.

**Draw on the partner's judgment in selection.** Define job-related criteria with the partner, and ask them to join interviews where their specialist judgment helps. For a CTO, examine decisions about delivery, reliability and technical change alongside people leadership. For a CPO, examine customer judgment and product investment choices. For an AI leader, examine experience in checking AI output, preparing data and running a live AI service. Ask the partner to declare any prior relationship with a candidate or any recruitment fee that could affect their recommendation, and assess an investor-introduced candidate against the same criteria as the rest of the shortlist.

**Settle appointment authority before the search.** Write down who can approve the role and budget, choose the candidate, agree pay and make the appointment. These decisions follow your company's delegations, its written record of who may decide what, and anything the investment agreements reserve for the board or the investor. The partner may recommend, take part or hold an explicitly assigned approval role; closeness to the investor does not settle which, so ask. [Clarify Authority: Decide Who Decides Before You Disagree](#clarify-authority) and [Clarify the Adviser’s Role: Are They Helping, Assessing or Deciding?](#clarify-adviser-role) provide the underlying distinctions.

**Help the new leader succeed, with clear roles.** Agree the first outcomes and a review date with the new leader and their manager. The partner can explain the investment assumptions, introduce peers and arrange specialist support. Keep coaching, performance assessment and formal management separate, and say who holds each, or the new leader will not know whether the partner is a coach or a judge. Judge the partner's contribution by the capability your company gains and the outcomes agreed for the role, then by retention and time to fill the vacancy.

## How AI Changes the Partnership

{id: technology-operating-partners--testing-the-investment-thesis-together}
### Testing the Investment Thesis Together

Expect the partner to ask how AI could change your business. What will **customers pay** for once AI is available? Which capabilities can **competitors now reproduce quickly**? Does your company **hold useful data** it can legitimately use? Blackstone's May 2026 account raises these questions in software diligence; it shows a stated approach, not proof of a durable advantage. [S86: Blackstone](https://www.blackstone.com/insights/article/ai-at-scale-a-conversation-with-blackstones-cto-and-global-head-of-the-operating-team/)

Your side of the exchange is the evidence behind the forecast. If the plan depends on an AI feature commanding a higher price, the CPO needs evidence that customers will pay for it, and the CTO and specialists need evidence that the feature can reach acceptable quality at acceptable cost. Bring these questions to the partner before the targets are fixed, because a plan built on an untested assumption is a plan you will be held to anyway.

{id: technology-operating-partners--working-together-on-ai-adoption}
### Working Together on AI Adoption

The partner can help you choose a **use case** (a specific, limited task for AI, such as drafting support replies), find **expertise** and compare what worked in another company. The more valuable help is often with the work a demonstration leaves unfinished: **preparing data**, connecting the tool to **existing systems**, testing its results and running it day to day.

Agree the split locally. One proposal: the **CPO** ties a customer-facing use case to the product and its commercial purpose; the **CIO or relevant business leader** owns changes to internal ways of working; the **CTO and engineering leaders** own delivery and operation; the **Chief Architect** checks how the tool connects to existing systems and whether the company could switch suppliers later; data, security and legal specialists answer within their remits; the partner supplies challenge, coordination and scarce expertise. Name one executive accountable for the result.

Evaluate the whole workflow with the partner, not just the tool: useful results, errors and their correction, human review, adoption, running costs and the work displaced. Faster code generation does not by itself mean faster delivery to customers, and before anyone changes the staffing plan, show which work has actually disappeared and which new duties remain. See [Clarify AI Strategy: Three Different Investment Questions](#clarify-ai-strategy) and [Scale the Team Up: Headcount Is Not Capacity](#scale-the-team-up).

{id: technology-operating-partners--how-the-partner-may-use-ai-on-your-company-s-information}
### How the Partner May Use AI on Your Company's Information

Partners increasingly use AI in their own work: comparing **diligence documents**, flagging contradictory claims, **drafting questions** for management and preparing **portfolio reports**. You will see the results as questions and summaries about your company, so agree three things. Conclusions that affect a decision about your company should be checked by a qualified person and traceable to the source documents, because a generated summary only restates what the sources say. Your confidential information should be kept separate from other portfolio companies' in any shared AI tool. And a claimed productivity gain is a hypothesis: if reviewers must re-verify every summary, the savings shrink, so the whole task should be timed, from first draft through final review, before anyone claims a gain.

{id: technology-operating-partners--when-you-get-an-ai-specialist-as-well}
### When You Get an AI Specialist as Well

Acertitude describes two models: separate **AI operating partners**, and roles that combine AI with the wider technology remit. It lists the specialist demands as product strategy, data, building or adapting AI models (the trained software that produces predictions or text), oversight of their use and team capability. This shows how one recruiter frames the assignment, not that every portfolio needs another executive. [S83: Acertitude, AI and technology operating partners](https://www.acertitude.com/insights/choosing-between-ai-and-tech-operating-partners-what-pe-leaders-need-to-know)

A dedicated AI partner makes sense for a firm whose portfolio has **repeated, material AI projects** needing skills its team lacks; otherwise an external specialist or a technology operating partner with AI experience can cover the need. What you need to know is who covers AI questions for your company, for how long and at whose cost.

If two specialists are assigned to your company, ask for one **shared business outcome**, such as a working tool that customers use, and a written split of responsibilities: the AI specialist judges the quality of the tool's output and its testing, the technology lead coordinates integration and keeps the tool operational, and you approve the work within your mandate. One named person in your company must own the adoption and customer results, or each specialist can finish their part while no one is accountable for whether the tool is used.

## What a Useful Partnership Looks Like

Return to the three companies. A useful partner helps the first design its pilot with an agreed budget and review date. With the second, the partner helps confirm which records the company may legally and contractually use before anyone commits to implementation. With the third, the partner supports the leadership's decision to prioritize service reliability and defer the rollout, and explains that decision to the investment team. Each company approves resources through its existing decision routes and records which planned work its choice delays.

The partner can still **share evaluation methods** across all three companies. The partnership's contribution is a better sequence of decisions, not that every company buys the same tool.

![Shared AI methods inform three fictional company decisions: pilot with ready data, confirm permitted use of records whose permissions are unclear, or fix an unstable service first.](private-techuity/posts/14-technology-operating-partners/assets/images/14-technology-operating-partners/shared-ai-expertise-different-decisions.png)

**Figure 3:** *In the fictional example, reusable expertise supports different decisions: pilot, confirm permitted data use or restore reliability. Company conditions determine the sequence.*

At review, the first company's leaders and the partner examine four measures together: resolution quality (whether customers' problems were actually solved), customer waiting time, human review effort and total running cost. The hours the tool frees are a **capacity benefit**, time available for other work, until the company shows how it was used. They become a **cash saving** only when spending falls below an agreed baseline, such as support spending before the pilot, after counting the tool's setup and running costs. Staff answering more questions on unchanged pay is capacity, not cash. Even a successful pilot cannot show how much of the result came from the partner and how much from your own team, so agree in advance not to claim more than the evidence supports. [Test Revenue Assumptions: Do Customers Respond as Expected?](#test-revenue-assumptions) develops these distinctions.

## What to Agree With the Partner

Before any assignment starts, write a short summary of it with the partner: the decision to be made or the constraint to address, the people available, the cost, who has authority to act, and the date of the next review. If any point is missing, the two of you have not yet agreed what the assignment is. The charter in [Set the Terms of Help: Agree the Work, Authority and Handover](#set-terms-of-help) supplies the full detail.

Three further agreements protect the relationship. **Keep advice and assessment separate, or name the overlap.** A partner who coaches you may also shape the investor's view of your leadership, so agree which conversations are private coaching and which feed the investor's assessment. **Ask about supplier interests.** If a recommendation earns the partner or their firm implementation revenue or a referral fee, you need to know, so the benefit can be weighed against the alternatives. **Ask for a business case for your company in any shared program.** A discount negotiated for all the investor's companies can be outweighed by your company's migration, disruption or continuing service costs.

The partnership earns its place when it helps you make a better decision, delivers an improvement your company has funded, or builds a capability your company can sustain on its own. Counts of assessments or technologies deployed show only activity; what you need to know is what changed, and whether it justified its cost.

Part IV turns investor expectations into work the company can deliver, starting with the outcomes the work should move: [COMMIT: Turn Expectations Into Work You Can Deliver](#part-4) and [Adopt Outcome Thinking: Balance Customer and Business KPIs](#adopt-outcome-thinking).

## Questions to Consider

1. *Which assumption in the investor’s plan most needs technical judgment, and who follows it after diligence, you or the partner?*
2. *What expertise can the partner actually supply to your company, for how long and at whose cost?*
3. *Which decisions can the partner make, and how would your team recognize a change from advice to assessment or instruction?*
4. *At the next review, what company result or better-supported decision would justify the partnership?*
5. *For your next leadership hire, who defines the role, who assesses candidates, who approves the appointment, and what will its first review examine?*
