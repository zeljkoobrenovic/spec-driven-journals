# 6. Match the Funding to the Work

![Match the Funding to the Work — logo](private-techuity/posts/03-raise-what-you-need/assets/images/03-raise-what-you-need/logo.jpeg)

> **IN THIS SECTION, YOU WILL:** Learn to size the work the company must do next, compare the funding arrangements that could support it, and act on an arrangement you inherited.

> **WHY INVESTORS CARE:** A raise that is too small can force a rescue round on worse terms; one that is too large can dilute returns and fund work that was never justified. Sizing trades off the ownership given up, the reserve kept and the risk of having to raise again, and investors weigh that trade-off against the plan for the same reasons the company should.

> **WHY YOU SHOULD CARE:** Funding that does not match the work either starves a plan or buys it with rights, obligations and expectations the company cannot afford; the mismatch lands on the people who have to deliver.

> **KEY POINTS:**
>
> * Begin with the **problem the money must solve, and size it**: base work, transition costs, an allowance for uncertainty and the runway to the next decision. Funding expansion, paying a retiring founder and separating a business from a parent are different needs.
> * Examine the **terms behind the investor’s label**. The terms of the funding and the investor’s expectations affect the pace, the tolerated losses and the time available for the plan; the category on its own does not.
> * Product and engineering leaders who **inherit the arrangement** can still act: bring a sized scope, a cash requirement and an alternative to the people who can renegotiate it. Changing the financing, changing the plan or continuing without a new owner can each be the right answer.

A business has customers and a useful product. Its founder might want money to expand it, or might want to retire and sell their shares. Those are different needs, even if both conversations begin with “we need an investor.”

The preceding chapters explained funding, ownership, valuation and returns. This chapter uses them to answer a narrower question: how much money does the next piece of work actually need, and which arrangement can supply it without attaching conditions the work can’t meet?

Product and engineering leaders may not choose the investor, but they inherit the consequences of the choice. The terms of the funding and the investor’s expectations affect the pace at which results are expected, how much loss the shareholders will tolerate and how long the plan has before it is judged. Those terms come from the agreement, not from the category on the investor’s website: two growth investors can offer different rights and different patience. A migration or a product bet that fits one set of terms can be unaffordable or unwanted under another. Knowing the actual terms tells you which kind of plan you’re being asked to write.

There is no required journey from one investor type to the next. The arrangements below are alternatives, including staying under the existing ownership.

## Separate the Owner, the Financing and the Situation

**Investor type** describes who supplies ownership capital and what they seek. **Control** concerns the rights to decide. **Financing** concerns the money and its obligations. **Transaction context** describes an event such as a funding round, acquisition or separation. These dimensions interact, but none replaces the others. A founder might need personal cash from selling shares while the company needs no new funding; a large transaction can leave the company with no new money at all.

[Customers, Lenders and Investors: What Each Expects in Return](#customers-lenders-investors) and the [Introduction & Reading Guide](#introduction) introduce the arrangements. The recap below keeps only the question each one raises about the work; the operating consequences are conditional judgments, not evidence that all investors in a category behave alike.

| Arrangement | The question to test against the work |
| --- | --- |
| Venture funding | What evidence can arrive before current cash runs short, and what happens if another round does not close? |
| Growth funding | Which part of growth is already repeatable, and what must be funded to repeat it elsewhere? |
| Buyout ownership | How much cash reaches the company, what rights change and what financing constrains reinvestment? |
| Corporate minority investment | Which product and information requests serve the wider customer base, and which chiefly serve the investor? |
| Corporate acquisition | What stays local, what must integrate and which group budget funds the dependencies? |
| Continued founder or family ownership | Which growth pace and concentration of risk are acceptable to the current shareholders? |

A **carve-out** separates a business from a parent; a **turnaround** addresses serious operating or financial problems; an **acquisition** purchases a company or assets and may use cash, borrowing or shares. None of the three identifies the investor. Label them separately so an integration deadline or a cash crisis isn’t mistaken for a feature of the owner.

## Size the Work Before You Name the Investor

Take Larkspur, the fictional scheduling-software company used throughout this book. In this scenario customers already buy the product, but onboarding depends on manual configuration by the implementation team and the queue is growing. Ines, the CEO, and Sam, the CFO, are weighing outside money. Alex, the CTO, and Priya, the product leader, are asked what the fix costs. Every figure below is fictional and belongs to this scenario alone.

The first mistake would be to size the raise from the offer on the table. The second would be to size it from the build alone. The reusable setup step from [The Chain From Roadmap to Revenue Breaks Easily](#roadmap-to-revenue) costs about €180,000 to build; a request for €180,000 would fund the software and nothing that makes it work.

| Component of the need | Cash | Basis |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Base work: build the reusable setup step | €180,000 | 12 engineer-weeks, as in [You Cannot Fund Every Good Project at Once](#cannot-fund-everything) |
| First year of maintenance | €30,000 | |
| Transition: run the manual and new process in parallel for two quarters, migrate existing configurations, train the implementation team | €90,000 | Estimated from the current 80-hour implementations |
| Interim capacity: one contract implementation specialist for twelve months while the step is built and proved | €150,000 | Without it the queue grows during the build |
| Uncertainty allowance: 25% of build and transition | €70,000 | 25% of €270,000 is €67,500, rounded up to €70,000; effort may fall to 62 hours rather than the 50 assumed |
| Decision runway: six further months of interim capacity and maintenance if the cohort evidence arrives late | €90,000 | Keeps the choice open without a raise under pressure |
| **Cash need** | **€610,000** | About €160,000 is allowance and runway, not base work |

The scarce capacity is the twelve engineer-weeks and the one implementation specialist who understands the current configurations; both are needed in the same two quarters, whichever arrangement funds them.

### Two Arrangements That Could Fund It

Sam has two feasible arrangements. Both cover the €610,000; they differ in what else they bring. One assumption runs through both: Larkspur generates about €350,000 of operating cash a year after tax and existing obligations, stated before any interest on new borrowing and before the minimum cash balance the board requires the company to hold.

**Arrangement A: €2.5m of growth equity.** A growth investor offers €2.5m for a minority stake, with approval rights over the annual budget and senior hires and a stated expectation that Larkspur enters a second country within eighteen months. It funds the onboarding work about four times over. The surplus is a reserve against the pilot disappointing, and it makes a later round less likely to be needed soon; it does not remove the risk, because a raise would still be needed if the expansion consumed the surplus before the pilot had paid for itself. It also sets a pace: the second country needs a working setup step first, or it exports the manual bottleneck, and the surplus becomes a sales-hiring budget before the pilot has produced any evidence.

**Arrangement B: a €750,000 term loan and staged work.** The company’s bank offers a four-year term loan at 8% with a one-year principal holiday. Interest is charged on the balance at the start of each year and paid at year end together with the principal; principal is repaid at €250,000 a year from the second year, so the loan closes at zero at the end of year four. The current shareholders keep control, and the €140,000 of headroom above the need is the reserve. The repayments are fixed whatever the pilot shows, and if the company later needs equity after a disappointing pilot it would raise on worse terms than today.

| Year | Opening balance | Interest at 8% | Principal | Payment at year end | Closing balance | Margin over €350,000 of operating cash |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 1 | €750,000 | €60,000 | €0 | €60,000 | €750,000 | €290,000 |
| 2 | €750,000 | €60,000 | €250,000 | €310,000 | €500,000 | €40,000 |
| 3 | €500,000 | €40,000 | €250,000 | €290,000 | €250,000 | €60,000 |
| 4 | €250,000 | €20,000 | €250,000 | €270,000 | €0 | €80,000 |

Total interest over the term is €180,000, and the four payments add up to €930,000. The last column tests each payment against operating cash alone; the stated assumptions below say why nothing else is counted.

**Why the requirement cannot simply be carried from operating cash.** €350,000 a year sounds close to €610,000 spread over eighteen months, but the requirement is front-loaded. The build, the transition and the first two quarters of the contract specialist fall in the same two quarters and add up to about €345,000 (€180,000 + €90,000 + €75,000): almost a full year of operating cash, which arrives over twelve months, and which is measured before the €60,000 of year-one interest once a loan exists and before the minimum cash balance the board requires. Paying for the work as the cash came in would empty that balance in the first two quarters and leave the allowance and the runway unfunded, which is the raise-under-pressure the runway exists to prevent.

**Stated assumptions.** The setup constraint is supported by customer evidence. Demand in a second country is not yet evidenced. The financing comparison is this chapter’s own scenario; what it imports from the shared Larkspur chain is the pilot: its cost basis of €180,000 to build and €30,000 a year to maintain, and its result. The 62-hour case used here as the weaker outcome is the figure Priya’s day-90 cohort in [The Chain From Roadmap to Revenue Breaks Easily](#roadmap-to-revenue) later measures. At 62 hours the model there projects about €135,000 of annual implementation capacity released; at 50 hours, about €225,000. Released staff time is a capacity benefit. Finance excludes it from repayment cash until cancelled contractor spending or additional collected contribution is evidenced.

**The choice.** Under these assumptions Ines and Sam recommend B to the board. The work is bounded, the evidence arrives within the first year, and every payment in the schedule is covered by the €350,000 of operating cash alone: by €290,000 in year one, €40,000 in year two, €60,000 in year three and €80,000 in year four. The €135,000 is left out of that test on purpose. On the day the cohort is measured no contract has been cancelled and no additional contribution has been collected, so released time is not cash on any repayment date. Year two is the year to watch, and Sam’s downside forecast is dated to it: if operating cash fell to €300,000 from year two, a 15% fall from slower collections and lost renewals if the queue has not moved, the €310,000 payment would be €10,000 short, and years three and four would clear by only €10,000 and €30,000. Three mechanisms with dates stand behind that margin, none of them the capacity figure. If the queue is moving by month twelve, the contract specialist is not extended, so up to €150,000 a year of the requirement is never spent, the first €75,000 of it inside the decision runway. The two-specialist hire stays deferred, which avoids €300,000 a year but reduces nothing that is spent today. Additional contribution from customers served faster is counted only after the second cohort, measured at month six once the data-quality step is in place, shows 50 hours or less with waiting time down. The €140,000 of headroom does not include financing costs: interest and principal are paid from operating cash on the schedule above, and the headroom is a reserve against the work overrunning or the cohort arriving late. If it were drawn to meet a payment, the plan would be short by the same amount.

A is rejected on pace, not on money: it would commit the company to an expansion the pilot has not yet earned, and the €1.9m surplus would cost a permanent share of the company to insure against a risk the staging covers with a smaller cushion. Also rejected: raising nothing and funding only the €180,000 build from cash, which leaves no transition, no interim capacity and no runway, so a late cohort result would force a raise under pressure.

**What would reverse the decision.** Signed commitments from customers in the second country would make A’s pace fit the work. On the loan side the review is dated: Priya measures the first cohort of eight at day 90, counted from the board meeting that adopts the plan, and the board decides at day 100, as in [The First Hundred Days: Turn Expectations Into a Funded Plan](#first-hundred-days). Below 60 hours is green: request the expansion at the next quarterly review. Between 60 and 70 hours is amber: fund the data-quality step, €40,000 and four engineer-weeks from the €140,000 reserve, and keep expansion and hiring deferred; the 62-hour case sits here. Above 70 hours, or no reduction, is red: reopen hiring, narrow the target or reopen the equity conversation, and do it while the loan still has headroom, not when it doesn’t. Red does not mean the loan is being repaid from capacity that failed to appear; the payments never depended on it. It means the work the loan was sized for is not delivering, and the €40,000 margin in year two has to carry that disappointment as well.

**Who is authorized.** Ines and Sam negotiate the loan or the investment and bring it to the board; the shareholders’ agreement says which decisions also need shareholder consent. Alex and Priya don’t sign the financing. What they can do, and did here, is bring the revised scope, the €610,000 requirement with its components, the alternative of hiring two implementation specialists at about €300,000 a year recurring, and the dated review: the day-90 cohort and the day-100 decision. Their recommendation is about the work: which option to fund, in what sequence, and what would show it isn’t working. Renegotiating the terms belongs to the CEO, the CFO and the board.

**The scale of the financing matters more than its label.** Suppose Larkspur were instead carrying acquisition borrowing. Annual interest of €4m leaves €3m less for other uses than interest of €1m, all else equal, and that €3m is five times the onboarding need. Neither figure establishes affordability on its own: the company also has to fund tax, investment and the timing of customer payments. [Find the Cash Behind Your Technology Budget](#obligations-before-budget) follows those obligations through a worked bridge.

## Control and Partnership Are Separate Questions

A majority investor can have substantial influence, but the actual exercise of control depends on the agreements: a minority investor may hold approval rights over financing, budgets, hiring or a sale, and a founder who keeps shares may not keep the same role. Ask for the decision boundaries in plain language — who approves material hiring, who funds a product transition if the original plan proves wrong, which decisions need board or shareholder consent — and let legal advisers resolve the contractual interpretation. [Decide Who Decides, Before You Disagree](#decide-who-decides) builds that authority map. Whether the investor can supply follow-on money, relevant experience and steady behavior when the plan runs into difficulty is a separate assessment. The survey by Gompers, Kaplan and Mukharlyamov of 79 private equity investors reports what investors say they emphasize, growth more than cost reduction, not what each one delivered, so treat such claims as hypotheses to test with specific evidence. [S04: PE practitioner survey](https://www.nber.org/papers/w21133) [Judge an Investor by Their Behavior Under Pressure](#investor-under-pressure) sets out that test.

## Revisit the Fit When the Plan Changes

An arrangement that fitted the original plan may become restrictive when demand slows, a migration takes longer or a new market needs more investment. In the Larkspur case, a day-90 cohort that comes in red turns the loan from comfortable to tight: the payments do not change, and the €40,000 margin in year two was set on operating cash alone, with the €140,000 reserve behind it and nothing else. Recheck the remaining cash, repayment dates, approval rights and investor expectations against the revised work, not the original one.

The answer need not be another ownership transaction. The company might change its pace, stage an investment or renegotiate financing. Staying founder-owned, accepting minority funding or selling to a **strategic buyer**, another operating business buying it for commercial reasons, are alternatives to assess against the work ahead. Any of three answers can be correct: the transaction should change, the plan should change, or the company should stay as it is. A business that decides against outside ownership hasn’t failed a test — it has answered the question.

## Questions to Consider

1. *What work does your company need to do next, and what does it cost in full: base work, transition, an allowance for uncertainty and runway to the next decision?*
2. *Which two arrangements could fund that need, and what does each bring besides the money: rights, an expected pace, fixed repayments, a reserve?*
3. *If demand slowed or a migration took twice as long, which part of the arrangement would become restrictive first: cash, repayment dates, approval rights or investor expectations?*
4. *What can you recommend from your role, and who in your company can renegotiate the financing?*

## To Probe Further

- **[Bootstrap Finance: The Art of Start-ups](https://hbr.org/1992/11/bootstrap-finance-the-art-of-start-ups)** — Amar Bhidé, Harvard Business Review, 1992.  
  *A study of fast-growing companies that started without venture capital, the case that raising less can be right, which this chapter treats as one legitimate outcome of a sized comparison rather than a rule.*
- **[How to Fund a Startup](https://paulgraham.com/startupfunding.html)** — Paul Graham, 2005.  
  *A seed investor's walk through the funding sources as a sequence, to read alongside this chapter's point that the sequence is not a required ladder.*
- **[How Do Venture Capitalists Make Decisions?](https://www.nber.org/papers/w22587)** — Paul Gompers, Will Gornall, Steven Kaplan and Ilya Strebulaev, National Bureau of Economic Research working paper, 2016 (Journal of Financial Economics, 2020).  
  *A survey of 885 venture investors on selection, valuation and exits, which is what the "venture funding" row of this chapter's table expects, in their own words.*
- **[Financial Contracting Theory Meets the Real World: An Empirical Analysis of Venture Capital Contracts](https://www.nber.org/papers/w7660)** — Steven Kaplan and Per Strömberg, National Bureau of Economic Research working paper, 2000 (Review of Economic Studies, 2003).  
  *Evidence from real contracts that cash-flow rights, votes, board seats and liquidation rights are allocated separately, the research behind this chapter's claim that control is more than a percentage.*
