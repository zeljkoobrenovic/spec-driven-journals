# 25. Plan Acquisitions and Separations: Account for Extra Work, Not Just Expected Value

![Plan Acquisitions and Separations: Account for Extra Work, Not Just Expected Value — logo](private-techuity/posts/24-acquisition-adds-work-first/assets/images/24-acquisition-adds-work-first/logo.jpeg)

> **IN THIS SECTION, YOU WILL:** Learn how combining businesses and separating one differ, how to fund the transition and decide what waits, and how to test that a new boundary works.

> **WHY INVESTORS CARE:** A buyer’s case for a purchase can assume combined savings, or independence from a former parent, by a stated date; the investor needs to know what work stands between signing and that date, and what planned product work gives way to it.

> **WHY YOU SHOULD CARE:** A transaction plan can assume those benefits before anyone has scheduled the work; the same specialists who carry the product plan will carry the integration, and something will wait.

> **KEY POINTS:**
>
> * The transaction plan can **assume savings or independence before the operating teams have validated the work**. Bring those dependencies into the discussion while scope, price and timing can still change.
> * Combining businesses and separating one are **different processes under one principle**: a new boundary and a continuing customer promise. Choose the depth of integration for a specific benefit; fund independence before the parent’s services end.
> * Integration and separation **use the same specialists as the planned product work**. Decide what waits, who approves the delay and what happens to the date the plan assumed, then test that the boundary works before counting the benefit.

An **acquisition** is the purchase of a business, or of a share of its ownership. It can add customers and products to a group immediately. **Integration**, the work of making selected activities operate together, still has to be planned and done. A **carve-out**, the separation of a business from the larger business that owns it — its **parent** — can transfer ownership legally while leaving essential operating dependencies behind.

The two fictional scenarios in this chapter both involve Larkspur, a company that sells scheduling software to firms with staff working in the field. Four people appear in them: **Ines**, Larkspur’s chief executive; **Alex**, who leads technology; **Priya**, who leads product; and **Sam**, who leads finance.

In the first scenario Larkspur is owned by a **buyout fund**. That is a pool of money gathered from many investors and spent on buying companies outright — or on buying enough of one to **control** it, meaning to have the power to decide the things that matter most: who runs it, what it invests in, whether it is sold. The pool is managed by a firm that decides what to buy and is paid for doing so. The money belongs to the investors; the decisions are made by the firm. The plan is to improve each company and sell it again some years later.

Three separate bodies have to agree before anything happens. The fund’s **deal team** — the people who find and negotiate purchases — proposes buying a smaller company. The fund’s **investment committee**, the group inside the fund that approves purchases, agrees the price and terms. The Larkspur **board**, the directors accountable for the company, approves the integration plan and its budget. Timing also depends on the seller, and on a **regulator’s waiting period**: a legally required pause before certain purchases may be completed, so that competition authorities can review them.

In the second scenario Larkspur is instead a division inside a larger parent, and is being separated from it. The two scenarios use their own dates and figures, separate from each other and from the shared operating-plan record that runs through [LEAD: Manage Funding and Ownership Changes](#part-7).

The plan behind such a transaction can assume savings or independence before the operating teams have checked that the work is possible in the time allowed. It is priced on assumptions about combined savings and separation costs that product and engineering leaders may not have been asked about. Bring those dependencies into the discussion while the scope, the price and the timetable can still change; once the assumptions become the company’s plan, they are obligations the company has to meet.

This chapter closes Part V by taking its question, what a change in size costs before it pays back, to the company’s boundary. An acquisition or separation tests product logic, architecture, costs, security and organization against a transaction timetable with little room for delay. The chapter [TeamSystem: Each New Owner Inherits Progress and Unfinished Work](#teamsystem) shows what accumulates when that happens repeatedly.

## One Principle: A New Boundary, a Continuing Customer Promise

Combining and separating look like opposites, but they pose the same challenge. A transaction draws a new boundary around a business. Customers on both sides of it expect the product to keep working, contracts to be honored and support to answer. The work is to define which capabilities must operate across the new boundary, who is accountable for each, and what transition makes that possible. The transaction date changes ownership; operational readiness is a separate condition with its own evidence.

A buyer’s **thesis** is its explanation of how the purchase will create value: what will be better, and by when. To **price it in** is to use that expectation to justify the price paid, which means the buyer has already paid for a benefit that the company still has to produce.

Each process has its own completion test, stated here and developed below. **Combining** is complete when the chosen integration works for customers, the specific benefit in the thesis has been evidenced, and any duplicated cost counted as a saving has actually ended. A thesis that needs only shared reporting is complete without a merged product or a single support team; the test follows the chosen depth, it does not make deeper integration obligatory. **Separating** is complete when the business has run for a defined period with no temporary service from the parent, under its own accountable leaders, at a cost the operating plan recognizes. A service the business deliberately keeps buying from its former parent under a priced, terminable contract is a supplier relationship the plan carries, not a dependency the test has to remove.

## Combining Businesses

{id: acquisition-adds-work-first--write-the-thesis-as-what-changes}
### Write the Thesis as What Changes

A **buy-and-build** strategy uses a company as a base for further acquisitions. The base is often called the **platform company** and each later purchase an **add-on acquisition**; “platform” here describes the business’s role in the strategy, not a software system. The proposed value may come from reaching customers through more sales routes, from a wider product range, from capabilities one company lacks, from buying supplies more cheaply, from spreading **overhead** — the cost of running the organization that is not attached to any single sale, such as central finance or legal — or from combining several of the many small firms that serve a **fragmented market**, one with no large supplier.

It can also depend partly on how each business is valued. Larger companies are often bought and sold at a higher price for each euro of annual profit than small ones are; a buyer that pays six euros per euro of profit for a small company, and is later valued at ten for the enlarged group, gains from that difference alone. That financial mechanism does nothing to the business and is distinct from **operating synergy**, a benefit created by actually combining activities, such as a lower shared cost or a better customer offering.

**Organic growth** is the increase in sales from the same set of businesses, measured over comparable periods — this year’s three months against last year’s same three months, for the businesses the group held in both. It excludes the increase that arrives simply because a company was bought: **revenue** — sales income before costs — that was already being earned somewhere else and has merely changed owner. Two things follow. A larger revenue number for the combined group doesn’t establish organic growth, and neither does separating acquired revenue from existing revenue: that separation shows where the sales *come from*, while growth is a question about *change over time*, which needs the earlier period to compare against. The TeamSystem case shows how acquisition dates and constructed full-year comparisons can change the apparent growth rate: the chapter [TeamSystem: Each New Owner Inherits Progress and Unfinished Work](#teamsystem). A combined product catalogue isn’t necessarily a more useful customer proposition, and a fall in the group’s costs measured against its revenue isn’t evidence that every acquired business improved.

Write the thesis in terms of what customers or the company will do differently. If the thesis is **cross-selling** — selling an additional product to customers the group already has — identify overlapping customer needs and a practical way for the sales team to offer and support those extra products. If it’s product integration, identify the shared workflow and data boundaries. If it’s cost reduction, identify the actual assets, contracts and roles that can change. Where the roles are people, the saving arrives only after the **notice period**, the time an employee must be told in advance and usually paid for, and after **severance**, the compensation owed when employment ends — and only once the receiving team has passed the knowledge-transfer test in the chapter [Scale the Team Up: Headcount Is Not Capacity](#fix-decisions-before-hiring); the chapter [Scale the Team Down: Decide What Work Stops, Not Just Who Leaves](#anatomy-of-a-layoff) reconciles a staffing reduction by date and shows what a consolidation after an acquisition has to check before the synergy date is fixed.

### Choose the Depth of Integration

| Model | Potential value | Main question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Preserve separate products | Retain focus and domain expertise | Which group capabilities justify common ownership? |
| Share selected services | Reduce repeated work or improve scarce capabilities | Does the service improve outcomes after transition and coordination costs? |
| Connect customer workflows | Make complementary products more useful | Can sign-in, data, contracts, support, and experience work together? |
| Consolidate products or platforms | Remove duplication and create one coherent offering | Can customers migrate without losing essential value? |

These are design options, not maturity levels. A company can sensibly choose different depths in different areas. The intended benefit and the authority behind it decide the depth. A buyout fund that wants the two businesses to report their results in the same format, so that it can compare and combine them, needs the first or second row. A **corporate buyer** — an operating company buying another business to run as part of its own, rather than to sell it on — that expects the acquired product to be sold to its own customers *through one combined offering* needs the third or fourth. Note the qualification: simply selling the acquired product to its own customers needs neither row. The sales team can offer two separate products to the same customer, and often that is the cheaper and faster way to test whether the demand is there. It is the promise of a single joined thing — one sign-in, one bill, one screen — that pulls the work down into the deeper rows. A **minority investment**, the purchase of less than a controlling share, can fund a commercial partnership without authorizing the deeper rows; the chapter [Clarify Authority: Decide Who Decides Before You Disagree](#decide-who-decides) covers what each arrangement authorizes.

Figure 1 sets the three broad choices side by side. On the left, the two businesses keep their own products, their own teams and their own running costs, joined only by a light commercial agreement. In the middle they keep separate products but share the services underneath — the same set of tools, the same data services, the same working practices — which removes repeated work but makes each product depend on a shared foundation. On the right they become a single product with one combined store of customer data behind it — one way of delivering a joined-up customer experience, and the hardest of the three to undo.

That last column is a design choice, not a definition. What customers experience is a consistent view of their own information: the same customers and jobs wherever they look, and changes appearing in both places.

One combined store delivers that directly, and it is what this scenario picks. It is not the only way. Two systems can each keep their own records and exchange what the other needs. That leaves each product free to evolve on its own timetable, at the cost of keeping the exchange working and accepting that the two sides are briefly out of step. Microsoft’s guidance for services that deliberately do not share a database sets out the trade-off, including how one screen can present information drawn from several separately owned stores. [S28: Challenges and solutions for distributed data management, Microsoft](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/dotnet/architecture/microservices/architect-microservice-container-applications/distributed-data-management)

So state the customer outcome the thesis needs, then choose the storage that delivers it. Be clear which of the two you are funding: the cost and the reversibility differ sharply, and neither is the mature version of the other.

The same boundary question governs group standards. Common security expectations, incident definitions or financial reporting can make coordination easier; requiring unrelated businesses to build and run their applications on the same technologies needs a much stronger case. Products serving different customers can need different structures, and an acquired business can carry useful local knowledge in the way it works. A proposed standard should state the problem it solves, the cost of compliance, the exceptions process and the person who funds the transition; if no one can explain the customer or operating benefit, uniformity is being treated as an end in itself. The chapter [Plan for Growth: Decide What (Not) to Change in Your Systems](#growth-into-design) applies the same test to the company’s own system boundaries.

The Skype case in the chapter [Hilton and Skype: A Successful Exit Still Needs Explaining](#hilton-and-skype) shows why ownership of essential technology can be a transaction issue. **Intellectual property (IP)** means legal rights over assets such as software and inventions. Skype’s filings before the sale describe settling a dispute and acquiring core technology rights, alongside investment in the product and its supporting systems. [S27: Skype registration filing](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1498209/000119312511096544/ds1a.htm) The bounded lesson is to inspect the capability boundary, not just the source code.

### Decide What Waits: A Capacity Decision

The same engineers may be needed to deliver the planned product work, keep the service running, support the investigation of the next purchase and integrate the last one. That investigation is **due diligence**: the examination of a business before buying it, covering its finances, its contracts, its technology and its risks. The business being examined is the **target**. A plan that allocates the same people’s time to every one of those activities independently creates capacity that does not exist. Build a single view of who is needed where, identify the people who can’t be duplicated, and test the pace of purchases against it. An attractive target can still be hard to absorb right now.

**Larkspur example (fictional, separate assumptions).**

*The situation.* Larkspur, owned in this scenario by a buyout fund, buys a smaller company with forty employees whose product is **field dispatch**: deciding which engineer goes to which job, and sending them there. Larkspur’s own product is a **scheduling engine**, the software that plans that work in advance. The purchase closes on 1 April 2026. The priced thesis is the third row of the table: connect the two products so that a customer signing in once sees the same customers and jobs in both, and Larkspur’s scheduling customers can buy dispatch as an add-on. The combined offering is to be on sale nine months after closing — 1 January 2027 — with the extra sales it brings appearing in the plan from February 2027.

*The competing demands.* Three pieces of work need the same two engineers, the only two who understand the scheduling engine, in the six months from April to September 2026:

- **The integration** — sixteen engineer-weeks. One **engineer-week** is one engineer working for one week, so sixteen is either engineer for sixteen weeks or both for eight.
- **The customer portal** — ten engineer-weeks. This is the online service through which customers see and change their own bookings; it is the next promised item on Larkspur’s product plan, committed to named customers for the quarter running July to September 2026.
- **Diligence on a second target** — three engineer-weeks. The deal team wants a second company examined in May 2026, the month after closing.

*The arithmetic.* Two engineers across twenty-six weeks is fifty-two engineer-weeks. Six go to leave and about twenty to running and supporting the scheduling engine, which leaves **twenty-six engineer-weeks of project capacity** against **twenty-nine** required. The twenty weeks of support are an average, not a promise: they are the **operating reserve**. An incident requiring more time than the reserve allows reduces project capacity. Three engineer-weeks of work do not fit in the half-year even before that happens.

*The revised schedule.* Both engineers work on the integration from April to the end of June, which absorbs the sixteen weeks and leaves the portal and the diligence to share the ten project weeks remaining in July, August and September:

| Period | Integration | Customer portal | Second-target diligence | Support and leave | Total |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Apr–Jun 2026 | 16 (design, then sign-in and customer data) | — | — | 10 | 26 |
| Jul–Sep 2026 | — | 7 of the ten | 3, starting July | 16 | 26 |
| Oct–Dec 2026 | 2 in November, release support only | remaining 3; portal delivered by 31 December | — | 10 | 15 of 26 used |

Every figure is engineer-weeks, and each quarter has twenty-six of them — two engineers for thirteen weeks. Support and leave are not spread evenly: the six weeks of leave fall mostly in July and August, which is why that quarter absorbs sixteen weeks of non-project time against the first quarter’s ten, and why only ten project weeks survive in it. Across the April–September half-year the two rows add to the twenty-six project weeks and the twenty-six of support and leave derived above. October–December is the following quarter and sits outside that arithmetic: sixteen project weeks are available there and five are used, which is the slack the portal’s move relies on.

*The decision.* The integration goes first. What finishes at the end of June is **the scheduling-engine engineers’ part of it** — the sixteen engineer-weeks of design, sign-in and customer-data work that only they can do. That is the milestone the table tracks, and it is not the launch.

What happens between July and December is packaging, testing and selling the combined offering, and it has its own funded team: the acquired company’s own twelve-person dispatch team, who keep working through the transition and who build the joined screens on top of the data connection the two engineers have finished; Priya’s product and marketing people, who write the pricing and the sales material; and the existing support team, who are trained on the combined product in November. None of that work is in the table above, because none of it needs the two scheduling-engine engineers. They are back on ordinary product work from July, which is what makes the portal’s remaining three weeks in October–December possible at all. The one exception is named and bounded: **two engineer-weeks of their time in November** for release support and fixes found in testing, which come out of the sixteen project weeks that quarter and leave the portal’s three untouched.

So the date the transaction was priced on holds, and holds for a reason that can be checked. The portal moves one quarter, from the July–September quarter to the October–December one, and is delivered by 31 December 2026. The diligence moves from May to July — the deal team asked for a start on 18 May 2026, and it now starts on 6 July, seven weeks later — once the sign-in design is settled, so it no longer competes with the piece of work the purchase was priced on.

- **Rejected:** doing all three at once, which is capacity that does not exist; giving the integration to contractors, who do not know the engine and would add a fourth person to a two-person dependency without removing it; and delaying the integration instead, which would push the combined offering to April 2027 and the extra sales with it, changing the case the transaction was priced on.
- **Funding:** the integration’s €150,000 is in the financial forecast the buyer used to assess the purchase. The portal delay costs no cash, but a commitment made to named customers slips by three months.
- **Scarce capacity:** the two scheduling-engine engineers.
- **Authorized:** the Larkspur board approves the change to the product plan; Ines tells the fund’s deal team the new diligence timing, and the investment committee is told that the second target waits.
- **Evidence that would change it:** the two engineers’ initial April–June work running past its sixteen engineer-weeks — the November release-support fortnight is planned separately and does not count against that figure — at which point Sam re-works the expected extra sales against a later date for the combined offering; or a portal customer signalling that it will not renew, at which point the board revisits the order.

Integration estimates should include data cleanup, customer communication, training, running old and new systems together, contract changes, helping customers move and retiring the old systems. A platform isn’t retired when its replacement launches. It’s retired when the remaining customer and operating obligations have been resolved; only then can the duplicated cost be counted as saved, and for a consolidation thesis that is the completion test.

## Separating a Business

{id: acquisition-adds-work-first--the-independence-bill}
### The Independence Bill

A division can rely on its parent for sign-in and account control, networks, finance systems, **procurement** — the buying of supplies and services — licences, customer contracts, support, security and employment services. Its past accounts may show a share of the parent’s costs charged to it, an **allocation**. An allocation is an accounting split of what the parent already spends; it is not a quotation for the same services bought separately, and the two can differ substantially in either direction.

A **transition services agreement**, or TSA, provides temporary access to services after separation. Examine its scope, price, duration, **service levels** — the agreed standards for how available the service will be and how quickly problems are answered — and the conditions for ending it. A TSA buys time; it doesn’t perform the separation. It is also different from a service the business chooses to keep buying from the former parent after separation under an ordinary supplier contract; that choice belongs in the operating plan at its real price, and it is not what the independence test is meant to end.

The technology plan should distinguish three stages: **Day 1 continuity**, keeping services working on the first day of separate ownership; establishing independent operations; and making later improvements. Trying to optimize every system before separation can put continuity at risk. Reproducing the parent’s setup without questioning its cost can create a permanently expensive business. Whatever the future owner, someone must fund the replacement of the parent’s services before those services end; bring that budget into the transaction discussion while there is still time to change the scope, price or timetable.

**Larkspur example (fictional, separate assumptions).** In this scenario Larkspur is a division being separated from a larger parent. The share of the parent’s **information technology (IT)** costs charged to it is €400,000 a year. Buying the same services independently costs €700,000 a year, plus €300,000 of one-time work to set them up. The financial forecast behind the transaction has to carry both: €300,000 more every year, and €300,000 once. A division’s past **margin** — its profit as a share of its sales — is calculated on the €400,000, so quoting it without this bridge overstates what the separated company will actually have left.

{id: acquisition-adds-work-first--complete-the-bridge-dates-milestones-and-an-independence-test}
### Complete the Bridge: Dates, Milestones and an Independence Test

The cost bridge is not a plan until it has dates, an accountable leader for each service and a test that decides when the parent’s services can end. In the scenario the separation closes on 1 July 2026. The TSA runs for twelve months, to 30 June 2027, and covers three service bundles at €35,000 a month in total, close to the parent’s old allocation of €400,000 a year: **sign-in and account controls** — deciding who is allowed into which system — together with email and the management of work laptops and phones, at €10,000; finance, procurement and payroll at €10,000; and network, security monitoring and the licences held in the parent’s name at €15,000. A bundle’s charge stops when it is switched off. A **standby charge** of €5,000 a month then runs from the first switch-off to the end of the agreement, which keeps the parent’s staff available to turn a service back on. Three further terms matter as much as the monthly price, and all three are agreed before closing rather than negotiated in a crisis:

- **Reconnection.** Standby buys availability, not the service itself. If a bundle is switched back on, its own monthly charge resumes for every month it is on again — €10,000, €10,000 or €15,000 — on top of the standby charge. Reconnecting all three would cost €35,000 a month.
- **Extension.** One extension of three months — July, August and September 2027 — is available at 150% of the full monthly charge: €52,500 a month, €157,500 in total. That price covers all three bundles and the standby charge for those months, so nothing else is billed alongside it. The three months are sold together; individual months cannot be declined once the extension is running, and an early pass does not refund the rest. Two dates govern it. It must be **reserved in writing by 31 March 2027**; after that date the parent is not obliged to grant it at all. Reserving costs nothing, and the reservation can be cancelled in writing **up to 31 May 2027**. From 1 June it becomes a binding purchase of the full €157,500, whether or not the extension is used. Those two dates are what make the reservation a reflex rather than a judgement: it cannot be made after March, and a March forecast cannot tell anyone what May will show.
- **Corrective work.** Neither charge pays for repairing the replacement that failed. That engineering work is Larkspur’s own cost and needs its own allowance.

A missed date therefore has a price as well as a delay: every month a bundle stays on costs its share, a bundle turned back on costs its share again, and a test that cannot be run inside the twelve months costs the extension.

Alex is the accountable company leader for the technology services and Sam for the finance, procurement and payroll systems. Each replacement follows the same rule: it **goes live** — starts being used for real work — on its milestone date, then runs for two months alongside the parent’s service in a **parallel run**, with the parent’s service kept as the **fallback**, the thing to return to if the replacement fails. The parent’s service is then switched off.

The dates work backwards from the test. It needs every parent service off for a whole calendar month, so the last replacement has to be live by the end of February 2027 and switched off by the end of April. The network work is planned against that date rather than against the end of the agreement, and it starts in November while the sign-in systems are in their parallel run. The finance system goes live at the year end so that the new **financial year** — the twelve-month period a company’s accounts are drawn up for — starts on it, which avoids splitting one year’s accounts across two systems.

| Date | Milestone | Parallel run | Parent service off | What is tested |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 Jul 2026 | Closing; TSA starts at €35,000 a month | — | — | Day 1 continuity: customers served, support answered, staff paid, all still on the parent’s services |
| 31 Oct 2026 | Sign-in and account controls, email and device management live on Larkspur’s systems (Alex) | Nov–Dec 2026 | 31 Dec 2026 | Every employee signs in and receives mail on the new systems; one new joiner is set up without the parent |
| 31 Dec 2026 | Finance, procurement and payroll systems live (Sam) | Jan–Feb 2027 | 28 Feb 2027 | The January **month-end close** — completing and checking one month’s accounts — done in both systems with matching results; February suppliers and salaries paid from the new system |
| 28 Feb 2027 | Network, security monitoring and remaining licences live (Alex) | Mar–Apr 2027 | 30 Apr 2027 | All traffic on Larkspur’s network; a test alert detected by its own monitoring; every licence in Larkspur’s name |
| 31 Mar 2027 | Written deadline for reserving the extension; it is reserved whatever the March position shows | — | — | Nothing is being judged here: the reservation is free, cancellable to 31 May, and cannot be made later |
| May 2027 | Independent-service test | — | All off | Month-end close, a new customer set up to use the service, a software release and a restore within the recovery objective, with no access to the parent |
| 31 May 2027 | Last date to cancel the reservation at no cost; from 1 June the €157,500 is committed in full | — | — | Acting on the May test result, which is known before the month ends |
| Jun 2027 | If the May test fails: the reservation is left to run and the €157,500 falls due; the failed bundle is reconnected under the existing agreement at its own monthly charge (€10,000, €10,000 or €15,000) plus standby, and the replacement is corrected | Jun–Jul 2027 | 31 Jul 2027 | Whether the correction is understood well enough to retest in August |
| 30 Jun 2027 | TSA expires if the May test passed | — | — | — |
| Jul–Sep 2027 | Extension if the May test failed: 3 × €52,500 = €157,500, committed in full from 1 June | — | — | Second attempt in August, the whole month with every parent service off; September is spare time for a narrow fix, not for another full cycle |

On this calendar the parent’s charges come to €320,000: six months at €35,000 (€210,000), two months at €25,000 plus standby (€60,000), two at €15,000 plus standby (€40,000) and two on standby alone (€10,000). Had every bundle run to 30 June, the twelve months would have cost €420,000. Switching services off as they are replaced therefore **avoids €100,000 of the parent’s charges** against that full-term comparison.

That €100,000 is not a saving on the transition, and it does not pay for the parallel runs. Three different things are being spent, and the plan keeps them on separate lines:

| Line | This calendar | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Parent’s charges under the agreement | €320,000 over the transition year | €420,000 if no bundle were ever switched off |
| Larkspur’s own running costs for the replacements | builds from €0 in July 2026 towards €700,000 a year | Each replaced service starts costing from the month it goes live, so during a parallel run both are being paid for |
| One-time separation work | €300,000 | Building, migrating, testing and switching over |

During each two-month parallel run the company is paying the parent for the old service *and* itself for the new one. That overlap is a cost, not something the €100,000 covers; the avoided charges and the overlap are separate figures that happen to sit in the same months. The net cost of the transition is the second and third lines added to the first, compared with the €400,000 the division used to be charged.

The **independent-service test** is May 2027: a full month in which every parent service has already been switched off, and the business does four ordinary things with no access to the parent. It closes its month-end accounts. It sets up a new customer from scratch. It puts a new version of its software into use. And it deliberately destroys and rebuilds a working service and its data within the **recovery objective** — the agreed maximum time to be back in service and the maximum amount of recent data it is acceptable to lose — from the chapter [Build And Test Resilience: Backups Are Not Enough](#prove-you-can-restore).

May is chosen because the agreement is still in force on the standby charge, so a failed service can be turned back on under terms already agreed rather than renegotiated under pressure. The result is known before the month ends, which is what lets the 31 May cancellation date do any work at all.

If the month passes, the reservation is cancelled before 31 May, the agreement expires on 30 June, and none of the contingency is drawn. If it fails, the failed bundle is reconnected in June and the replacement corrected; the reservation is left to run, so the €157,500 falls due; and the second attempt is the whole of August. A failure discovered in May cannot reach back to a request that had to be made in March — which is why the reservation is made in March whatever the forecast then says.

*The decision.* Replace the services in stages against dated milestones, and hold the test in May 2027, while the agreement can still turn a service back on under terms already agreed.

- **Rejected:** relying on repeated extensions with no end, which the parent will not offer and which leaves the €300,000 a year unexplained; testing after the agreement has expired, which would turn a failed test into a renegotiation with no contractual fallback; and copying the parent’s setup service for service, which would lock in a cost the separated business does not need.
- **Funding.** Four amounts, each with its own purpose. €300,000 one-time for the separation work, in the forecast behind the transaction. The parent’s charges, €320,000 on this calendar, as operating cost in the transition year in place of the old €400,000 allocation, alongside each replacement from the month it goes live. €300,000 a year recurring in the operating plan, in full from the first year after the agreement ends. And a **fallback contingency of €252,500**, held separately and sized for the worst case: €35,000 if all three bundles had to be reconnected for one month, €157,500 for the extension, and €60,000 for corrective engineering work, which neither the reconnection charge nor the extension fee buys.
- **Reserving the fallback, which is not the same as spending it.** Alex reserves the extension in writing by 31 March 2027; the board approves that standing reservation with the plan in 2026, so nobody has to convene in March to authorize a free option. The money is then **released against events**: the board, on Sam’s and Alex’s joint recommendation, releases the reconnection charge and the corrective allowance when a bundle actually has to be turned back on. The extension fee is the one item with a date rather than an event attached to it — cancel by 31 May and it costs nothing; let 31 May pass and all €157,500 is owed.
- **Scarce capacity:** the operations lead, who runs the sign-in and network migrations and the restore test, which is why the network work waits until the sign-in systems are live.
- **Authorized:** the board of the separated company approves the plan, the standing reservation and each release of money against it; Alex signs the reservation itself. The parent agrees only the agreement’s terms, and nothing beyond its end date: continued parent service past 30 September needs the parent’s fresh consent, which the board cannot supply. Commissioning an outside replacement instead is the board’s own decision — but only if it is taken early enough to matter.
- **Evidence that would change it:** the network milestone slipping, which is the one date the whole calendar rests on; a slip visible by 31 March 2027 also triggers the outside-supplier decision below.

*What a slip costs.* The network replacement is due live on 28 February 2027. Every later go-live pushes the whole chain by the same number of months, because the rule after go-live is fixed: two full months running beside the parent, the parent’s service off at the end of the second, then the first whole month with everything off is the test.

| Network go-live | Parallel run | Parent service off | First test month | Months left before the extension ends on 30 Sep |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| End Feb 2027 (the plan) | Mar–Apr | 30 Apr | May | four — enough for a full repair and an August retest |
| End Mar 2027 | Apr–May | 31 May | June | three — a narrow fix only |
| End Apr 2027 | May–Jun | 30 Jun | July | two — a narrow fix only |
| End May 2027 | Jun–Jul | 31 Jul | August | one — a narrow fix only |
| End Jun 2027 | Jul–Aug | 31 Aug | September | none |

Two dates come out of that table, and the plan names both.

**31 March 2027 is the last go-live that keeps the first test inside the original agreement.** It moves the last switch-off to 31 May and the test to June. But the cancellation date is 31 May, and a June test is not finished until 30 June, so the company cannot know the result in time to cancel. On this path the €157,500 is committed whether June passes or not. Going live a month late therefore costs the extension fee even when the test succeeds — which is the clearest reason to protect the February date.

**31 May 2027 is the management cutoff**, the last go-live that still leaves a spare month. Past it, the test and the deadline land in the same month.

*Spare months are not recovery months.* This is the distinction the table above cannot show. The recovery sequence after a failure is fixed and takes three months: correct the replacement, run two months beside the reconnected parent service, then hold another full independent month. Only the planned May test leaves room for it — fail in May, correct in June, run parallel June–July, switch off 31 July, retest in August. A first test in July or August leaves one or two spare months, which is time for a narrow fix that does not need another parallel run, not for the full cycle.

*If that is not enough.* The **absolute contractual limit is 30 June 2027**. A replacement live at the end of June still runs beside the parent through July and August, switches off on 31 August, and gets September as a complete independent month — but that September month is a *first* test, not a retest, and nothing follows it. A September failure cannot be answered inside the agreement at all. That is why the board works to 31 May rather than to 30 June, and why it protects 28 February hardest of all: the difference between the plan and the limit is the whole of the recovery time.

Past 30 June, or after a failed September test, the plan has run out of contractual room. Two routes remain, and neither is assured.

The first is to **ask the parent to keep supplying the service past 30 September**, under a new contract at whatever price the parent then names. The agreement obliges the parent to nothing after that date, so this is a request, not a right. The parent may refuse outright, or quote a price that makes the answer obvious.

The second is to **buy the failing service from an outside supplier**. This needs no one’s permission, which is its advantage — but permission is not the binding constraint. Contracting, access rights, migration and testing all have to finish before the parent stops, and none of that can be compressed into the weeks left after a September failure. It is a real route only if it was prepared in advance: a named provider, reserved capacity, the rights to move the data, funded migration and a dated handover, all agreed while there was still time. Prepared that way it becomes a second fallback; discovered in September it is a plan for next year.

So the board sets a decision deadline of **31 March 2027** for this too, the same date as the extension reservation. If the network milestone has slipped by then, the board commissions the outside replacement in parallel — because a supplier engaged in March can be ready by September, and one engaged in September cannot.

If neither route is in place, the honest statement is that the plan cannot prevent the interruption. The parent stops on 30 September; a service the separated company cannot yet run itself stops with it, and customers feel it. That possibility is the reason the February milestone, the March reservation and the May test are all held as firmly as they are.

Either way the transaction’s funding is re-opened on the new figures, and the separated company carries a dependency its operating plan did not price. A test that turns up a service the plan never listed does the same thing on a smaller scale: the recurring cost has to be revised to carry it, because the replacement has to be bought, run and funded from then on.

Figure 2 draws the problem the whole calendar exists to solve. On the left, inside the parent’s boundary, the **business unit** — the part being separated — hangs off four services it does not own: an **identity service** (the sign-in and account controls described above — what decides who is allowed into which system), a **billing service** (charging customers and collecting the money), a **support service** (answering customers when something is wrong) and an **infrastructure service** (the computers, storage and network the software actually runs on). Drawing the separation line cuts all four connections at once. On the right, the independent company needs each one replaced by something it owns or by a written agreement to keep using the parent’s — and the gold arrows mark where neither exists yet. Those are the gaps that become someone’s milestone, or become a negotiation held under time pressure.

## Investigate Rights and Dependencies Before the Purchase

Diligence is where the rights question belongs. It should produce evidence of who holds and can use the code, the data, the **trademarks** — the protected names and symbols a business trades under — the licences and the contracts. Identify services that continue only with a supplier’s or parent’s agreement. Test whether customer data can be separated accurately and whether the business can run if a parent-provided service ends.

Use qualified specialists for legal rights, employment arrangements, privacy and transaction restrictions. Before closing, planning and information sharing must follow the applicable permissions and legal constraints. Operational enthusiasm isn’t authority to combine systems or direct the target’s business early.

A material separation or integration risk can change the price, financing, conditions or feasibility of the investment. It shouldn’t be buried in a technical appendix because the deal team already likes the company.

{id: acquisition-adds-work-first--measure-the-customer-and-cash-results}
## Measure the Customer and Cash Results

Whatever is measured should follow the benefit the thesis actually named. For cross-selling, count customers who have a real prospect of buying — the ones that fit the product and have budget and a reason to act — and the money the extra sales leave after the cost of making and supporting them, not the number of accounts someone contacted. For combining products, track how many customers have moved, which features they kept, how many left, whether service quality held, and which costs have genuinely stopped rather than merely been scheduled to stop. For a carve-out, track the replacement milestones, the independence test, and any obligation left stranded on the wrong side of the new boundary — a contract, a licence or a commitment that nobody is now clearly responsible for.

Finance should be able to show how the group’s result is made up: the profit that came with the purchased businesses, the change within the existing business, the one-time costs, and the benefits actually achieved rather than merely forecast. A cost reduction counted in both companies’ plans counts once. A sales opportunity is not realized because the products are now under common ownership.

Company leaders need to make the transition feasible and its claimed benefits verifiable. An investor’s adviser can bring patterns and specialists from other acquisitions; the company still needs **an accountable leader for each part** of the operating plan. Completing a transaction changes ownership. Completing the operating transition requires the evidence in the two completion tests above: customers can still use the product, the benefit the thesis named has appeared, responsibilities are clear and the costs or dependencies the plan said would end have actually ended. The two milestones can be far apart, and the obligations that remain open at an ownership change are the subject of the chapter [Manage the Handover: Carry Forward the Evidence and Obligations](#handover-of-obligations).

Both Larkspur examples used specialists the company did not have, from the fund’s deal team, the parent and outside advisers, and each depended on people the company could not duplicate: the two scheduling-engine engineers in one, the operations lead in the other. Part III set out where such additional capability comes from, on what terms, and how to keep the authority to decide inside the company, beginning with [Plan Investor Support: Match the Help to Company Priorities](#operating-model-blueprints). Part VI turns to the technology the company already runs: what it costs, and whether it would survive a failure.

{id: acquisition-adds-work-first--questions-to-consider}
## Questions to Consider

1. *For the acquisition or separation closest to your company, what specific benefit is expected, which depth of integration does it need, and what will customers or the company do differently to realize it?*
2. *Which promised product item or diligence request waits while the same specialists do the integration, who approved that order, and what happens to the date the plan assumed?*
3. *If your business were separated from its parent tomorrow, which services would stop, when does each replacement land, and what test would show the business runs without the parent?*
4. *What rights to code, data, licences and contracts does your company actually hold, and which depend on someone else’s continued agreement?*
