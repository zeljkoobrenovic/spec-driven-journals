# 30. Critically Evaluate AI Costs: Measure the Return per Task and per Period

![Critically Evaluate AI Costs: Measure the Return per Task and per Period — logo](private-techuity/posts/29-ai-worth-its-cost/assets/images/29-ai-worth-its-cost/logo.jpeg)

> **IN THIS SECTION, YOU WILL:** Learn to compute the return on an AI feature or tool for a stated period from figures you actually have, to explain why an AI bill grows faster than revenue, and to decide how much to commit to a vendor under a stated demand range, so that “what is our AI ROI” gets an answer with a unit, a period and a condition attached. **ROI**, return on investment, is the money an investment leaves after its costs, for each euro of those costs, over a stated period; the chapter explains it with an example before using it.

> **WHY INVESTORS CARE:** Larkspur sells its AI feature for a fixed monthly subscription and pays a vendor for the model’s work by the amount of text the model reads and writes, which the vendor counts in tokens. An investor sees the subscription revenue in the plan and the vendor’s bill in the accounts, and wants to know whether the difference between them is holding, shrinking, or only expected to improve.

> **WHY YOU SHOULD CARE:** When a feature is priced by the month and paid for by the amount of text it processes, the price stays where you set it while the bill moves with what customers send, how your engineers build the feature and what the vendor charges. That gap can turn a profitable feature into a loss-making one while the dashboard still shows growth.

> **KEY POINTS:**
>
> * **Measure per useful customer task, on one basis.** Cost per token, or per request sent to the model, is the vendor’s unit. Cost per document sorted and accepted, per ticket resolved, per change completed is the company’s, and only that unit can be set against a price.
> * **Separate why the bill changed.** Usage (more customers, more documents), rates (the vendor’s price per token) and construction (how the feature is built: how much text it sends with each document, how often it retries, which model it uses) move the bill for different reasons, have different owners and take different remedies. A vendor price cut can offset part of a construction increase and make the bill look tamer than the construction is.
> * **Return has a period and a condition, and a commitment is a minimum payment.** State the return for a dated period under the quality and review conditions that make it true, set the stop and reprice rules before the review, and size any committed spend, the fixed amount a vendor is owed even when usage falls short, at the low end of a demand range the company can pay for.

**The problem.** Larkspur is the fictional scheduling-software company this book follows. Since 1 April 2027 it has sold an **artificial intelligence (AI)** feature, software that uses a trained model to sort a customer’s incoming inspection documents into categories before maintenance is scheduled, for €300 a month per customer. It is now September 2027 and forty customers pay for it. Sam, who leads finance, has noticed that the vendor’s bill for the feature has grown faster than the feature’s revenue, the fees its customers pay, for four months running. Morgan, the technology adviser to Larkspur’s investor, has asked for “the AI ROI” before the next meeting of the board, the group that oversees the company’s major decisions. Both want a number.

The chapter gives them one by answering three questions in turn: what does the feature cost per useful piece of customer work; why did the bill change; and what is the return for a stated period, under what condition. It then makes the decision those answers lead to: how much of next year’s usage to commit to the vendor in exchange for a discount, and what would make the feature stop paying.

**Where the feature came from.** The chapter [Clarify AI Strategy: Three Different Investment Questions](#ai-strategy-three-questions) set the feature’s conditions. A paid three-customer trial passed its **release gate**, the set of results required before general release, on 11 December 2026: at least 95% of ordinary documents sorted correctly, no safety-relevant document missed, review effort below 1.5 hours a week per customer, and two of three trial customers willing to keep paying €300. The board then funded general release in the 2027 operating plan, the approved plan for running the business and the money attached to it. Those results are taken as given here. The same chapter’s support assistant, which drafts responses for Larkspur’s six support agents, is also running; its figures return below.

The people are the same. Alex leads technology as chief technology officer (CTO); Priya leads product; Ines is the chief executive, who leads the company; Sam leads finance; Morgan advises the investor. Every figure is fictional, and the shared Larkspur ledger, the record of the company’s finances that runs through the chapters [Plan the First Hundred Days: Turn Expectations Into Funded Work](#first-hundred-days) and [Manage the Handover: Carry Forward the Evidence and Obligations](#handover-of-obligations) is not touched.

**Why this bill needs its own reading.** The chapter [Critically Evaluate Cloud Costs: A Lower Bill Is Not Always Better](#cheaper-cloud-bill) showed how to read a cloud bill: through useful service, on a comparable basis, with the reasons costs change separated. This feature’s bill adds one habit. The vendor meters it in **tokens**, the small pieces of text an AI service counts when the model reads and writes, so the cost of the same feature moves with how the feature is built as well as with how much it is used. Not every AI cost works this way: the support assistant is bought as a **licence** per agent, a paid permission to use the software for each person, and some vendors sell fixed subscriptions. But a feature sold by the month and bought by the token has a gap between price and cost that only measurement closes. The claim that AI earns a return, in a feature with a price or a tool that saves staff time, is checked here on money, the way [Scale the Team With AI: Capacity Claims Need the Same Evidence as Headcount](#scale-the-team-with-ai) checks it on capacity.

## Choose a Unit That Explains the Business

The vendor bills per million tokens, and Larkspur’s engineers can tell you the tokens per **model call**, one request sent to the **model**, the trained system that produces each classification, and its answer. Neither is the company’s unit. Larkspur charges a customer €300 a month for documents sorted so that a planner can schedule without reading them, and the useful unit is therefore **a document sorted and accepted**: classified by the feature and not corrected by the customer afterwards. A rejected classification costs tokens and delivers nothing useful.

The same choice applies to the internal tools. The support assistant’s unit is a ticket, one recorded customer request for help, resolved without being reopened, inherited from the pilot in [Clarify AI Strategy: Three Different Investment Questions](#ai-strategy-three-questions); the coding tool’s unit, from [Scale the Team With AI: Capacity Claims Need the Same Evidence as Headcount](#scale-the-team-with-ai), is a software change completed and passing the team’s usual quality checks. A cost per token can fall while the cost per useful unit rises, because the number of tokens the feature spends to produce one accepted result is not fixed. That is the first fact about this kind of spending: **the construction of the feature is a cost driver**, something that changes the bill, and construction is decided by the company’s own engineers.

Three things move the tokens spent per document, and Larkspur’s feature has moved all three since the trial. The **prompt**, the instructions and examples sent to the model with each document, grew when Priya added rules learned from the trial. The **context**, the extra material the feature retrieves and sends along, grew when Alex added similar past documents from the same customer to hold accuracy on unusual formats. And **retries**, repeat calls when the first answer fails a check, grew when the safety check was made stricter, because a document that fails the check is sent again to a **larger model**, a more capable and more expensive version of the trained system. Each of these was a reasonable product decision. Together they raised the **model cost per document** by about 80% between April and August, and nobody had set that figure against the price.

**What the 80% measures.** That measure is what the vendor would charge for the average document at unchanged prices, April’s prices throughout, so that the June price cut does not blur it. It is not a raw token count: a retry to the larger model costs more per token as well as more tokens, so the model mix is inside the figure; the FinOps Foundation’s tokenomics paper listed under To Probe Further separates model tiers, input and output text and cached material for the same reason.

## Put Every Figure on the Same Basis

Sam’s observation that the bill grows faster than revenue is true and, on its own, uninformative. The table puts two months on one basis: cost assigned to the month in which the work was done, as the cloud chapter defines it, so a supplier’s bill for August work counts in August whichever month it is paid, with money paid to suppliers separated from the time of employees who are on the **payroll**, the salaries the company pays every month anyway. Their salaries are money too, but they sit in the company’s payroll forecast whether or not the feature exists; assigning part of them to the feature creates no extra payment. It shows what the feature consumes.

| Feature, monthly | April 2027 | August 2027 |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Paying customers | 20 | 40 |
| Revenue at €300 | €6,000 | €12,000 |
| Documents processed | 9,000 | 26,000 |
| Documents accepted (96%) | 8,640 | 24,960 |
| Model charges: the vendor’s bill for tokens (paid to the vendor) | €1,400 | €5,824 |
| Upkeep: sampled review, test-set runs, and hosting, the paid computing that runs the feature’s own store of past documents (paid to suppliers) | €1,200 | €1,600 |
| **Running cost paid to suppliers** | **€2,600** | **€7,424** |
| Engineering time keeping the feature working (allocated salary cost, already in payroll) | €2,250 (a quarter of one engineer) | €4,500 (half of one engineer) |
| Support time caused by the feature (allocated salary cost, already in payroll) | €400 | €1,200 |
| **Contribution after supplier running cost** | **€3,400** | **€4,576** |
| **Result after supplier cost and allocated staff time** | **€750** | **−€1,124** |
| Supplier running cost per customer | €130 | about €186 |
| Supplier running cost per accepted document | about €0.30 | about €0.30 |

Read across and the picture is more precise than “growing faster than revenue”. Supplier cost per accepted document is flat at about 30 cents (€2,600 over 8,640 documents; €7,424 over 24,960), because the upkeep line is largely fixed: it rose €400 while documents nearly tripled, and spreading it over more documents offset the rise in model charges per document. Supplier cost per customer rose from €130 to about €186 against a €300 price, because customers send more documents than the trial customers did, 650 a month each instead of 450, and each document costs more tokens. And after the allocated staff time, the feature loses money in August, which nobody had seen because engineering time is not on the vendor’s invoice.

A word on the two bold lines. **Margin** means what remains of revenue after a named set of costs, and the name matters: after supplier running cost the feature contributes €4,576 in August; after staff time it loses €1,124. The staff figures are allocations of salaries Larkspur pays anyway, valued at cost. They measure what the feature consumes; they are not the value of the work those engineers would otherwise do, and that work has to be named separately. Half an engineer on a feature that earns €12,000 a month is half an engineer not on the second country’s rules, whose value is the revenue that country’s customers would bring. Which lines Larkspur uses depends on the question: a **cash forecast**, the plan of money expected to enter and leave the company, uses the supplier lines, because payroll is already in it; a decision about whether to keep the feature uses both.

## Separate Usage, Rates and Construction

The model charges rose from €1,400 to €5,824. Three causes, applied in this order and each computed from the inputs above; the order matters because the effects multiply:

| Cause | What changed | Effect on the August bill | Who owns it | Remedy |
| --- | --- | ---: | --- | --- |
| **Usage** | 9,000 documents became 26,000: twice the customers, and 650 documents a month each instead of 450 | +€2,644 at April’s cost per document (€1,400 over 9,000, about 15.6 cents), which puts 26,000 documents at €4,044 | Sales and customers; the growth the plan wanted | None needed if the price covers it; a per-document allowance in the price if it does not |
| **Construction** | Model cost per document up about 80% at unchanged prices: longer prompt, retrieved context, retries to a larger model at its higher price | +€3,236, 80% of €4,044, taking the bill to €7,280 | Product and engineering | Trim the prompt, cap the context, route easy documents to a smaller model (the same tokens at a lower price per token), cache repeated context (keep material that repeats between requests so it is not processed again; the vendor bills it at a reduced rate); each a measured engineering change |
| **Rates** | The vendor cut its list price per token by 20% in June | −€1,456, 20% of €7,280 | The vendor | None available; note that the cut offset part of the construction increase |
| **Total** | | **€5,824** | | |

Each step is rounded to the euro; computed unrounded, the three steps land exactly on the August bill. The cloud chapter separated usage, rates and architecture for the same reason: each has a different owner and a different remedy, and a bill that reports only the total lets the wrong person be blamed. Here the finding is uncomfortable for the product side. The June price cut was reported to the board as good news; without it the bill would have been about €7,300. The cut offset about 45% of the construction increase, €1,456 of €3,236, which is why the reported bill looked tamer than the product decisions behind it. Those decisions, each defensible, raised the cost per document, and nobody tracked it.

Usage adds €2,644, construction adds €3,236, and the vendor's rate cut removes €1,456, so the bill peaks above the August total before the price cut brings it down.](private-techuity/posts/29-ai-worth-its-cost/assets/images/29-ai-worth-its-cost/usage-construction-rates-bridge.jpeg)

**Figure 1:** *The August bill in its three parts. Usage added €2,644, construction added €3,236, and the vendor's June price cut took €1,456 off, offsetting about 45% of the construction increase. Each part has a different owner and a different remedy.*

**Do not count the same saving twice.** Alex proposes construction work: a shorter prompt, a cap on retrieved context, a smaller model for documents the checks pass easily, and caching of the context that repeats within a customer. He estimates it cuts the model cost per document by about 35% at unchanged prices and unchanged quality, two **engineer-weeks** of work (one person’s full working week of effort each), tested on the same 200-document samples the release gate used.

**How the saving is measured.** The saving is not a token count either: the shorter prompt and the capped context send fewer tokens, while the smaller model and the cached context make the remaining tokens cheaper, so a document can cost less with its token count unchanged. That is why the measure throughout is cost per document at unchanged prices, and why a vendor price change is kept as a separate cause.

**Why it comes before the commitment.** If that work is done, the volume eligible for any vendor discount falls with it. The commitment decision below is therefore made after the construction change, not before, so that Larkspur does not commit to paying for tokens it is about to stop using.

**What the saving would do to the monthly result.** Hold August’s volume, upkeep and staff allocation constant and apply the 35% cut. Model charges fall from €5,824 to €3,785.60 (65% of €5,824). Supplier running cost falls from €7,424 to €5,385.60, and supplier cost per customer from about €186 to €134.64, under the €150 limit the stop rules below set. After the allocated staff time the month shows a surplus of €914.40 (€12,000 less €5,385.60 less €5,700) where August lost €1,124: an improvement of €2,038.40 a month.

Two things sit outside that calculation. The two engineer-weeks of construction work, about €4,200 of allocated engineering time, are a one-off, not a monthly cost. And a better month does not repay the trial and release costs at once; it only reduces, month by month, the unrecovered balances the next section counts. The €914.40 is a projected monthly surplus, conditional on the 35% being measured, not evidence that the investment has paid for itself.

## Return With a Unit, a Period and a Condition

**Return on investment (ROI)** is the money an investment leaves after its costs, divided by those costs, for a stated period. Spend €100 on something that brings in €190 over a year: €190 less €100 leaves €90, and €90 divided by €100 is a 90% return for that year. Every word in that sentence does work, and the one most often dropped is the period. An AI feature does not have an ROI; it has an ROI for April to September 2027 under the conditions that held then.

Three different numbers answer “what is the sorting feature worth”, and they should never be added.

- **Customer value.** Before the trial a customer’s planner spent about four hours a week sorting documents by hand, and the trial’s condition keeps checking the feature’s work below 1.5 hours a week. At that limit the planner spends about 10.8 fewer hours a month on sorting: 2.5 hours a week over about 4.33 weeks. At a **loaded hourly cost** of €50, what the employer pays for an hour of that person’s time including employment taxes and benefits, that time is worth roughly €540 a month before the €300 fee. That is why the customer buys. The €540 is a conservative estimate from the limit, not a measured saving: review has to stay below 1.5 hours, so the time freed is at least that. It is also the gross value of time freed, not money the customer necessarily stops spending, and not Larkspur’s money.
- **Revenue.** €300 a month per customer for 190 **customer-months** from April to September, one customer paying for one month: 20 customers in April, 25 in May, 30 in June, 35 in July, 40 in August and 40 in September add up to 190, so €57,000. It is Larkspur’s money before costs.
- **Contribution before payroll.** Revenue less the supplier running cost, which grew from €2,600 in April to €7,424 in August: about €30,100 over the six months, leaving about €26,900. It is a contribution rather than a profit because the engineers’ and support staff’s salaries are not yet deducted. It is also a period figure rather than money in the bank, because each month’s supplier bills are counted in that month, not when they are paid; the reconciliation follows the monthly table.

The monthly figures behind those totals, with September estimated at August’s level because the month is not closed:

| Month 2027 | Customers | Revenue | Supplier running cost | Allocated staff time |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| April | 20 | €6,000 | €2,600 | €2,650 |
| May | 25 | €7,500 | €3,150 | €2,850 |
| June | 30 | €9,000 | €4,000 | €5,300 |
| July | 35 | €10,500 | €5,500 | €5,500 |
| August | 40 | €12,000 | €7,424 | €5,700 |
| September (estimate) | 40 | €12,000 | €7,424 | €5,700 |
| **Total** | **190** | **€57,000** | **€30,098** | **€27,700** |

**How the contribution reconciles to cash.** Customers pay monthly in advance, so all €57,000 had been collected by the end of September; suppliers are paid the month after the work, so the bills for April to August, €22,674, had been paid and September’s €7,424 was still payable in October. Money in less money out was therefore about €34,300 at the end of September, with €7,424 still owed; once that bill is paid the two views agree at €26,902.

The two period figures are projections because September is estimated at August's level.*

On that basis the feature’s **running return** for April to September 2027, its return on supplier running cost before staff costs, is about €26,900 on about €30,100 of supplier running cost, roughly 90%, on the condition that ordinary-sample accuracy stayed at or above 95%, no safety-relevant document was missed and review effort stayed below 1.5 hours a week for each customer, which the monthly samples through August show they did. Because September is estimated at August’s level, the figure is a projection at the September review and becomes a result when the month closes.

A running return is a return before staff costs. It counts only the supplier running cost, the model charges and upkeep paid to outside suppliers, and not the engineering and support time the feature consumes. That time is a running cost too, charged at salary cost: €27,700 over the same six months. With it deducted, the €26,902 becomes a loss of about €800 (€26,902 less €27,700 is −€798), before the trial and release costs below. Both figures answer Morgan’s question about the feature as it runs. The 90% says what the feature earns over what it pays its suppliers; the −€798 says what it earns over everyone it employs. Quoting the first without the second would let it pass for operating profitability.

It is not the answer to whether the feature has paid for itself, which needs the one-off costs as well. The trial cost €45,000 of cash and six engineer-weeks; the general release build cost a further ten engineer-weeks. An engineer-week is valued at about €2,100, the same allocated salary basis as the table’s €4,500 for half an engineer for a month. The reconciliation counts every cost once, money and staff time in separate columns, on the same basis as the contribution above: supplier costs in the months they concern, so September’s bill is counted although it is paid in October.

| | Money from customers and to suppliers | Allocated staff time |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Trial, October to December 2026 | −€45,000 | −€12,600 (six engineer-weeks) |
| General release build, January to March 2027 | — | −€21,000 (ten engineer-weeks) |
| Operating, April to September 2027: revenue €57,000 less supplier running cost €30,098; engineering and support time from the monthly table | +€26,902 | −€27,700 |
| **Balance at the end of September 2027** | **−€18,098** | **−€61,300** |
| **Balance with staff time charged** | **−€79,398** | |

Fees from the three trial customers before April, about €4,000 after their running cost, are left out; counting them would shorten the gap by that amount. So the feature is still about €18,000 short before staff time after six months, and about €79,000 short if the engineers’ and support staff’s time is charged at cost. At August’s contribution of €4,576 a month, on the same cost basis, the €18,000 is recovered in about four more months, by the end of January 2028. The staff time is never recovered at August’s figures, because after staff time the feature loses €1,124 a month; that changes only if the construction work lands, which would turn the month to a surplus of about €914, as computed above. Even then each month would reduce the €79,398 balance by only about €914, so recovering it would take more than seven years at that rate. Both statements are true, for different questions, and the board should hear both.

The internal tool shows why the period matters even more. In the chapter [Clarify AI Strategy: Three Different Investment Questions](#ai-strategy-three-questions), Larkspur’s support team was due a seventh agent on 1 April 2027 at an **employment cost** of €64,000 a year, salary plus the employer’s taxes and charges. Because the assistant passed its January check, the start was moved to 1 October 2027: six months of that cost, €32,000, not paid in 2027. Against it stand €8,000 a year of recurring payments: €3,600 of licences for the six agents and €4,400 of evaluation upkeep, the sampled checks that show the assistant is still answering well. €32,000 less €8,000 leaves €24,000, and €24,000 divided by €8,000 is a return of about 300% for 2027 on those recurring payments.

**What the 300% leaves out.** Rolling the assistant out and training the agents took about €2,000 of existing staff time in 2027, a week of the support operations lead’s time, as [Clarify AI Strategy: Three Different Investment Questions](#ai-strategy-three-questions) counted it. Charge that week as well and the costs are €10,000, the benefit left is €22,000, and the return for 2027 is 220%. The 300% is a return on money paid out; the 220% is a return on all the costs, the same distinction as the feature’s 90% before staff costs. One scenario assumption sits inside both figures: the seventh agent works unassisted during induction for the rest of 2027, as the pilot chapter’s €3,600 for six licences assumes. A seventh licence from October, at about €50 a month, would add €150 and lower the 300% to about 293%.

At the September review that figure is a projection, not a result. Five of the six deferred months have passed and the October start is a month away, and the figure holds only on conditions: the hire stays deferred to October rather than being brought forward; quality holds at the pilot’s reference, with reopened tickets and sampled errors no worse than the comparison group’s; ticket volume stays under about 1,400 a month, the point at which recruiting would have restarted; and no paid cover, overtime or a contractor, is bought to handle the load in the meantime, since that would eat the saving. The 2027 result is confirmed when the year closes.

It is a **benefit of that period**. In 2028 the seventh agent is on the payroll and on the tool from January. Seven licences cost €4,200 and upkeep €4,400, so the tool’s recurring cost rises to about €8,600 a year. The pilot chapter’s “about €8,000 from 2028” is the six-licence figure, written while hiring the seventh agent in October was still an open decision. The tool defers nothing by itself; a further return requires a further operating change, another hire not made or capacity used for work that earns. A tool whose return was a one-off deferral, reported as if it recurred, is how an internal AI programme ends up with a large cumulative ROI and no cash to show for it.

## A Commitment Changes Future Spending

In September the vendor offers Larkspur a **committed-spend agreement** for 2028: promise a fixed monthly payment for twelve months and pay 25% less per token on the usage that payment covers, with usage above it billed at the **list price**, the vendor’s standard published price. The offer is worth considering, and it is a floor under next year’s cost whatever next year brings, because the fixed payment is owed whether the usage arrives or not. The 25% and the amounts are assumptions for this example, not a vendor’s published terms.

Larkspur follows the cloud chapter’s method. Demand is measured as what the feature’s usage would cost at flexible list prices, after the construction work, so every option is compared against one yardstick.

**Demand range.** The forecast is built from three inputs: customers, documents per customer and the cost per document after the construction change. With the 35% saving applied, August’s €5,824 of model charges would have been about €3,790, or about 14.6 cents per document at today’s list price (€3,790 over 26,000 documents). The board’s 2028 forecast, approved in the autumn planning round, puts the feature between 35 customers, if renewals disappoint, and 80, if the second country signs, with 55 expected, at 650 documents a month each. At 14.6 cents a document that is about €3,300 a month at the low end (35 customers, 22,750 documents), about €5,200 expected (35,750 documents) and about €7,600 at the high end (52,000 documents), before any discount. A **stress case** of €2,000 a month, about 21 customers, the largest customers leaving or a substitute product arriving as the chapter [Clarify AI Strategy: Three Different Investment Questions](#ai-strategy-three-questions) described, is kept to test the decision, not as a forecast.

**Funding condition.** The board has approved a 2028 **cash provision**, money set aside in the plan, for supplier payments covering the forecast range, and Ines has standing permission to sign contracts of a year or less. No usage beyond 31 December 2028 is planned or funded, so a longer commitment is not on the table, as in the cloud chapter. The provision does reserve the payments that 2028 usage produces after the year ends, because usage above the covered amount is billed and paid after the fact; that tail is money for 2028 work, not a further year of usage.

| Option, monthly | Cost at €3,300 demand (low) | Cost at €5,200 demand (expected) | Cost at €7,600 demand (high) | Cost at €2,000 stress case |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| A. Stay flexible at list price | €3,300 | €5,200 | €7,600 | €2,000 |
| B. Commit €3,900 fixed, covering €5,200 of list-price usage; usage above that at list | €3,900 | €3,900 | €6,300 | €3,900 |
| C. Commit €2,400 fixed, covering €3,200 of list-price usage; usage above that at list | €2,500 | €4,400 | €6,800 | €2,400 |

Each committed option costs its fixed payment plus whatever usage exceeds the covered amount, at list price: at €7,600 of demand Option C pays €2,400 plus the €4,400 above its €3,200 allowance. Option B saves €1,300 a month at expected and high demand, costs €600 more at the low end and €1,900 more in the stress case. Option C saves €800 a month at every point in the range and costs €400 more in the stress case. **Larkspur approves Option C at the September review**, sized at the low end of the range, on one condition: the construction work must show its 35% saving on the release-gate samples and in October’s bill, the first month under the new construction. Ines signs in November, once Sam has both, so the commitment covers usage the feature will actually have. In this example the vendor holds the offer open until 30 November.

**Figure 3:** *A commitment is a floor under next year's cost. **What the contract says**, and what Sam checks before Ines signs in November. The ordinary terms mirror the cloud contract; three clauses are ones AI vendors add. Each is listed with the action Larkspur attached to it.

- **Term and payment.** 1 January to 31 December 2028; €2,400 paid monthly in advance, €28,800 in all. **Overage**, usage above the covered €3,200 a month, is invoiced on the first day of the following month and due 45 days after the invoice, that is, paid **in arrears**, after the usage. November’s overage is therefore invoiced on 1 December and due by 15 January 2029; December’s is invoiced on 1 January 2029 and due by 15 February 2029. Both are payments for 2028 usage, a tail Sam reserves inside the 2028 provision up to 15 February 2029, the latest due date; no 2029 usage is funded.
- **No early cancellation.** Leaving in month three still leaves the remaining months payable in full, even if the feature is withdrawn from sale. Reviewing, changing or withdrawing the feature never removes the remaining fixed payments. The one negotiated exception is the model-retirement right below.
- **No transfer without consent.** The agreement is the operating company’s, the business entity that signed it, and cannot be handed to another company without the vendor’s written consent, which the vendor is not obliged to give. If Larkspur is sold, the buyer inherits a company that still owes the payments.
- **Expiry.** 31 December 2028, with no automatic renewal.
- **Model retirement**, the first AI clause. The vendor may retire the model version the feature is built on with six months’ notice, and committed spend transfers to the successor model at that model’s price, so a commitment could otherwise be honoured on a model the company has not tested. Larkspur asked for, and got, three rights:
  - **Access.** Usable access to the successor for testing on the day of the notice, or within thirty days of it.
  - **Testing.** The right to run its release-gate samples on the successor before the switch.
  - **Ending.** The right to end the commitment on the retirement date in either of two cases: the samples fail, with Larkspur’s notice given within sixty days of getting access; or usable access has not arrived by the **access deadline**, sixty days before the retirement date, with Larkspur’s notice given on that deadline.
- **Model retirement, worked through.** Two dates are counted in sixty days, and they are different. The **testing window** runs for sixty days after access arrives; in it Alex runs the samples and Larkspur decides. The access deadline is fixed by the retirement date, sixty days before it, whenever access comes. The feature keeps running on the retiring model until the retirement date whatever else happens. Take a notice on 1 February 2028 retiring the model on 1 August 2028, so the access deadline is 2 June:
  - **Access by the access deadline.** Access on 1 February opens a testing window to 1 April; access on 1 April, late on the vendor’s thirty-day promise but before the deadline, opens one to 31 May. If the samples pass, the feature switches to the successor on 1 August and the commitment continues: the fixed payments run to December and cover usage at the successor’s list price less 25%. If they fail, Larkspur gives its notice within the window and the commitment ends on 1 August.
  - **No access by the access deadline.** On 2 June, with no usable access, Sam gives the notice of ending under the second trigger; Larkspur does not wait for a test it may not be able to finish. If access then arrives, say on 22 July, ten days before retirement, Alex runs the samples in the time left, so that Larkspur knows on 1 August whether the successor is usable. If access never arrives, there is nothing to test.
  - **What ending costs.** Whichever trigger applies, the fixed payments stop on the retirement date. The payment due on 1 July, for July, is the last one; nothing fixed is owed for August onward, and July’s overage is still invoiced on 1 August and due by 15 September.
  - **From the retirement date, once the commitment has ended.** Ending the commitment is a right, not a replacement model, and all usage is now at flexible list prices. Automated sorting continues only on a model that has passed the samples: the successor, if it passed after the notice was given, or another vendor’s model tested on the same samples in the meantime. A model that ran the samples and failed them can carry the **review-aid mode** described in the stop rules below, in which the planner confirms each proposed category, but only for customers who confirm the planner time for it. If no model has been measured at all, because access never came or came too late to run the samples, sorting is suspended for every customer on the retirement date, documents go back to the sorting by hand the planners did before the trial, and Priya agrees the changed service and fee with each customer, as the accuracy rule below provides.
- **Price change**, the second AI clause. List prices may change with thirty days’ notice, and the new price applies from the thirty-first day. The fixed €2,400 does not change; what changes is how many tokens it covers, because the discount is 25% off the list price on the day, so €2,400 always covers €3,200 of usage at that day’s price. If the price rises by 30%, €3,200 buys about 23% fewer tokens than before, more of the feature’s usage falls outside the allowance, and that overage is charged at the higher price: the fixed payment does not rise, total spending does. If the price falls by 20%, the same €2,400 covers 25% more tokens and less falls outside. Either way the €2,400 is owed.
- **Data**, the third AI clause. The terms on **data retention**, how long the vendor keeps customer documents, and **training use**, whether it uses them to improve its models, are unchanged from the trial and confirmed in writing. Sam reviews them against the questions posed by the generative AI profile of the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST), the United States standards body; the profile is a checklist of risks to consider for AI that generates content. [S18: NIST generative AI profile](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ai/NIST.AI.600-1.pdf)

**Why the others are rejected.** Options B and C cost the same at €4,700 of demand; B is cheaper above that, and its full extra €500 a month of saving begins at €5,200, the expected case. That extra is bought with a floor €1,500 higher, €3,900 against €2,400, in the case where the feature is losing customers, which is exactly the case in which Larkspur would want to spend less. Option A is rejected because €800 a month with no downside inside the range is real money, €9,600 over the year. A three-year offer, at a larger discount, is not compared, because no plan exists for the years it would bind.

## Set the Stop and Reprice Rules Before the Review

A feature that was worth its cost in April can stop being worth it in October for five different reasons, and the response to each is decided now, so that the October meeting applies a rule rather than invents one. The customer promise from the trial still holds and still bounds the rules: a customer keeps the feature for as long as quality holds and Ines renews the approval; nothing here promises more than that.

- **Running cost per customer above €150, half the price, for two consecutive months.** Priya first checks construction, because that is the cause Larkspur controls; if construction is already at the measured minimum, the price is changed, either a per-document allowance above which documents are charged, or a higher-priced tier for heavy users. No customer is moved to a new price without the notice the contract gives.
- **Ordinary-sample accuracy below 95%, or any safety-relevant document missed.** Both are measured every month on the trial’s two separate samples per customer, at least 200 ordinary documents drawn as they arrive and at least 30 documents the customer flagged as safety-relevant, never pooled. Exactly 95% passes; one missed safety document fails, however good the ordinary figure. Throughput, the number of documents handled in a period, and cost do not enter this rule at any value. The response that day has two branches:
  - **Planner time confirmed.** The affected customer switches to the **review-aid mode**, in which the feature proposes a category and the customer’s planner confirms it before scheduling. That mode was built for the trial’s redesign branch, is kept tested on every monthly sample, and switches on per customer without engineering work; it costs the customer more planner time and Larkspur the same tokens. Automated sorting returns only when a fresh sample passes.
  - **No planner time, or the mode unavailable for that customer.** Sorting for that customer is suspended and its documents go back to the sorting by hand the planner did before the trial. Priya agrees the changed service, and the fee for it, with the customer that day rather than assuming the customer can absorb the work.
- **Review effort at or above 1.5 hours a week for a customer, with accuracy passing.** Below 1.5 hours is the trial’s condition, so exactly 1.5 fails it. Priya checks that customer’s document mix and the construction first; if a second monthly sample is still at or above 1.5 hours, she remeasures the time the feature saves that customer against the hours its planner spent sorting by hand before the trial. The extra review is the customer’s own time, so a higher fee alone would not restore what the customer has lost. If the feature still saves the customer time, Priya agrees a revised scope with the customer, for example automating only the document types the checks pass easily, or a price that matches the measured saving; if it no longer saves time, the feature is withdrawn for that customer. The €300 promise made in the trial does not extend to a customer for whom its condition has failed.
- **A vendor list-price rise of 30% or more, or a retirement notice whose successor fails the samples or cannot be tested in time.** A decision by the end of the testing window, on the access deadline if usable access has not arrived by then, or within sixty days of a price notice, between the successor if it passed, another vendor’s model tested on the same samples, the review-aid mode on a model that has at least been measured, for customers with confirmed planner time, and suspension with an agreed service change for the rest. The retirement branches above say which of these is available on the retirement date; none of them is guaranteed by ending the commitment. For a price rise, the sixty days is Larkspur’s own decision clock, not a protection the contract gives: the fixed payment is owed throughout, and the new list price applies to overage from the thirty-first day after its notice. Only the retirement terms were negotiated: the testing window and the access deadline both fall inside the six months’ notice, and a successor that passes in time keeps the commitment running.
- **Fewer than 80% of customers renewing the feature at €300 at their annual renewal.** A reprice test with the next twenty renewals, run the way the trial tested willingness to pay, before the price is changed for everyone.

The counterargument deserves its own rule. A feature can be worth keeping at a loss for a period if it wins renewals of the main product: a customer who leaves takes €2,400 a month of scheduling revenue with them, and the sorting feature may be what keeps them. Larkspur allows exactly that, as a short sequence with a limit.

- **Eligibility.** A **loaded loss**, the loss after allocated staff time as well as supplier payments, of up to €1,000 a month on the feature is accepted, and is stated in the monthly report as a loss, not folded into a vague “platform value”.
- **Evidence.** Among customers of similar size whose main-product renewal falls between 1 October 2027 and 31 March 2028, those who have had the feature for the whole preceding quarter must renew at a rate at least ten **percentage points** above those who have never had it, for example 85% against 75%, with at least twenty renewals in each group. That comparison is a provisional reason to continue, not proof that the feature causes renewals: the customers who chose it may be the ones who would have stayed anyway.
- **Loss cap.** A month in which the loaded loss exceeds €1,000 is outside the exception; the feature is judged on its own figures for that month.
- **Expiry.** On 31 March 2028 the rule expires whatever the evidence says. If the gap is ten points or more, Priya may propose a new dated rule with a new limit; if it is smaller, or if fewer than twenty renewals fell in each group, the retention argument is unproven and the feature is judged on its own figures or repriced.

## What to Say When the Investor Asks

Morgan’s three questions, and the answers that point at the method rather than at a single number:

**“What is our AI ROI?”** For the sorting feature, a projected running return of about 90% on supplier running cost for April to September 2027, before staff costs, September estimated, with accuracy and review conditions holding; about €800 short once the engineers’ and support staff’s time is charged; and with the one-off trial and release costs not yet recovered: about €18,000 short before staff time, about €79,000 short if staff time is charged. For the support assistant, a projected 300% for 2027 on its licences and upkeep, or 220% once the €2,000 of rollout time is charged, on a hire deferred from April to October, a benefit of that year that does not recur by itself, confirmed when the year closes and only if the hire stays deferred and quality holds. Two figures, each with a period and a condition, and neither one a property of “our AI”.

**“Why is the AI bill growing faster than revenue?”** Customers doubled and the documents they send nearly tripled, from 9,000 to 26,000 a month; construction raised the model cost per document by 80% at unchanged prices, through product decisions that held quality; a 20% vendor price cut offset about 45% of that construction increase. The construction share is Larkspur’s to fix, and the fix is measured before the vendor commitment is sized.

**“Should we commit to a bigger contract?”** A one-year commitment sized at the low end of the approved demand range, after the construction work, saves €800 a month across the range and costs €400 a month more in the stress case; a larger one buys €500 more a month in the expected case with a floor €1,500 higher in the bad one; nothing longer than the year the board has funded. The obligation travels with the company, which matters if ownership changes.

Those answers are longer than a number. They are also what an investor who has read [Critically Evaluate Cloud Costs: A Lower Bill Is Not Always Better](#cheaper-cloud-bill) will recognize as the same discipline applied to a newer bill, and an adviser who wanted a **benchmark**, a comparison standard drawn from other companies, will find that the unit figures are what such a standard would need to be made of.

Larkspur’s AI is worth its cost on stated terms, for a stated period, with rules for the day it stops being so, and on one condition still open: the construction change has to deliver its estimated 35% on the release-gate samples and in October’s bill, the first month under the new construction. If it delivers, August’s figures turn from a €1,124 monthly loss after staff time to a surplus of about €914, a projected improvement of about €2,038 a month from October onward. Each such month reduces the unrecovered balances, about €18,000 before staff time and €79,000 with it; it does not clear them. If it delivers less, the demand range, the commitment and the running return are recomputed from the measured figure before Ines signs in November. The chapter [Build And Test Resilience: Backups Are Not Enough](#prove-you-can-restore) set the same shape for resilience, a saving counts only after the quality it depends on has been tested, and the chapter [Critically Evaluate Cloud Costs: A Lower Bill Is Not Always Better](#cheaper-cloud-bill) supplied the method. Part VII follows the company through funding and ownership changes, where a committed contract like this one is among the obligations the chapter [Manage the Handover: Carry Forward the Evidence and Obligations](#handover-of-obligations) carries to the next decision-maker.

## Questions to Consider

1. *For each AI feature or tool you run, what is the useful unit, and can you state its supplier cost per unit for last month on the same basis as the month before?*
2. *Of last quarter’s change in your AI bill, how much was usage, how much rates and how much construction, and who owns each part?*
3. *For the return figure you last reported, what period and what quality condition does it belong to, and did it include a one-off benefit that will not recur?*
4. *If a vendor offered you a committed-spend discount today, what demand range and what funded period would you size it against, and what do the retirement, price-change and data clauses say?*
