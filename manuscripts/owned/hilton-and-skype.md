# 31. Hilton and Skype: A Successful Exit Still Needs Explaining

![Hilton and Skype: A Successful Exit Still Needs Explaining — logo](private-techuity/posts/28-hilton-and-skype/assets/images/26-hilton-and-skype/logo.jpeg)

> **IN THIS SECTION, YOU WILL:** Learn to separate investor gains, company improvements and claims about technology’s contribution in two historical sales, and see which operating question each case asks.

> **WHY INVESTORS CARE:** An **exit** is the moment an investor sells its ownership stake in a company. Investors cite exits like these as evidence that their approach works; understanding what actually created each return is how they, and their own investors, judge whether it can be repeated.

> **WHY YOU SHOULD CARE:** Leaders are routinely asked to justify work through a future sale value; two real exits show how little the exit price alone says about what created it.

> **KEY POINTS:**
>
> * A **valuable sale needs an explanation**. Better operations, financing changes, market recovery and the buyer’s interests can all affect the result, and no two sales need the same mix of them.
> * Keep the **company result separate from the investor’s cash**, and keep an **inherited capability separate from a change the owner made**. Purchase and sale headlines do not include every investment, payment or fee, and a filing that dates a system or an acquisition does not measure what it added to the sale price.
> * Follow what happens to the **product and people**. A completed sale does not establish whether customers and employees benefited over the longer term.

Hilton operates a hotel business and brand network. Skype provided internet calling and communications software. Both were owned for a time by **private equity** investors: firms that buy companies with money pooled from outside investors, own them for some years, and then sell. Both sales were valuable, but they came by very different paths, and they need different explanations: **Hilton needs a financing explanation; Skype needs an explanation of technology rights and of what one particular buyer valued.**

Comparing them helps apply the book’s central distinction: an exit is an event to explain rather than proof that every earlier decision worked. Exits matter to product and engineering leaders because much of what investors ask for during ownership is justified by the sale they expect to make. A **sponsor** is the investment firm behind a transaction. Its account is useful evidence about its stated actions and reported result, but may emphasize favorable interpretations.

| | Hilton | Skype |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Years owned | About eleven, 2007 to 2018, through a severe downturn | About two, 2009 to 2011 |
| How the purchase was funded | Largely by borrowing; the borrowing arrangements were changed in 2010 | A mixed arrangement: an investor group bought about 70%, the seller (eBay) kept about 30% of the ownership and accepted a promise of later payment for part of the price |
| The technology question | A shared booking and hotel-operations system, OnQ, in place since 2003, before the purchase; later improvements described but not measured | Legal rights to the core calling technology, settled in 2009; the purchase of Qik, a mobile video software company, and group video calling followed in January 2011 |
| How the investors sold | In stages: Hilton’s shares returned to a stock market in 2013, but Blackstone’s funds sold none of theirs then; the funds sold their own shares later, from 2014 to 2018 | To one buyer, Microsoft, in 2011, which wanted Skype for use inside its own business |

The values behind each row are in the case sections. We examine Hilton first, then Skype, using the same questions: what changed, how might technology have helped, what did investors receive, and what remains unknown?

## Hilton: Follow the Ownership Period From 2007 to 2018

Blackstone’s 2007 acquisition of Hilton was a large **leveraged take-private**: it bought a company whose shares were traded on a stock market, took it into private ownership, and paid largely with borrowed money. A later account in Private Equity International, an industry publication, hosted by Blackstone, describes a $26 billion transaction. It then lists what followed. During the downturn came a **writedown**, meaning Blackstone lowered the value at which it carried the investment in its own books. The owner put in more money, the number of rooms expanded, and a **debt restructuring** changed the loan terms and the amounts owed. Then came a 2013 initial public offering (IPO), a sale of the company’s shares to public investors that returned them to a stock market, and the final exit in 2018. This is an industry award account and should be read as a favorable retrospective, not an independent assessment of what caused the result. [S25: PEI Hilton retrospective](https://www.blackstone.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2019/03/pei_blackstone_hilton.pdf)

The company's 2013 registration statement, the document a company files with the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) before selling shares to the public, gives a firmer basis for examining particular mechanisms. It reports that the April 2010 restructuring cut what Hilton owed by $4.0 billion. Its debt note shows how, in three steps, each with its own source of money.

- **New money from the owner.** Hilton's parent company, the company through which the Blackstone funds owned it, put in $819 million. Hilton used that money to buy back $1.8 billion of its loans from the lenders at 54 percent below the amount owed. Buying back a loan for less than the amount owed removes the whole amount owed, so $819 million of owner money removed $1.8 billion of debt.
- **Loans the owner gave up.** The parent itself held Hilton's two most junior loans, meaning the loans that would be repaid last if Hilton could not pay every lender. It cancelled them: about $2.0 billion, plus $87 million of unpaid interest. Hilton recorded that cancellation as money put in by its owner, because the owner gave up a claim it held against the company.
- **Hilton's own set-aside cash.** Hilton repaid $76 million of its main **mortgage loan**, a loan secured against its property, so the lender can take the property if the loan is not repaid. The money came from cash the loan agreement had required Hilton to keep in reserve.

Together these steps account for the $4.0 billion. The first two, about $3.9 billion of it, came from the owner's choice to keep supporting the company, with new money and by giving up loans it held, not from the hotel business itself; an owner can decline to make such a contribution. Only the last $76 million was Hilton's own cash. The reduction lowered Hilton's debt and its yearly interest cost; it was not $4.0 billion of cash available to spend on technology. [S23: Hilton registration filing](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1585689/000119312513364703/d593452ds1.htm)

The same filing explains why this chapter keeps talking about hotel owners as well as Hilton. Most hotels carrying Hilton's brands belong to other people: the filing counts 3,843 hotels owned by third parties that Hilton either manages or licenses its brands to, against 157 that Hilton itself owns or leases. Those owners pay Hilton fees for using the brand and its shared systems and, where Hilton runs the hotel, for management. It also describes three connected core pieces of its information technology. A single worldwide reservation system means every booking channel sees the same rooms and prices. A guest-profile system gives staff a guest's history and preferences. OnQ, Hilton's own system for running each hotel's daily operations, links the brands and hotels together. The filing says these systems are installed across its hotels and give Hilton and the hotel owners one shared view of the business. [S23: Hilton registration filing](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1585689/000119312513364703/d593452ds1.htm)

The 2013 public offering and Blackstone’s exit were different milestones. In the initial public offering, Hilton itself sold new shares and planned to use the money to repay part of its borrowing; the offering document states that no Blackstone fund was selling shares or receiving cash in place of selling. [S116: Hilton amended registration filing](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1585689/000119312513435529/d593452ds1a.htm) The listing made Hilton’s shares publicly traded; it did not put cash into Blackstone’s funds. Their own sales came later. In June 2014, funds affiliated with Blackstone sold 103.5 million shares for over $2.3 billion, in a sale from which Hilton received nothing, and the last shares were sold in 2018. [S117: Hilton June 2014 secondary offering](https://ir.hilton.com/news-events/investor-news/detail/394/hilton-worldwide-completes-2-3-billion-secondary-offering)

The operating observations matter because the business involved more than owning hotel buildings. Shared systems for selling rooms and running hotels could serve a wide network of hotels owned by others. The plausible technology mechanism is coordination: the same room availability and prices everywhere, usable guest information, and tools that help each hotel sell and manage its rooms. That is an interpretation of the operating design the filing describes, not a measured estimate of the value created by a specific system.

A product or engineering leader examining such a business would need to connect the shared systems to what a hotel owner earns and pays, as well as to the guest's experience. Does the service help hotels attract guests who pay well? Does it lower the cost of selling a room? Does it make the brand more valuable to an owner deciding whether to join it? Does it stay reliable as the network grows? These questions differ from asking whether the company's technology has a modern design. A central system can be valuable because it coordinates a network of hotels, even when parts of the technology are old. The same scale cuts the other way: an outage in one shared booking system could disrupt bookings across every hotel that depends on it, so recovery and fallback arrangements for that dependence need to be managed deliberately.

### What $14 Billion Does and Does Not Establish

In its second-quarter 2018 investor call, Blackstone reported completing the Hilton exit, generating approximately 3.1 times investors' capital and $14 billion of profit. A multiple of 3.1 means about $3.10 came back for every $1 invested, counting the original dollar. These are the sponsor's own figures for money received from completed sales. They are not an independent calculation of what the **limited partners (LPs)**, the outside investors whose money a fund pools and invests, received after all charges. [S24: Blackstone 2018 investor call](https://ir.blackstone.com/files/doc_events/BLACKSTONE-Second-Quarter-2018-Earnings-Investor-Call.pdf)

The reported outcome supports describing the investment as a major investor success. It doesn’t say how much of the gain came from running the hotels better, from borrowing, from the debt restructuring, from decisions to sell or reuse hotels, or from the prices buyers were paying for hotel companies by 2018. Nor does it establish what each LP finally received. Three things separate a deal's result from an investor's. The fund's management fees and **carry**, the fund manager's contractual share of the profits, reduce what investors receive. The fund's other investments, some of which may have lost money, affect the fund's overall result, because investors own a share of the whole fund rather than of Hilton alone. And the fund's agreement with its investors, not the sale of any one company, sets when money is actually paid out.

**Adjusted EBITDA** is a company-defined earnings measure. The letters stand for earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization: it leaves out borrowing costs, tax, and the accounting charges that spread the cost of buildings, equipment and software over the years they are used. "Adjusted" means the company also removes other items it specifies. It describes one way of counting earnings, not the cash left after all obligations are paid. The 2013 filing says that for the hotels Hilton itself owned or leased, as distinct from those it only managed or licensed its brands to, adjusted EBITDA over the twelve months to June 30, 2013 was still below 2008 levels. [S23: Hilton registration filing](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1585689/000119312513364703/d593452ds1.htm) This is a useful counterweight to a smooth transformation narrative. Different parts of a business can recover at different rates, even when the overall investment eventually performs well.

### Was It the Technology, the Timing, or the Terms?

A hotel business is exposed to travel demand and economic conditions. The purchase period, downturn, subsequent recovery, financing decisions and ability to remain invested all belong in the explanation. The consulted evidence doesn’t establish a **counterfactual**, a supported estimate of what Hilton would have achieved under a different owner with different financing.

Before crediting the owner with the technology, separate what the company already had from what changed. The 2013 filing describes the systems as they stood in 2013. Hilton's own earlier annual reports date the platform: the company introduced OnQ in 2003, and by December 31, 2005 it was installed at virtually all of the hotels in its system. [S64: Hilton 2003 annual filing](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/47580/000104746904007535/a2128996z10-k.htm) [S65: Hilton 2005 annual filing](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/47580/000110465906016388/a06-2283_110k.htm) Both dates precede the October 2007 closing, so Blackstone inherited OnQ rather than creating it. The 2013 management narrative says the platform was strengthened and expanded as part of a transformation it dates from 2007. It also reports **capitalized software costs**, software spending recorded as an asset and charged against earnings over later years rather than all at once, and it records some of that software as placed into use during the fourth quarter of 2012. None of these statements identifies a specific change to the inherited platform or measures its effect. The open question is therefore not whether the owner inherited the platform, which it did, but what its later work added and what that contributed to the return.

| Hilton’s technology | What the consulted sources establish |
| --- | --- |
| Inherited capability | Dated. OnQ was introduced in 2003 and installed at virtually all hotels in the system by the end of 2005, before Blackstone's 2007 acquisition. [S64: Hilton 2003 annual filing](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/47580/000104746904007535/a2128996z10-k.htm) [S65: Hilton 2005 annual filing](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/47580/000110465906016388/a06-2283_110k.htm) The 2013 filing describes OnQ, the reservation system and the guest-profile system as connected to one another and owned by Hilton. [S23: Hilton registration filing](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1585689/000119312513364703/d593452ds1.htm) |
| Documented change | A management statement that the commercial platform was strengthened and expanded during a transformation dated from 2007, plus software spending on systems projects, some of which the filing records as placed into use during the fourth quarter of 2012. That is dated evidence of software entering use. The consulted disclosure does not identify which system changed, whether the change touched the inherited OnQ platform, or what it contributed to the investment result. The filing's other dated changes are financial: the April 2010 restructuring and the $4.0 billion debt reduction. |
| Remaining uncertainty | What changed in the inherited platform during ownership, and what those changes contributed to the fees Hilton earns from hotel owners, to owners' willingness to join the brand, or to the sale value. |

The technology interpretation is therefore conditional: shared systems may help a network of hotels grow and operate, but the size of that contribution needs evidence on the cost of selling rooms, what hotel owners earned, how guests behaved and what the systems cost to build. A later statement that technology was important can’t supply that split.

The transferable lesson is the interaction of **operating capability and financial room**, meaning the money and time a company has to keep working while meeting its obligations. Technology work needs time and funding to matter. In Hilton's case the owner's 2010 contribution bought that time by cutting debt and interest. Shared systems, meanwhile, can support a way of earning money, fees from a network of hotel owners, whose value is broader than the software itself. Neither lesson licenses the assumption that a company carrying heavy debt will always receive such support through a downturn.

## Skype: Establish Rights to the Technology Before Expansion

In November 2009, eBay announced completion of a sale valuing Skype at $2.75 billion. A Silver Lake-led investor group controlled approximately 70%, while eBay retained approximately 30%. eBay reported receiving cash and a **buyer note**, a written promise by the buyer to pay part of the price later. It also bought $50 million of the **debt securities** issued to help fund the deal. A debt security is an investment that represents money lent to the issuer, so eBay became a lender as well as a seller. The transaction therefore can’t be understood simply as one buyer paying cash for 100% of a debt-free company. [S26: eBay Skype sale announcement](https://investors.ebayinc.com/investor-news/press-release-details/2009/EBay-Inc-Completes-Sale-of-Skype/default.aspx)

**Intellectual property (IP)** means legal rights to own and use things such as software and inventions. Skype's later registration statement, filed for a planned sale of its own shares to the public, describes the purchase of rights to its core **peer-to-peer** technology, the software that connects users’ computers directly to one another, from a company called Joltid in November 2009. The purchase was part of an agreement that ended a legal dispute between the two. The filing also dates later developments. Employees and long-term contractors grew from 733 at the end of 2009 to 911 at the end of 2010. Group video calling launched in January 2011. In the same month, Skype bought Qik, a mobile video software provider, for an initial cash payment of approximately $121 million. A further approximately $29 million was promised to Qik employees as cash bonuses and retention awards in return for future service. A partnership with Citrix was announced in March 2011. These are company disclosures prepared for a planned share sale; their positive interpretation remains management's. [S27: Skype registration filing](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1498209/000119312511096544/ds1a.htm)

The lesson for anyone investigating a company before investing in it, the work called **due diligence**, is unusually concrete. A product can work well for customers while the legal rights to technology it cannot run without belong to someone else. Reviewing the quality of the software, or testing whether it copes with more users, wouldn’t by itself uncover that risk.

| Skype’s technology | What the consulted sources establish |
| --- | --- |
| Inherited capability | A large, functioning communications product whose core peer-to-peer technology rights were held by Joltid and the subject of a legal dispute at the time of the 2009 sale. [S27: Skype registration filing](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1498209/000119312511096544/ds1a.htm) |
| Documented change | Dated: the November 2009 settlement that brought the peer-to-peer rights into the company; growth in employees and long-term contractors from 733 to 911 during 2010; the January 2011 launch of group video calling; the January 2011 Qik acquisition; the March 2011 Citrix partnership announcement. [S27: Skype registration filing](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1498209/000119312511096544/ds1a.htm) A date places each event inside the ownership window; it does not isolate that event's contribution to the sale price. |
| Remaining uncertainty | How much of the later purchase price the settlement, the product work, market growth and Microsoft’s own uses each account for. |

### What Changed in the Product and Business

The filing reports 2010 **revenue**, the income from selling services before any costs are deducted, of about $860 million, adjusted EBITDA of about $264 million, and a **net loss**, the bottom-line result after every cost is counted, of about $7 million. The two earnings figures can point in opposite directions because adjusted EBITDA leaves out the interest on the borrowing used to buy Skype and the accounting charges for the assets that came with the purchase, both of which the net figure includes. The filing describes ambitions to grow paid use, win more business customers and find new ways to earn money. These measures and plans shouldn’t be confused: a plan isn’t an achieved outcome, and adjusted EBITDA isn’t the bottom line. [S27: Skype registration filing](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1498209/000119312511096544/ds1a.htm)

For technology leaders, the interesting mechanism is the combination of a large communications product, new ways of earning money being tried, and a more secure ownership of its core technology. The public record supports those components. It doesn’t establish the extra revenue any particular engineering change produced, or the share of the later purchase price that the IP settlement accounts for. Nor should all improvement after Skype left eBay be credited to its standing alone: market growth, users accumulated earlier, management changes, partnerships and acquisitions can all contribute, and there is no way to know how Skype would have done under eBay or another owner.

### A Completed Sale to a Strategic Buyer

Microsoft announced the $8.5 billion acquisition agreement in May 2011 and announced completion in October 2011. It said it wanted Skype to work with its own communications products and devices. This makes Microsoft a **strategic buyer**, one that values a company for what it can do inside the buyer's existing business. The announcement is evidence of a completed transaction and of the buyer's stated reasons, not evidence that the combined businesses later produced all the extra sales and savings Microsoft hoped for. [S28: Microsoft Skype completion](https://news.microsoft.com/source/2011/10/13/microsoft-officially-welcomes-skype/)

Dividing the $8.5 billion sale price by the $2.75 billion at which Skype was valued in 2009 doesn’t calculate Silver Lake's fund return. Both are values for the whole company at two dates. Silver Lake's fund owned only part of it, lenders and other financiers had to be repaid before owners, the deal had costs, and money went in and came out at different times. A proper calculation would need each of those amounts and its date. The reported sale clearly shows that the company's value rose a great deal and that the sellers received cash, while the exact return after all deductions remains outside this case's verified evidence.

{id: hilton-and-skype--the-afterlife-and-its-limits}
### The Afterlife, and Its Limits

Microsoft retired Skype in May 2025 and directed users toward alternatives including Teams Free. [S29: Microsoft Skype retirement](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/skype/22ccebb6-0cf9-4e1d-916f-aaf978e1b129) That event belongs in the product's history. It doesn’t retroactively prove that the 2009–2011 ownership period destroyed value, nor does the successful 2011 sale prove that users were served well indefinitely afterward. The long interval includes subsequent ownership choices and changing communications markets; a causal account of the retirement would need to study that later period directly. Here, the event shows why the moment an investor turns its stake into cash and the product's longer life need to be judged over different periods. For customers, having to move to another product is a real consequence even when the buyer considers folding one product into another sensible.

## Two Operating Questions

The documented Skype sequence runs from a corporate owner to an investor group and then to Microsoft. For a company leader, that is a reason to check again, at each sale, who has authority to decide, who holds the rights to essential technology, and what the new owner intends the product to be for. The next buyer’s strategic interest need not match the selling investor’s reason for owning it.

Hilton supplies a different question: what happens to the operating plan when financing and the time needed for recovery change? The case supports examining those dependencies; it doesn’t tell a young company backed by start-up investors how to fund its next stage.

![Hilton prompts separate questions about operations and financing; Skype adds technology rights and the product’s life across owners.](private-techuity/posts/28-hilton-and-skype/assets/images/26-hilton-and-skype/two-histories-two-operating-questions.jpeg)

**Figure 1:** *Use each history to identify a specific operating question before transferring its lesson.*

The lessons are mechanisms to investigate, not instructions to imitate. Both cases show that technology becomes investment-relevant through its relationship to business capability, ownership rights, financing and a buyer's needs. Use the histories to formulate questions for the current owner: **which capability must continue through the transaction**, which assumption has changed, and **who funds the unfinished work**?

![A sale produces an investor payment record while product, customer and employee outcomes continue afterward.](private-techuity/posts/28-hilton-and-skype/assets/images/26-hilton-and-skype/sale-result-and-later-outcomes.jpeg)

**Figure 2:** *An investor’s result from a completed sale and the company’s longer operating history need separate evidence.*

One evidence limit is common to both cases. The consulted material dates the technology events: OnQ before the Hilton buyout, the rights settlement and the 2011 product and acquisition steps inside the Skype window. It does not contain complete fund cash flows, an independent allocation of either gain among operating, financing, market and buyer effects, or a comparison of employee and customer outcomes across the ownership periods. A sponsor's reported multiple and the headline transaction values are the start of the explanation, not its end.

**Hilton asks whether the company has financing and time for the operating plan. Skype asks whether it controls the rights the product depends on and what a particular buyer values. Neither answer can be read from the exit price alone.**

Both cases end with a completed sale. The next case asks what a leader should recheck when the sale is a secondary one, meaning one investor sells its stake to another investor, and the familiar investment manager stays while the money behind it changes: [Visma: Continuity of Manager Is Not Continuity of Money](#visma).

{id: hilton-and-skype--questions-to-consider}
## Questions to Consider

1. *When a sponsor describes a successful exit, how much of the result can you explain by operating improvement, financing changes, market recovery and the buyer’s own interests?*
2. *Does your product depend on technology rights that a code review would not examine? How do you know?*
3. *What financial room would your company have to keep investing in technology through a severe downturn, and who would supply it?*
4. *Which capability in your company must continue through a change of owner, and who would fund the unfinished work?*
