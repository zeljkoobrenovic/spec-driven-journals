# 24. Plan for Growth: Decide What (Not) to Change in Your Systems

![Plan for Growth: Decide What (Not) to Change in Your Systems — logo](private-techuity/posts/23-growth-into-design/assets/images/23-growth-into-design/logo.jpeg)

> **IN THIS SECTION, YOU WILL:** Learn to recover the business requirement behind a request for flexibility, compare implementation options against it and choose one with its funded transition.

> **WHY INVESTORS CARE:** Growth is the assumption the valuation rests on; investors need the design choice that makes it possible to be funded, dated and reversible, not a request for flexibility with no cost attached.

> **WHY YOU SHOULD CARE:** “Make it more flexible” funds nothing on its own; a design chosen without the recovered business requirement is either over-built or wrong, and both cost cash before benefits arrive.

> **KEY POINTS:**
>
> * An investor’s growth assumption reaches the team as a request for flexibility. **Recover the business requirement** before comparing designs: which changes must become easier, for which customers, by when.
> * Compare **options against that requirement**, not against each other’s fashion: the same customer need, delivery date, ongoing responsibility and cash limit. Choose one under stated assumptions and name the evidence that would reverse it.
> * Follow **spending and benefits through time**. An attractive future saving still needs funding before it arrives, and a design that adds operating responsibility adds a cost line the next review will question.

Larkspur is a fictional company that sells scheduling software to business customers, and an investor owns a large share of it. Suppose that investor expects Larkspur to grow quickly, and the technology team is asked to make the software “more flexible.” The request leaves out its reasoning, and that is the condition this chapter starts from. Any company translates financial expectations into technical work; what is particular here is that the assumption arrived as a demand, with the customers, dates and constraints behind it left with the investor. The leader’s task is to recover that reasoning, which changes must become easier for the growth to happen, and turn it into an implementation choice the company can fund.

A **valuation assumption** is a belief used when estimating what a business is worth, such as an expectation of future sales or profit. An **operating requirement** states what the company must do to make that belief plausible. An **implementation choice** is how the company meets that requirement in its technology: how the systems are structured, what is built, what is bought, and how the parts fit together. The examples below come mostly from software a company builds; the same reasoning applies across the landscape of systems, suppliers and integrations the company runs, when the choice is which supplier system to adopt or how deeply to integrate it.

The boundary with two Part IV and Part V chapters is deliberate. The chapter [Assess Capability: Can the Team Deliver?](#can-the-team-deliver) produced the assessment this chapter uses. Its finding for fictional Larkspur’s second country was: *market entry depends on coordinated changes to billing, legal work, support and product configuration, because the country rules are coupled into the invoicing module that all four read; every engineering change to those rules passes through the same two specialists and takes about three weeks end to end.* The chapter [Scale the Team Up: Headcount Is Not Capacity](#fix-decisions-before-hiring) dealt with the organizational half: delegation removed the decision waits, and one billing engineer is being brought into the module. This chapter takes the system half and chooses. It does not repeat the assessment. One word in that finding carries the whole problem: the rules are **coupled** into the module, meaning they are written into the same body of code as everything else it does, so a change meant for one country can disturb work that has nothing to do with it — which is why only two people are trusted to make one.

## Recover the Requirement

“We are valued on growth” is incomplete. Growth in which customers, products or markets? How many will stay, what will it cost to serve them, and what further investment is required?

“We are valued on **EBITDA**” is also incomplete, and the abbreviation hides more than it says. EBITDA stands for *earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization*. Earnings are what is left of sales income after the costs of running the business; this particular measure then leaves four things out. **Interest** is the cost of borrowing money. **Taxes** are what is owed to governments. **Depreciation** spreads the cost of a physical asset, a building or a server, across the years it is used, and **amortization** does the same for something you cannot touch, such as purchased software. Leaving all four out gives a number meant to describe the trading business on its own, before how it is financed and how its past purchases are accounted for. It is an estimate of profitability, not a count of money in the bank: a company can report healthy EBITDA in a year when its cash falls. Which year’s earnings, under which adjustments, and how will the company sustain them? The chapter [Understand Valuation: An Estimate, Not a Fact](#valuation-is-an-estimate) introduces the terms.

If much of the valuation depends on expansion, management may weight learning quickly, entering markets and onboarding customers, and may accept lower current earnings to build those capabilities if the cost, funding and evidence justify it. If much of it depends on repeatable earnings, management may weight cost to serve, reliable operations and predictable investment. Neither removes the other: a growth plan with worsening cost per customer needs cost work, and an earnings-focused company whose product is becoming obsolete needs experimentation. The useful question is which business uncertainty or constraint matters most right now.

For Larkspur, recovered from the investor’s reasoning, the requirement is: invoice, contract with, support and **onboard** customers — get them set up and using the service — in a second country under that country’s tax and pricing rules, with **first paying customers within twelve months**; and, because the investor’s growth plan and the assumptions behind it also assume a third country within two years, adding a country must become cheaper each time. That is what “more flexible” meant.

Read the twelve-month milestone precisely, because four different events are easy to blur. A customer *commits* when it signs. The company *goes live* when the customer can actually use the service. It *invoices* when it asks for the money, and it is *paid* when the money arrives. The board's requirement is the last of those. Larkspur's standard contract for these customers invoices in the month the service starts, on thirty-day payment terms, so the money arrives about a month after the first invoice. The option table below gives each design's **first invoice** date, because that is what the engineering work determines; add roughly one month to reach first cash. Month eight invoiced means paid in month nine. It is that payment, not the invoice, that has to fall inside twelve months, and the margin in the comparison is measured against it.

## What Each Priority Asks of the Design

A valuation method doesn’t dictate an implementation. The overview below names, for each business priority, the trade-off the design has to face. Two shorter tables then separate the questions for a system the company builds from those for one it buys; a common arrangement is a bought core with built extensions around it, so many readers will need both.

| Business priority implied by the investment plan | Trade-off to evaluate |
| --- | --- |
| Learn which products or markets can grow | Flexibility costs effort; elaborate computing systems underneath can slow the learning they were meant to enable. |
| Serve more customers without proportional cost growth | Sharing computing resources between customers may lower the cost per customer while increasing coordination, or making one customer’s problem reach another. |
| Improve sustainable earnings and cash generation | Savings depend on a completed transition; cutting the ability to withstand and recover from failures, or cutting development, can damage future earnings. |
| Combine businesses the company buys, or prepare to sell part of it off | Connecting systems together can improve the customer offer but reduce local flexibility and complicate a later separation. |
| Reduce dependence on a single supplier or person | Compare the dependency’s possible impact and likelihood with the cost and effectiveness of the alternatives; include consequences the company cannot accept. [Build And Test Resilience: Backups Are Not Enough](#prove-you-can-restore) shows why a rare failure can still dominate a decision. |

**If the company builds the system.** A few terms first. **Modularity** means dividing software into parts with clear responsibilities and connections. **Feature flags** let a team enable or disable selected behavior without releasing a new version each time. **Tenant isolation** keeps different customers’ data or workloads appropriately separated in a shared service. **Interfaces** are the agreed ways software parts exchange information.

| Business priority | What to examine in a built system |
| --- | --- |
| Learn what can grow | Isolated changes, configurable workflows, feature flags, releasing new versions to customers reliably, and measuring what the experiments show |
| Serve more customers | Automated onboarding, keeping enough computing resources available for demand, appropriate tenant isolation and cost visibility |
| Improve earnings and cash | Remove duplicate systems, simplify operations, automate repetitive work, switch off computing systems nobody uses |
| Combine or separate | Clear product boundaries, reliable interfaces, data that can be moved out in a usable form, and a written list of what the shared services depend on |
| Reduce a dependency | Spread knowledge beyond the one or two people who hold it |

**If the company buys the system.** **Configuration** changes behavior through settings rather than by changing the software itself. **Customization** means altering a supplier’s product beyond its intended settings, which often makes later upgrades harder. **Exit cost** is what the company would spend to move to another supplier, including moving its data and retraining its staff.

| Business priority | What to examine in a bought system |
| --- | --- |
| Learn what can grow | How much can be changed through settings without specially written code, whether there is a **sandbox** — a separate copy of the system to try changes in, where mistakes reach no real customer or record — and how quickly the supplier delivers a change you need; customizing to gain flexibility can remove it at the next upgrade |
| Serve more customers | How licence fees and usage-based charges grow as customers are added; a price charged per user or per transaction can eat into the **margin** the growth plan assumes — the share of each sale left after the costs of delivering it |
| Improve earnings and cash | Consolidate overlapping products, renegotiate at renewal, retire licences still being paid for; a negotiated discount can be reversed at the next renewal |
| Combine or separate | Whether licences transfer on a sale, whether data can be exported in usable form, and which contracts bind the whole group |
| Reduce a dependency | Know the exit cost, the **notice period** — how long a contract requires you to warn the supplier before ending it, which sets how soon you could leave, not how well the supplier will perform meanwhile — and whether an equivalent supplier exists |

A growth-oriented company might need modular boundaries because teams must change a few parts of the product independently. To **release** software is to make a new version available for customers to use. **Microservices** are smaller services that are released and run separately from one another. Modular boundaries don’t automatically require them. A modular application released as one piece can be cheaper and easier for its team to operate; separately released services become an option when their specific independence is worth the extra operating work.

A company that buys most of its systems faces the same question in a different form: flexibility comes from staying close to the supplier’s intended use, so upgrades stay routine, and from keeping its own distinctive work in parts it controls. Heavy customization loses both, and the knowledge of why the product was changed leaves with the people who changed it. Equally, a blanket preference for the lowest immediate cost is not an earnings strategy. A reliable **managed service**, operated by a supplier on the company’s behalf, can cost more on an invoice while reducing the total work of operating the product; a commitment that lowers this year’s price for the computers and services running the software may limit the ability to shrink or change later.

## Three Options for One Requirement

Alex, who leads Larkspur’s technology, and Priya, who leads its product, compare three ways to meet the second-country requirement. Sam leads its finance, and Ines is its chief executive, the person the board holds responsible for running the company. Every figure below is fictional.

**The money.** Sam has confirmed a limit of €300,000 of additional cash this year for the expansion’s technical work. “Additional” means on top of what Larkspur already spends: the billing engineer hired in the chapter [Scale the Team Up: Headcount Is Not Capacity](#fix-decisions-before-hiring), about €90,000 a year, is needed under every option and is already paid for out of the budget for running the business, so his salary is not counted again here. What differs between the options is how much of his time each one consumes.

**The people.** The scarce capacity is the two invoicing specialists, the only people who can safely change the module today. Effort is counted in **engineer-weeks**: one person working for one week. “Six specialist-weeks” therefore means six weeks of specialist effort in total, three from each of the two, spread across the work — not six weeks on the calendar. Elapsed time and effort are different quantities throughout this table.

**The clock and the basis.** The months count from the board’s decision to fund the expansion. Costs are compared on one basis: additional cash in the first year, then the recurring cost in each year after it.

> **A note on which scenario this is.** This is a separate, later scenario from the hundred-day plan in the chapter [Plan the First Hundred Days: Turn Expectations Into Funded Work](#first-hundred-days). That plan’s €500,000 envelope deferred the expansion beyond its first year, and the €300,000 appearing in it was the annual cost of two deferred hires — a different figure that happens to match. The €300,000 here is a fresh envelope for the expansion, approved once the board has taken it up. The two scenarios are not to be added together.

| | A. Configure a supplier | B. Own the boundary | C. Replace the core |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| What it is | Buy a ready-made tax-and-billing service for the second country, set up for its rules, and feed it the invoice data the existing module already produces | Separate the country-specific billing rules out of the invoicing module into a part Larkspur builds and maintains itself, with a defined connection to the rest; move the first country onto it, then add the second by changing settings | Replace the billing system underneath everything, so that every country’s rules become settings rather than code, at the cost already estimated in the assessment |
| First second-country invoice (first payment about a month later) | Month 4–5 | Month 8 | About month 12 |
| Additional cash, first year | €100,000: €60,000 setup plus the first €40,000 annual subscription, billed at go-live | €140,000: a contractor covering the specialists’ product work during the extraction, plus test tooling | €1.3m over eighteen months, roughly €720,000 of it in the first twelve months (twelve months of the four-person team and half of the parallel running — operating the old and new systems side by side — costed in [Assess Capability: Can the Team Deliver?](#can-the-team-deliver), before the cost of moving customers and their data across) |
| Recurring cost and responsibility after the first year | €40,000 a year per country, rising with invoice volume; the supplier maintains tax rules, Larkspur maintains the integration, and finance has to reconcile two billing routes each month — compare the two sets of records and explain any differences — which costs staff time rather than appearing as a payment | No supplier fee; the billing engineer maintains the country-rules component within his already funded role, and each further country is configuration | Larkspur operates a new core; the maintenance saving arrives only when the old one is retired |
| Engineer-weeks and scarce capacity | 8, of which two are specialist-weeks | 16: six specialist-weeks, three from each, and ten from the billing engineer | Four people for eighteen months; the specialists throughout |
| What it preserves or sacrifices | Fastest and cheapest now; leaves the tangle in place and adds a second route to billing for every country | Touches the fragile module, so existing invoices carry **regression risk**: the danger that a change breaks something that already worked. Removes the tangle for every later country | Broadest capability; far exceeds the cash limit, its first payment would land after month twelve, and it is still unclear when the old system could be switched off |

On that basis B costs **€80,000 more upfront** (€140,000 against A’s €60,000 setup) and €40,000 more in the first year (€140,000 against €100,000). From the second year B adds **no further supplier subscription**, while A adds €40,000 every year; B’s upkeep is the billing engineer maintaining the component inside the salary Larkspur already pays, which is an existing budget line rather than a cost that disappears. Counting only the additional cash, A reaches €140,000 after two subscription years — exactly level with B — and €180,000 after three; a third country on A adds its own setup and another €40,000 a year. So B is the cheaper design only if Larkspur keeps it beyond the second year or the third country arrives. Both are assumptions, and the record below says so.

They choose **B**, under three explicit assumptions. First, the design will be kept in use past the two-year crossover. That is the reason B is cheaper, and it stands on its own: Larkspur’s investor expects to hold its ownership share for about four more years — the **holding period**, the number of years an investor expects to own its share before selling — so on a four-year horizon A’s subscription reaches €220,000 against B’s €140,000, with no third country needed to get there. The third country in the investor’s plan is a separate, reinforcing reason rather than the deciding one: if it arrives, it adds A’s setup fee and another €40,000 a year, bringing B’s advantage forward; if it never arrives, B is still the cheaper design from the third year on. Second, the six specialist-weeks are available, which is true only because the delegation changes in the previous chapter hold. Third, last year’s invoices can be **replayed** through the separated rules — rerun through the new code and compared line by line against the bills Larkspur actually sent — so that any difference is found in testing rather than by a customer.

Rejected: A, because it meets the date but leaves the constraint that made expansion hard in the first place and adds a reconciliation burden the growth plan would multiply; it is held as the fallback, priced at €60,000 setup plus €40,000 a year, not at €60,000. C, for the reasons in the table; it would be reconsidered only if a third country and larger customers made the whole core the constraint rather than the country rules.

**Funding.** €140,000 of the €300,000 approved budget. The €160,000 left over is not an estimate of what the fallback would cost; it is the money still available, enough to pay for the fallback’s first year if it is ever needed.

**Scarce capacity.** Six specialist-weeks, protected in the plan.

**Authority.** The Larkspur board approved the budget. Ines, as chief executive, authorizes the choice within it, on Alex and Priya’s recommendation. Alex is answerable for delivering the work; Priya is answerable for the country requirement actually being met, including the legal and support work engineering cannot do.

**Evidence that would change the decision.** Three signals are written down in advance, and each justifies a different response — switching supplier, delaying a launch, or reopening the comparison. They are not interchangeable.

1. *The replay fails.* If by month four the replayed invoices show differences the specialists cannot explain within two weeks, or incidents have eaten the protected specialist time, Larkspur **switches to A** for the second country and stops B in the state described below.
2. *Demand does not appear.* If fewer than five customers have signed in the second country by month six, the **go-live is deferred** — the second country waits for evidence of demand — while the boundary work continues for the first country, where its benefit does not depend on the second country at all. This delays a launch; it does not change the design.
3. *The third country leaves the plan.* Then the **decision is reassessed**, not automatically reversed. Losing the third country removes one of the two reasons for B, not the main one: the expected years of use still carry the case on their own. It weakens the margin rather than reversing the answer, and when that news arrives matters more than the news itself. The review compares the money still to be spent, the delivery risk still ahead, the specialist time still required and the number of years the design is expected to be used — and records its answer with the date it was taken. Three moments give three different answers.

**Before any money is spent.** How long Larkspur expects to use the design decides this, and with only two countries the two options cross over quickly. A costs €100,000 in the first year against B’s €140,000, and adds €40,000 in each year after. So A is cheaper if the design is kept for one subscription year, level with B at two years (€140,000 each), and dearer from the third (€180,000 against €140,000). Under a single year of expected use A is clearly the better answer; at two years the cash is a tie, and A wins the tie-break on the other grounds, because it also reaches the date earlier and avoids B’s particular regression risk: it never reaches into the fragile module to pull the country rules out, so it cannot break a first-country invoice that way. That is a lower risk, not an absent one — connecting a new billing service still has to be proved not to disturb what already works, which is why A carries its own two specialist-weeks to check the invoice data it hands the supplier. An earlier launch or a shortened expected life both argue for it. Past two years the ranking turns over, so the honest form of the question is not “which is better?” but “how many years do we expect to run this, can we deliver B on the date, and do we have the specialist weeks?”

**After €80,000 has been spent.** The comparison is now between the €60,000 needed to finish B and the €100,000 needed to start A, because the €80,000 is gone under either choice and should not tip the decision either way. Finishing B is cheaper from the first year on any horizon, and with no further supplier subscription it stays cheaper for as long as Larkspur keeps it.

**After B is live.** Switching means paying to replace a system that works, so the bar is higher again: A would have to solve a problem B actually has, not merely have been the cheaper start.

The first trigger needs its state spelled out, because calling something a “safe intermediate state” cannot make the half-built path safe. Take the steps one at a time.

**What stays live.** Until the separated component passes the invoice replay, **the existing invoicing module remains the authoritative one** — the approved source of every bill a first-country customer receives. The new path runs only in replay, against copies. No customer is billed from it.

**What stops.** If the replay fails at month four, the component stays switched off, the extraction stops, and whatever did pass is kept as documented work rather than as a running system.

**What the money looks like at that point.** About €80,000 of B’s €140,000 has been spent: four months of the contractor, plus the test tooling bought early because the replay needs it. That money does not come back. The remaining €60,000 is not spent, because the contractor stops.

**What the fallback then costs.** A’s €60,000 setup plus €40,000 a year, shown as two separate lines rather than one. Ines can authorize it from the €220,000 of the budget still unspent, because the fallback and its trigger were part of the decision the board approved, and she reports the switch at the next board review. After A’s first year, €120,000 of the budget would remain.

**Who does the work.** Cash is not the binding constraint here; people are. A needs eight engineer-weeks, two of them specialist-weeks, and the trigger that invokes it may be the very thing that removed them. So the fallback is staffed explicitly. Six of the eight weeks come from the billing engineer, whose ten weeks of B work end when the extraction stops, and who is the right person to own the integration afterwards. The supplier’s own implementation team does the configuration of its product under a fixed-price setup fee. That leaves the two specialist-weeks, needed to confirm that the invoice data handed to the supplier is correct. Those are protected in the same way the six were: booked as named days in the specialists’ calendars at the moment the switch is authorized, with Ines reassigning the incident cover that would otherwise consume them.

**What if that protection fails.** If the switch was triggered by incidents eating specialist time, and those incidents are still running, the two specialist-weeks cannot simply be re-promised. Then Larkspur has two honest options, and must pick one at the review rather than assume the date holds: defer the second-country launch and tell the board the twelve-month milestone will be missed, or fund an incident response separately — contracted operational cover, a decision with its own cost, outside this €300,000 budget — so the specialists are genuinely free. Announcing the fallback without settling this is how a plan acquires a date nobody owns.

**Whether the date still works.** Narrowly, and only if the specialists are available. The sum has four terms: the switch is decided at month four and a half, at the end of the two-week explanation window; then Larkspur waits for the supplier to start; then the supplier's setup takes four to five months; then payment follows the first invoice by about a month. So first cash lands at month 4.5 + wait + setup + 1.

Larkspur plans on a one-month wait. That gives first cash in month ten and a half if setup runs four months, or month eleven and a half if it runs five — inside the twelve-month payment milestone, but by only half a month to a month and a half, and with no room for a second false start.

The one-month wait is the planning allowance, chosen to keep that margin, not the arithmetic limit. The limit depends on how long setup takes, and Larkspur will not know that when it has to commit. Against a five-month setup the longest workable wait is a month and a half, which reaches payment in month twelve exactly; against a four-month setup it is two and a half months, likewise landing on month twelve. Planning on the longer wait means betting on the shorter setup. Past those limits, or if the specialist time is not free, the date is gone and deferral is the truthful answer.

## A Bounded Illustration: Paying for a Saving

The choice above buys capability, not a cash saving. A **separate payback example** shows how a saving is financed, and it should not be added to the decision above. Assume one proposed change at Larkspur costs €200,000 in cash now and is expected to avoid €100,000 of annual external setup costs after a one-year implementation. Assume those supplier payments really can be avoided and the €100,000 is the annual cash saving after any added operating and maintenance costs. The €180,000 pilot in the chapter [Test Revenue Assumptions: Do Customers Respond as Expected?](#roadmap-to-revenue) illustrated staff capacity; this example illustrates cash savings. The two shouldn’t be added together either.

An earnings discussion examines the recurring cost reduction and its effect on the relevant earnings measure. A cash discussion must include the initial payment, the year’s wait and the timing of savings. A growth discussion asks whether easier onboarding also removes a constraint on selling and serving more customers. An implementation discussion asks which configuration or integration boundary would deliver the improvement without a much larger rewrite. These are complementary views of one proposal.

Follow the cash. The €200,000 is paid now. The implementation takes a year, so the first €100,000 saving arrives during the second year, not immediately after approval. Adding the savings up as they come, €100,000 and then €100,000, recovers the €200,000 after two full years of savings — about three years after the money went out. That is a **simple payback** calculation: how long until what you get back adds up to what you paid. It deliberately ignores tax, uncertainty, timing within each year, and **discounting**, the practice of treating money arriving in three years as worth less today than the same amount now. Simple payback adds future euros at face value, which makes it easy to explain and slightly flattering to slow projects.

There is a second, larger-sounding number to be careful with. A **valuation multiple** is a ratio used to estimate what a business is worth: take a financial measure, such as one year’s EBITDA, and multiply it. “10×” means ten times that measure. Work the example through a step at a time. The saving raises annual EBITDA by €100,000, because €100,000 of yearly cost disappears. If the business is valued at ten times its EBITDA, and that multiple does not change, then ten times €100,000 gives **€1 million of additional enterprise value**. **Enterprise value** is an estimate of what the whole operating business is worth, counting the claims of both its lenders and its owners; it is not cash anyone receives, and it is not the same as the value of the owners' shares.

Four assumptions sit inside that single multiplication, and each can fail: that the saving really continues year after year, that the EBITDA definition the buyer uses actually reflects it, that the multiple stays at ten, and that nothing else moves in the opposite direction. So €1 million is a **sensitivity calculation** — an illustration of how the estimate moves when one input moves — and not a demonstration that the project is worth a million euros. Meanwhile the €200,000 spent is real cash leaving the company, which may also raise its **net debt**, what it owes lenders after subtracting the cash it holds. Spending cash to buy an estimated gain is a genuine trade, not a free one.

One trap follows directly. **Present value** is what a future sum is worth today, reduced to allow for waiting and for the risk it never arrives. You may value the saving by taking the present value of those future €100,000s, or you may value it through the €1 million multiple — but not both added together. They are two ways of pricing the same saving, and summing them counts one benefit twice.

If Larkspur can’t fund the first year, the project may be **economically attractive and currently infeasible**. It could phase the work, seek funding or choose another intervention. Valuation doesn’t remove the financing constraint.

## Other Owners, Other Hypotheses

The same discipline applies when the expectation comes from a different owner. A **corporate parent** — a larger company that owns Larkspur outright — may value using Larkspur’s product across its own customer base; the implementation question becomes which connections and operating responsibilities enable that use, with a named sponsor and budget inside the parent. A **buyout**, where an investor buys a large enough ownership share to control the company, often using borrowed money, usually emphasizes generating cash to pay the interest on that borrowing and repay the loans; the case then needs the transition costs and the time before any saving actually reaches the bank account. A new **funding round**, a fresh effort to raise investment money, may require evidence that an opportunity is worth developing further, without funding the whole of it today — and a prospect of funding is not money received. None of these assigns a guaranteed lift in business value to a technical feature: **show the technical evidence separately from the financial inference**, and say which funding or commercial commitment must arrive before the next design stage is justified.

## The Decision Record

Four people record five answers together, drawing on the investor’s adviser where useful: the chief executive (Ines, who runs the company), the chief financial officer (Sam, who runs its finances), the product leader (Priya) and the chief technology officer (Alex, who runs its technology). For Larkspur’s second country they read:

1. **The value assumption:** the investor's growth plan assumes a second country within twelve months and a third within two years; each country’s signed customers and revenue are the assumption’s test.
2. **The operating requirement:** invoice, contract with, support and onboard customers under the second country’s tax and pricing rules, with the **first customer payments received by month twelve**, and each further country cheaper to add than the last.
3. **The technical options**, dates given as first invoice with payment about a month later: configure a supplier (invoice month four to five, €60,000 setup plus €40,000 a year per country, leaves the country rules tangled into the module); own the boundary (invoice month eight, €140,000 once and no supplier fee afterwards, though upkeep still consumes the billing engineer’s already funded time; untangles the rules, risks breaking existing invoices); replace the core (broadest capability, €1.3 million, far beyond the cash limit, and its first payment would arrive after month twelve).
4. **The funded transition:** option B, €140,000 of the €300,000 approved budget in the first year, sixteen engineer-weeks including six protected specialist-weeks, maintenance inside the billing engineer’s already funded role, first second-country invoice in month eight and first payment in month nine. Option A is held as the fallback at €60,000 setup plus €40,000 a year, funded from the unspent budget and staffed by the billing engineer plus two protected specialist-weeks; if that specialist time cannot be freed, the launch is deferred rather than the date assumed.
5. **The review evidence:** the month-four replay of last year’s invoices; five signed customer commitments by month six; the month-eight go-live and month-nine first payment; and the three triggers above, each tied to the one response it justifies — switch supplier, defer the launch, or reassess the comparison.

Then test the record against three changed conditions, taking each on its own and reassessing the remaining costs and benefits rather than defending the original choice.

**Slower growth, with no third country.** A would have met the requirement, and more cheaply in the first year. B’s €80,000 of extra upfront spending is recovered only if the design stays in use beyond the second year, the point at which A’s repeating subscription would have overtaken it. What remains of B’s benefit is faster rule changes for two countries — that is **staff time freed for other work**, which has to be measured the way the chapter [Test Revenue Assumptions: Do Customers Respond as Expected?](#roadmap-to-revenue) measures its pilot, and not reported to the board as a saving proving the money was not wasted. The extra cash was spent on a stated assumption, and the record says which one.

**A lower sale valuation.** Nothing in the design changes. The choice was made against a customer requirement and a delivery date, not against a multiple, so a change in what the company might sell for does not reach it.

**A longer ownership period.** More of C’s later benefit — the maintenance saving that only begins once the old core is switched off — now falls inside the years the investor expects to hold its ownership share, which changes the benefit side of any future comparison. It does not change C’s €1.3 million cash requirement against a €300,000 budget. Affordability and attractiveness are separate questions: C becomes a candidate only if a financing decision of the kind compared in the chapter [Understand Funding Choices: Match the Money to the Work](#raise-what-you-need) actually supplies that cash.

Option B leaves Larkspur operating the country rules itself: no supplier fee, but the billing engineer’s time, the protected specialist time and the test tooling are now committed inside the operating cost, and the next visible line an investor will question is the hosting bill — what Larkspur pays for the computers and services that run its software — because it is large, adjustable and improves the earnings measure directly. The chapter [Critically Evaluate Cloud Costs: A Lower Bill Is Not Always Better](#cheaper-cloud-bill) shows how to tell whether a lower bill reflects a real improvement or a worse unit of service.

## Questions to Consider

1. *What does “more flexible” or “lower cost to serve” mean in your company’s case: which changes must become easier, for which customers, by when, for the valuation assumption to hold?*
2. *For your most significant proposal, have you compared at least two designs against the same need, date, ongoing responsibility and cash limit, and written down what would reverse the choice?*
3. *Are the systems your company buys being customized in ways that trade upgrade flexibility for short-term convenience?*
4. *Would your chosen design still be right under slower growth, a lower sale valuation and a longer ownership period, and which of those changes the answer?*

## To Probe Further

- **[Architecture: Selling Options](https://architectelevator.com/architecture/architecture-options/)** — Gregor Hohpe, The Architect Elevator, 2016.  
  *Treats flexibility as an option in the financial sense — a right to do something later without being obliged to — bought at a price today. That gives you the language to explain to a board why modularity costs money now and what the company gets for it.*
- **[The Promise and Peril of Real Options](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/pdfiles/papers/realopt.pdf)** — Aswath Damodaran, NYU Stern, working paper.  
  *The finance-side check on that options metaphor, setting out the conditions a “real option” — a business opportunity to defer, expand or abandon — must meet before it is worth anything. Worth reading before you present flexibility as a valuation benefit to an investor who values companies by estimating future cash and discounting it back to today. NYU Stern is New York University's business school.*
- **[Design Rules, Volume 1: The Power of Modularity](https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262291859/design-rules-volume-1/)** — Carliss Baldwin and Kim Clark, MIT Press, 2000.  
  *The dense but original source behind this chapter's claim that a module that can change independently is an option the company holds, so modularity is an economic choice.*
- **[Monolith First](https://martinfowler.com/bliki/MonolithFirst.html)** — Martin Fowler, 2015.  
  *Supports this chapter's point that modular boundaries do not require separately released and separately run services. Fowler reports that almost every successful microservice system he had heard of began as a single application later split up, and is careful to call that a tentative observation from the cases reported to him rather than settled evidence.*
- **[Building Evolutionary Architectures](https://evolutionaryarchitecture.com/)** — Neal Ford, Rebecca Parsons and Patrick Kua, O'Reilly, 2017.  
  *Introduces fitness functions: repeatable checks that a system still has the properties you decided it needed, run like tests rather than argued about in review. Here that would mean an automated check that changing one country's rules leaves every other country's invoices identical — which turns this chapter's operating requirement into something tested rather than asserted.*
