# Grounded Architecture Across a Portfolio: Reuse the Foundations, Adapt the Mechanisms

![Grounded Architecture Across a Portfolio: Reuse the Foundations, Adapt the Mechanisms — logo](private-techuity/posts/grounded-architecture-portfolio/assets/images/grounded-architecture-portfolio/logo.jpeg)

> **IN THIS SECTION, YOU WILL:** See how a framework built for architecture practice in large, decentralized organizations can support technology oversight and value creation across an investment portfolio, which parts transfer almost unchanged, which parts must be redesigned around the investor’s actual role, and what a company gains when it adopts the same practice itself.

> **KEY POINTS:**
>
> * **A portfolio resembles a decentralized group in the problem it poses.** Many systems, many teams and nobody with the whole picture; the challenge is to understand the landscape, connect the right people and support better decisions without becoming the bottleneck everyone works around.
> * **Reuse the foundations for understanding.** Lightweight analytics and collaborative networks build a shared, evidence-based view of technology and of the people behind it. Both are highly transferable because neither depends on authority.
> * **Adapt the mechanisms for acting.** An investment portfolio is not one organization. Decision rights, company autonomy, time horizons and investment objectives differ from company to company, so the operating model must be designed around them rather than copied from a corporate group.

An investor holds stakes in a dozen software companies. Each has its own systems, its own cloud bills, its own engineers and its own way of describing all three. The investor wants to know where the technology risks are, where money is being spent twice, which companies could help each other and where its support would do the most good. The companies, meanwhile, want to run their own businesses without answering the same questionnaire every quarter.

I have worked on a version of this problem before, in a different setting. In large, decentralized technology groups, the ones I have practised architecture in, no single team owns the landscape either. Business units choose their own technologies, hundreds of teams ship independently and the central architecture function is at constant risk of becoming either an ivory tower that nobody consults or a bottleneck that everybody resents. **Grounded Architecture** is the framework I developed to work in that setting: an approach that keeps architecture embedded in the organization by prioritizing human interaction and current data over rigid processes and heavy tooling. It rests on three elements: **Lightweight Architectural Analytics** (its data foundation), **Collaborative Networks** (its people foundation) and an **Operating Model** that turns insight and relationships into decisions and action. [S110: Grounded Architecture, framework foundations](https://grounded-architecture.io/grounded-architecture)

This appendix argues that the same framework applies to technology oversight across a portfolio, with one important qualification. The two foundations transfer well, because understanding a landscape and connecting its people do not require authority over anyone. The operating model does not transfer as a structure, because a portfolio is not one organization and the investor’s authority varies from company to company. It transfers as a set of principles that each investor must adapt to its role. In one sentence: **reuse the foundations for understanding technology and people; adapt the mechanisms for making decisions and taking action.**

Two cautions before the argument. First, this is the author’s own framework, and this appendix is the author’s proposal for extending it; the framework’s pages describe a practice and its requirements, not measured outcomes, and no source establishes that the approach improves investment results. Second, the vocabulary of investing is used here as the rest of the book defines it: a **portfolio** is the set of companies an investor holds stakes in, a **portfolio company** is one of them, and an **operating team** is the group of people an investor employs to help those companies rather than to select investments. [Understand Technology Operating Partners: How They Work With Your Team](#tech-operating-partner) examines that function in detail; [Plan Investor Support: Match the Help to Company Priorities](#operating-model-blueprints) sets out the working arrangements this appendix builds on.

## Why a Portfolio Resembles a Decentralized Group, and Where It Does Not

The resemblance is in the shape of the problem. In both settings the technology landscape is diverse and nobody has an accurate picture of it. Decisions are made locally, by people who know their own context far better than any central function does. Expertise is scarce and unevenly distributed: one unit has the only person who really understands a payments integration; another has three. The same problems recur in several places without anyone noticing the pattern. And the central function, whether a group architecture team or an investor’s operating team, faces the same temptation: to compensate for its lack of understanding by demanding reports, and for its lack of influence by issuing rules.

Grounded Architecture starts from the observation that architecture becomes disconnected and ineffective when it is separated from the people and systems it serves, and that the disconnection shows up as process theater, document production or central bottlenecking. [S110: Grounded Architecture, framework foundations](https://grounded-architecture.io/grounded-architecture) Anyone who has sat through a portfolio technology review built from slides that were already out of date when they were made will recognize all three.

![A decentralized group with dotted internal boundaries and a portfolio of separately framed companies, each with a small connector holding a map in the middle.](private-techuity/posts/grounded-architecture-portfolio/assets/images/grounded-architecture-portfolio/two-landscapes-one-problem.jpeg)

**Figure 1:** *Two settings, one problem: a diverse landscape, nobody with the whole picture and a connector in the middle. The boundaries differ: dotted inside a group, solid between companies.*

The differences matter just as much, and they concentrate in one place: **how decisions are made and enforced.**

| Dimension | In a decentralized group | Across a portfolio | Consequence for the framework |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Legal and information boundaries | One legal entity or a group of subsidiaries under common control; information can usually flow freely inside it. | Separate companies with their own boards, confidentiality obligations and sometimes competing customers. | Each company decides what it shares; the shared view is a curated subset, not a raw feed. |
| Source of authority | The organization chart and delegated budgets. | Shareholder agreements, board composition and the investor’s stake, which may be a majority in one company and a minority in the next ([Clarify Authority: Decide Who Decides Before You Disagree](#decide-who-decides)). | Mandates are available in some companies and not others; principles must work without them. |
| Time horizon | The organization intends to exist indefinitely. | The investor intends to sell its stake within a period; the company continues afterward. | Improvements must leave capability with the company, not with the investor’s team ([Choose the Right Help: Compare Investor Support With Other Options](#help-that-changes-capability)). |
| Who pays | One budget, however contested. | The investment firm, the fund or each company, and the allocation is itself a negotiation ([Set the Terms of Help: Agree the Work, Authority and Handover](#useful-engagement)). | Every shared initiative needs a company business case as well as a portfolio one. |
| Incentives | Shared corporate objectives, at least nominally. | The investor’s return and each management team’s equity, which mostly align and sometimes do not ([Compare Incentives and Stakes: Equity, Carry and Jobs](#different-bets)). | “Decide in the organization’s best interests” needs a definition of which organization. |

The rest of the appendix follows this table. Where a dimension mostly concerns understanding, the framework transfers. Where it concerns authority, money or incentives, the framework has to be adapted.

## The Data Pillar: One Evidence Base, Two Levels of Reading

Lightweight Architectural Analytics builds a complete, current picture of the critical elements of a technology landscape from sources that already exist: source-code repositories and their commit history, public-cloud billing reports, incident records, and business and finance data. It does this with open-source or low-cost tools, starting small and adding just enough structure and visibility to support the decisions in front of people. [S106: Lightweight Architectural Analytics](https://grounded-architecture.io/analytics) [Build the Evidence Everyone Trusts: Data Foundations for Alignment](#data-foundations-for-alignment) has already extended that practice from the technology landscape to the investor relationship inside one company, and set out the five requirements that its data must meet: curated, current, credible, actionable and accessible.

Across a portfolio the same sources exist in every software company, and that is the first reason the data pillar transfers so well. Every company has repositories, a cloud bill, an incident history and a finance system. None of them needs to buy a platform to begin. What they may lack is the method: a consistent way to turn those sources into a **map** of systems, technologies, costs, risks and dependencies that can be read at two levels.

**The local level** is the company’s own management. A CTO who can see which systems attract the most change, where the cloud spend concentrates, which components fail most often and which depend on a single contributor has a better basis for the company’s own priorities, whether or not an investor ever looks at it. This is the use the framework was designed for, and it is where the investment in the method pays back first.

**The portfolio level** is the investor’s understanding. Read across companies, the same maps answer questions no single company can: where the portfolio is exposed to the same supplier, the same end-of-life technology or the same kind of operational fragility; where two companies have solved the same problem differently and could learn from each other; where a proposed acquisition would create integration work that nobody has counted ([Plan Acquisitions and Separations: Account for Extra Work, Not Just Expected Value](#acquisition-adds-work-first)).

The practical proposal is to **reuse the methods and the tooling, not to centralize the data.** A shared, open toolkit for producing maps, a shared vocabulary for what counts as a system, a technology, a dependency or a cost, and a shared standard for labelling figures can travel from company to company. The data itself stays with each company, and each company decides which curated views it shares with the investor, following the internal-and-shared boundary that [Build the Evidence Everyone Trusts: Data Foundations for Alignment](#data-foundations-for-alignment) describes. That boundary is not a concession; it is what makes the arrangement work when companies have competing customers or confidentiality obligations that a corporate group would never face.

![Three companies use the same toolkit over their own sources and keep detailed maps at home while one curated card from each forms a shared portfolio map.](private-techuity/posts/grounded-architecture-portfolio/assets/images/grounded-architecture-portfolio/reuse-the-method-keep-the-data.jpeg)

**Figure 2:** *Reuse the method, not the data. Each company runs the same lightweight toolkit over sources it already has, keeps its own map and shares a curated view.*

Two distinctions from the framework help keep the work honest. The first is between **maps and dashboards**. A map gives orientation across the whole landscape and invites exploration; a dashboard tracks specific goals and progress. Both are useful, and conflating them produces poor results: a portfolio scorecard that ranks companies on a handful of metrics is a dashboard pretending to be a map, and it will be gamed the moment it influences anything that matters. [S106: Lightweight Architectural Analytics](https://grounded-architecture.io/analytics) The second is the framework’s own statement of purpose: the point is not to replace judgment with dashboards but to give judgment something stronger to stand on. A portfolio review grounded in current, traceable evidence still requires a judgment about what to do. What it removes is the argument about what is true.

The failure mode to design against is the one the framework was built to prevent: **repeated manual reporting.** If the investor’s understanding depends on each company preparing a deck every quarter, the analytics is not doing its job, the numbers are stale by the time they are read, and each company’s engineers are spending time on the investor that they could spend on customers. The test is simple. When the investor asks a question about technology, can someone answer it from a current map, or does the question start a project?

## The People Pillar: Find the People, Then Connect Them

The second foundation of Grounded Architecture rests on a claim I have found true everywhere I have worked: architecture does not scale through organization charts alone; it scales through people who can connect strategy and execution, move across organizational boundaries and help teams make better decisions in context. Good architects are scarce, and the practical response is to organize, empower and amplify the talent an organization already has rather than wait to hire more. [S111: Grounded Architecture, collaborative networks](https://grounded-architecture.io/people)

The framework’s first step is to identify and connect the people already engaged in architecture work, regardless of title: staff engineers, tech leads, platform owners and solution experts who perform the role informally. Across a portfolio the same step applies, and some of the evidence for it comes from the data pillar. Commit history shows who actually works on which systems; incident records show who responds when something breaks; cloud bills show which teams own which costs. Combined with conversation, that evidence answers the questions an investor most needs answered about people: **who owns what, who knows what, and who works with whom.** The answers are usually different from the organization chart, and the differences are where the risk sits: the dependency on one specialist that [Use Diligence: Correct the Plan Before It Is Signed](#diligence-corrects-the-plan) follows through the book is a people finding before it is a technology finding.

The aim, though, is not a directory. A list of CTOs and their specialisms is a small step. The framework’s ambition is a **collaborative network**: trusted connections among company technology leaders, architects, specialists and the investor’s own operating people, functioning as what the framework calls a peer-to-peer learning and alignment engine. In a group it takes the form of a small central team acting as connector alongside architecture guilds and virtual teams drawn from across the business, meeting through regular forums, an annual summit and ad hoc workshops on specific problems. [S111: Grounded Architecture, collaborative networks](https://grounded-architecture.io/people)

Across a portfolio, the equivalents are already recognizable. [Learn Through Your Investor’s Network: Knowledge, Peers and New Perspectives](#learn-through-investors-network) describes the seminars, summits, peer communities and study visits some investors organize and how a company leader can get value from them. [Choose the Right Help: Compare Investor Support With Other Options](#help-that-changes-capability) describes how scarce expertise in one place can be made available to another. What Grounded Architecture adds is the insistence that these are one system rather than a calendar of events: the network is only as good as the trust in it, trust is built through repeated, useful contact, and someone has to hold the job of maintaining it. In a group that is the central architecture team’s role. In a portfolio it is the natural home of the operating team or technology operating partner, acting as connector rather than controller ([Understand Technology Operating Partners: How They Work With Your Team](#tech-operating-partner)).

![A crossed-out central controller with a stamp and a queue, beside a connector introducing company leaders who form direct peer links.](private-techuity/posts/grounded-architecture-portfolio/assets/images/grounded-architecture-portfolio/connector-not-controller.jpeg)

**Figure 3:** *Connect people without centralizing them. The connector's value is knowing who to introduce to whom; the peer links that form afterward do the work.*

Three adaptations follow from the differences table.

**Connect people without centralizing them.** The framework recommends a small central team with a distributed network rather than a large central function, and warns that a centralized model risks bottlenecks and detachment. [S111: Grounded Architecture, collaborative networks](https://grounded-architecture.io/people) For a portfolio the warning is stronger, because a central portfolio architecture team that approves company designs would cut across each company’s decision rights and its board. The connector’s value is in knowing who to introduce to whom, not in deciding for either.

**Make the purposes of contact explicit.** In a group, a conversation between an architect and a team is a conversation. In a portfolio, a conversation between an investor’s operating person and a company CTO is also, potentially, an input to the investor’s view of that CTO. The operating-partner chapter’s rule applies: keep advice and assessment legible, so that people know which conversation they are in. A network that people suspect of being an appraisal will produce polished presentations and nothing else.

**Agree the terms of shared expertise.** When a specialist from one company helps another, the time, the confidentiality and the cost belong in a short written agreement, however friendly the arrangement. [Set the Terms of Help: Agree the Work, Authority and Handover](#useful-engagement) provides the charter. Peer learning is voluntary and cheap; borrowed expertise is neither, and treating it as a favor is how goodwill runs out.

The people pillar transfers well for the same reason the data pillar does: nothing in it requires authority over anyone. It requires an investor willing to invest in relationships whose returns are hard to attribute, and companies willing to spend some of their scarce leaders’ time on each other. My experience is that both are worth it, and that the second is easier to secure once the first is visible.

## The Operating Model: Adapt the Principles, Not the Structure

The operating model in Grounded Architecture is the mechanism that turns insight and relationships into repeatable decisions, coordinated action and visible impact. It has three practical parts: general principles for how architects engage with teams, a governance approach that balances **nudges**, **economic incentives** and **mandates**, and support for organizational transformation. The framework insists on the order: people and data come first, process second, because an operating model without the foundations underneath it becomes the ivory tower the whole approach exists to avoid. [S112: Grounded Architecture, operating model](https://grounded-architecture.io/operating-model)

This is the element that should not be copied. A corporate group is one organization. Its architecture function can, when it must, use the group’s authority: a mandated security baseline, a banned technology, a cross-charge for cloud costs that lands on a department’s budget. A portfolio has none of that by default. **The investor’s authority is whatever the shareholder agreements, board seats and financing terms give it in each company, and it differs from one company to the next.** A majority-owned buyout with an integration thesis and a minority growth stake in a founder-led company are not two units of the same organization; treating them as such is the fastest way to lose the founder’s trust and the buyout’s management team.

What transfers is the principles, and they transfer well because they were written for a setting where authority was already limited. Grounded Architecture’s general principles include an **engagement mindset** (architects work with teams to strengthen their judgment, not replace it), **data-informed contributions** (bring relevant evidence and define decision boundaries rather than dictate outcomes), **distributed decision authority** (teams hold the richest local information, so most decisions belong to them within strategic guardrails), a general decision policy of **deciding in the organization’s best interests**, and **clear rules of engagement** covering how architects work with teams, how disagreements are resolved and what qualifies for architectural support. [S113: Grounded Architecture, operating-model principles](https://grounded-architecture.io/operating-model-principles) Read “teams” as “companies” and “architects” as “the investor’s technology people,” and four of the five principles apply as written. The fifth needs a change: **“in the organization’s best interests” must say which organization.** The company’s interest and the fund’s usually point the same way. When they do not, when a technology decision that is right for the company’s next five years is wrong for the investor’s exit in two, the operating model must say who decides, and the answer comes from [Clarify Authority: Decide Who Decides Before You Disagree](#decide-who-decides), not from a principle.

The governance approach adapts in the same way. The framework describes three instruments and argues that a strong governance model is a balanced hybrid of all three rather than a one-size-fits-all rule set. [S114: Grounded Architecture, governance principles](https://grounded-architecture.io/governance) Across a portfolio the instruments are still available, but each is attached to a different source of authority and a different cost.

| Instrument | In a group | Across a portfolio | Conditions for using it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Nudges**: guidance that influences without restricting choice, such as principles, recommended practices, golden paths and dashboards that make problems visible | The default instrument for day-to-day guidance. | Shared maps, reference practices, peer comparisons offered rather than imposed, introductions to a company that solved the problem. | Works everywhere, including minority stakes. Needs credibility, which comes from the data and people foundations. |
| **Economic incentives**: letting people decide but making the cost of the decision visible and borne by the decider, such as cloud cost cross-charging | Cross-charges, budgets and funded programs within one budget. | Funding earmarked for a specific improvement; the price of investor support; portfolio-wide procurement whose discount each company weighs against its own migration cost; a company already sees its own cloud bill, so the incentive is in what the investor funds. | Requires clarity about who pays and a company business case for anything shared; a portfolio discount can cost one company more than it saves ([Understand Technology Operating Partners: How They Work With Your Team](#tech-operating-partner)). |
| **Mandates and bans**: explicit rules about what must or must not be done | Available through the management chain; reserved for compliance and strategic constraints. | Available only through a board decision, an investment agreement or a financing covenant, and only in companies where the investor holds that right. | Reserve for risks that threaten the investment itself, such as evidence that backups can be restored ([Build And Test Resilience: Backups Are Not Enough](#prove-you-can-restore)) or a security baseline; fund what a mandate requires, because a mandate a company cannot afford is a wish. |

The design rule that follows is the appendix’s central recommendation for the operating model: **decide the mix per company, and write it down.** The inputs are the investor’s role in that company, its actual decision rights, the autonomy the company needs to execute its plan, and the investment’s objectives and horizon. The output is a short statement of how the investor’s technology people will work with that company, what they can see, what they can recommend, what if anything they can require and how a disagreement is resolved. That is the framework’s rules of engagement, and it is also the engagement charter of [Set the Terms of Help: Agree the Work, Authority and Handover](#useful-engagement) applied to the standing relationship rather than to one assignment. The four blueprints in [Plan Investor Support: Match the Help to Company Priorities](#operating-model-blueprints) are useful starting points for the statement; none of them should be applied to every company in the portfolio.

![Three companies with governance dials for nudges, incentives and mandates set at different levels, each with its own signed rules-of-engagement sheet.](private-techuity/posts/grounded-architecture-portfolio/assets/images/grounded-architecture-portfolio/decide-the-mix-per-company.jpeg)

**Figure 4:** *Decide the mix per company and write it down. The instruments are the same; the settings follow the investor's rights, the company's autonomy and the investment's objectives.*

Transformation support, the third part of the operating model, becomes in the portfolio setting the investor’s **value creation plan**: the intended improvements that turn an investment thesis into company work. Grounded Architecture’s contribution is to insist that the plan be grounded before it is executed: evidence about the current state from the data pillar, the right people connected through the network, and decision boundaries agreed before anyone is asked to change anything. [S115: Transforming organizations with Grounded Architecture](https://grounded-architecture.io/transforming) A value creation plan written before the landscape has been mapped is a set of assumptions, and Part V of this book follows what happens when assumptions meet diligence.

## A Fictional Illustration

Consider Tidewater, a **fictional** growth investor with nine software companies, none of which appears elsewhere in this book. Its two-person operating team has been asked by the investment partners for a view of technology risk across the portfolio. The previous attempt produced a questionnaire, forty pages of answers of varying honesty and a heat map that nobody trusted.

The team starts with the data pillar in the three companies whose CTOs volunteer. Each company runs the same open-source analysis over its own repositories, cloud bill and incident history, and each keeps its own results. What they agree to share is a curated set of views: their major systems and technologies, the age and support status of each, their top ten cloud cost lines and their external dependencies. The vocabulary is agreed once, the labels distinguish observed figures from estimates, and the views refresh monthly without anyone preparing a deck.

Three months in, two things are visible that nobody had reported. Four of the nine companies pay separately for overlapping monitoring tools, and two depend on the same payment provider’s interface that the provider has announced it will withdraw next year. The operating team does not issue a mandate about either. It shares both findings with the companies concerned and introduces the two CTOs facing the payment change to each other and to a CTO in a fourth company who migrated off the same interface last year. One company decides to change monitoring tools at its next renewal. Two do not, because the saving is small against their migration effort, and they say so with numbers. The payment migration becomes a funded item in both affected companies’ plans, with the investor covering the cost of the specialist who helped the fourth company.

None of this is spectacular. The value is that the next board conversation about technology risk in each company starts from a current map rather than a stale questionnaire, the next diligence for the payment-dependent companies contains no surprises about that dependency, and the three volunteer CTOs now call each other without the operating team in the room. Tidewater’s written rules of engagement differ for each company: the operating team may require restore evidence in the two companies where its stake and board seat give it that right, and may only recommend in the others. The example is a proposal about how the framework could be used, not evidence that it produces this result.

## The Reciprocal Benefit: A Company That Can Be Understood Is Easier to Support

The argument so far has taken the investor’s view. The same framework, adopted inside one portfolio company for its own reasons, may change the company’s relationship with its investor. Grounded Architecture describes its effect on organizational alignment as a unifying force through transparency: when everyone can access the same data, misunderstandings decrease and trust increases. [S115: Transforming organizations with Grounded Architecture](https://grounded-architecture.io/transforming) Inside a company that claim is about teams and leadership. Between a company and its investor it becomes a set of potential benefits worth naming, because they are benefits the company controls.

**Conversations about technology risk change register.** A company with a current, curated map of its systems, dependencies and costs can show an investor where the risks are before being asked, with the labels that distinguish an observed problem from an estimate of its consequence. Diligence conversations, board reviews and the first hundred days after a transaction are all shorter and more accurate when the evidence already exists ([Use Diligence: Correct the Plan Before It Is Signed](#diligence-corrects-the-plan), [Plan the First Hundred Days: Turn Expectations Into Funded Work](#first-hundred-days)). The company also controls the boundary: what is shared is a curated view, and [Build the Evidence Everyone Trusts: Data Foundations for Alignment](#data-foundations-for-alignment) explains how to decide what stays internal.

**Transformation priorities become discussable rather than asserted.** An investor asked to fund a platform migration is being asked to trust a judgment. An investor shown the change frequency, incident history and cost of the system being migrated, alongside the roadmap items that depend on it, is being shown the reasoning. The company still has to make the case, and [Test Revenue Assumptions: Do Customers Respond as Expected?](#roadmap-to-revenue) and [Set Priorities: You Cannot Fund Everything at Once](#cannot-fund-everything) show how, but the argument is about what to do rather than about what is true.

**Where support would be most useful becomes visible.** The people foundation inside a company reveals the same things the investor wants to know: who owns what, where the single points of expertise are, which capabilities the company has and which it is missing. A company that knows this can ask its investor for specific help, a specialist for a defined problem, an introduction to a peer who has solved it, rather than accepting generic support that changes nothing ([Choose the Right Help: Compare Investor Support With Other Options](#help-that-changes-capability)). It is also better placed to decline help it does not need.

**Progress becomes visible without a reporting exercise.** The framework’s emphasis on making information available on demand rather than through routine meetings applies directly to the investor relationship. A company whose maps refresh on a schedule can show what changed since the last conversation without preparing for it, and that visible progress is, in my experience, the single most effective builder of an investor’s confidence in a technology leader.

These are potential benefits, and they carry a risk worth stating. Evidence that makes a company easier to understand also makes its weaknesses easier to see, and an investor with better information may be tempted to steer more closely. The protections are the ones the book has already described: labels on every figure, an explicit internal-and-shared boundary, decision rights that are written down, and a relationship in which advice and assessment are kept legible. A company that adopts Grounded Architecture for its own management, and shares what it chooses to share, is in a stronger position in that relationship than one that answers questionnaires. It is not guaranteed a more patient investor.

{id: grounded-architecture-portfolio--what-this-appendix-does-not-establish}
## What This Appendix Does Not Establish

Grounded Architecture is the author’s framework, and its pages are a practitioner’s account of a method and its requirements rather than a study of its effects. Applying it inside an organization is what the framework describes; applying it across a portfolio is this appendix’s proposal, drawn from the resemblance between the two settings and from the investor–company material in Part IV. No source consulted here measures the effect of shared analytics or collaborative networks on investment outcomes, and the fictional Tidewater example illustrates the proposal rather than testing it. The proposal is offered because the alternative, questionnaires, stale slides and rules issued without understanding, is one I have watched fail in both settings.

{id: grounded-architecture-portfolio--questions-to-consider}
## Questions to Consider

1. *For your portfolio, or your company’s investor, which questions about technology currently start a reporting project, and which could be answered from a current map?*
2. *Who in your portfolio companies actually performs architecture work, whatever their title, and how many of them know each other?*
3. *In which companies does the investor hold the right to require anything about technology, and is that right written where the company’s leaders can read it?*
4. *For the next shared initiative, what is each company’s own business case, who pays, and what happens to the company that declines?*
5. *If your company adopted lightweight analytics for its own management tomorrow, which curated views would you choose to share with your investor, and which would you keep internal?*
