# Domain Coverage: From Three Model Families to Forty-Nine Chapters

![Domain Coverage: From Three Model Families to Forty-Nine Chapters — logo](commerce-platform-architecture/posts/domain-coverage/assets/images/domain-coverage/logo.jpeg)

> **IN THIS SECTION, YOU WILL:** Trace every capability of the three research model families behind this book to the chapter that covers it, see where the book departs from the models, and learn which capabilities it leaves out and why.

> **KEY POINTS:**
>
> * **Every capability has a home, and most have two.** The digest behind this page lists 152 distinct capabilities across eleven models. 141 landed in a chapter by plan; this page records where the other eleven, and six thin spots found while checking the written chapters, are mentioned briefly or left out on purpose.
> * **The models describe products; the book describes the joins between them.** No model has an integration layer, a scale-stage view, a journey across products or a failure pattern. The book adds Part VI (integration), Part IX (journeys), Part X (evolution) and the sixteen failure patterns for exactly that reason.
> * **The models are maps, not evidence.** They are architectural models inferred from public product behavior, with a modeled seed value for every number. Nothing on this page, and nothing in the book, uses them as a source for a fact about Shopify, Amazon or any specialist vendor.

This book was planned from **capability models, not from a table of contents**. Before the first chapter was written, three families of Productscape domain models were inventoried brick by brick: a model of a merchant commerce platform of the Shopify kind, a model of a retail marketplace of the Amazon kind, and nine models of specialist commerce products (a B2B webshop platform, a returns platform, an EDI network, a marketplace integrator, a commerce ERP, a store POS, a shipping platform, a cloud WMS and the hub that connects them). The chapters were drawn so that every brick lands somewhere, and so that what the models lack is added on purpose rather than by accident.

This page is the audit trail of that planning. It answers four questions: **"Where is my capability covered?"**, **"Where does the book disagree with the models?"**, **"What did the book add?"** and **"What did it leave out?"** The capability map itself is in [The Map of a Commerce Platform: Capabilities and Layers](#commerce-capability-map) and the rule for deciding which system owns which fact is in [Who Owns the Truth: Boundaries, Bounded Contexts and Systems of Record](#boundaries-and-systems-of-record); this page does not repeat their arguments. It is a lookup table and is meant to be used as one.

## Where the Models Come From

A **Productscape domain model** is a structured description of a product domain: the customers and what they care about, the product **bricks** (named business capabilities) and the **modules** inside each brick (screens, APIs, workers, services, adapters), the data each brick keeps, and a stress test of what could change in the business. The models are kept as JSON and Markdown in the author's sibling `productscape-workspace` repository and were compiled into a research digest for this book on 7 November 2026.

| Family | Model (short code) | Inspired by | Bricks | Root groups |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Merchant platform | `merchant-commerce-platform` (MCP) | The public behavior of a hosted platform of the Shopify kind | 29 | Storefront and buyer experience; commerce core and operations; money and merchant financial services; growth, channels and AI; platform and ecosystem |
| Retail marketplace | `online-retail-marketplace` (ORM) | The public behavior of a marketplace of the Amazon kind | 30 | Shopper experience; seller growth; marketplace foundations |
| Specialist products (nine models) | `b2b-wholesale-ecommerce-platform` (B2B), `ecommerce-returns-management-platform` (RET), `edi-and-b2b-document-exchange-platform` (EDI), `marketplace-channel-integration-platform` (MKT), `omnichannel-commerce-erp-platform` (ERP), `retail-pos-and-store-management-platform` (POS), `shipping-and-carrier-management-platform` (SHP), `warehouse-management-and-fulfilment-platform` (WMS), `katapull-commerce-hub` (HUB) | The public pages of nine specialist commerce products of a Dutch software group | 25, 25, 22, 27, 23, 28, 24, 26, 10 | Per product: a selling layer, a core, an integration layer and a platform foundation; the hub adds a connectivity layer and a shared customer view |

Four rules follow from what the models are:

- **Nothing in a model is a fact about a company.** The models' own notes say that the brick decomposition, module layering, data stores and deployment surfaces are "an architectural model inferred from public product behavior". The book never presents a brick, module or store as the architecture of Shopify, Amazon or any specialist vendor; facts about those companies come only from dated public sources cited in the chapters.
- **Every number in a model is a seed value.** The models' KPI values are, in their authors' words, "illustrative modeling baselines". None is quoted anywhere in the book.
- **The specialist models are used for their capability inventory only.** Which bricks a product has, which data it keeps, which roles it serves. Nothing about the group's or any label's plans, finances, pricing, roadmap or strategy is carried over, and the labels are not named on this page. Where a chapter names a specialist vendor, it does so from a public page verified in a research brief, like any other company.
- **The running example is fictional.** Kestrel & Pine, the fictional outdoor brand this book follows (see [Meet Kestrel & Pine](#meet-kestrel-and-pine)), is not a disguised customer of any vendor; its systems are named by role, and none of its figures or incidents belongs to a real company.

### How to Read the Tables

The capability tables list the models whose bricks carry a capability by short code, then the chapter that covers it. The first chapter named is the **primary** one: it owns the terms and draws the architecture. Chapters in parentheses cover one part of the capability or use it as an input. Placements were built from the digest's capability inventory and the chapter contracts in the book plan, then checked against each written chapter. For the chapters still in revision when this page was compiled ([Protecting the Shop: Card Data, Account Takeover, Bots and Skimmers](#commerce-security), [Rules Turned Into Requirements: Consumer, Payment, Invoicing and Platform Regulation](#commerce-regulation), [Journey 2: The Jacket Comes Back](#journey-return-and-exchange), [Assistants and Agents: AI in Commerce and What Is Real in 2026](#ai-and-agentic-commerce), [Case: Shopify, the Modular Monolith That Serves Millions of Merchants](#case-shopify)) the placements follow the chapter contracts.

Three chapters cover every capability at map level and are not repeated in every row: [The Map of a Commerce Platform: Capabilities and Layers](#commerce-capability-map) (the map and the layers), [Who Owns the Truth: Boundaries, Bounded Contexts and Systems of Record](#boundaries-and-systems-of-record) (the entity–owner table) and [What Changes at Each Scale: From First Store to Group](#commerce-scaling-stages) (every chapter's scale-stage table gathered into one). Part I, the journeys of Part IX and the cases of Part XI use capabilities as context and appear only where they own a point.

Two things the persona lists show. The same four wants recur in nearly every model: stock true in every system, orders that arrive without retyping, changes that do not break the live chain, and a month close without reconciliation; those are the Truth and Integration families of [Out of Stock, Out of Sync: Sixteen Ways Commerce Platforms Go Wrong](#where-commerce-platforms-go-wrong), seen from the customer's chair. And no model has a persona for the **architect**, the role the book speaks in: the models describe what each product's customer wants and leave how the products should fit together to whoever buys them. That is the gap the book is written into.

## How to Use This Map

1. **Match by meaning, not by name.** For each system you own, find the row that does the same job and read its primary chapter first.
2. **Read the departures before you copy a decomposition.** An order record in seven systems or a stock ledger in five is not a design; it is the models' shape, and the departures table names the chapters that explain what to do about it.
3. **Check the left-out list against your business.** If you sell food, pharma or cosmetics, lots are not optional; if you run a workshop or your own vans, the book leaves you a sidebar to write.
4. **Use the persona row as a reading route.** Find your seat at Kestrel & Pine and start with the chapters in its row.

The rest of the Reference section turns the map into working tools: [Toolkit: Cards and Templates for Commerce Architecture Decisions](#toolkit) holds the cards and templates the chapters introduce (the capability map, the entity–owner table, the integration inventory, the reconciliation card and the rest), [Glossary: Plain-Language Definitions](#glossary) defines the terms each chapter owns, and [Bibliography: Every Source the Book Cites](#bibliography) lists every source the chapters cite. Continue with [Toolkit: Cards and Templates for Commerce Architecture Decisions](#toolkit).
