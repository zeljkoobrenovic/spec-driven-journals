## How to Read This Register

**R** marks a consulted source; the numbers identify, they do not rank. The register holds **461 sources** harvested from the **Sources and Evidence** sections of 49 pages, deduplicated by normalized URL (scheme, `www.`, trailing slash, fragment, `.html` suffix and arXiv version ignored), by RFC number and by EU CELEX number; where chapters cited one document under two URLs, the entry keeps both. Identifiers were assigned in topic order for this first edition and stay stable: **new sources append from the next free R-number and are never renumbered**, and a removed source's number is retired rather than reused, so a reference to an identifier keeps meaning the same document. The book is still being written, so later chapters will add entries at the end of the numbering.

**Evidence types.** Each entry's type is the source group under which the citing chapters listed it; where chapters listed one source under two groups, the entry takes the group most of them used. The type tells you what kind of claim the source can carry:

| Evidence type | Sources | What it is | How to read it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Research papers | 143 | Peer-reviewed papers, preprints and benchmark reports; a preprint is marked as one where the citing chapter said so | A finding holds within the study's benchmark, setting, model generation and date; agent benchmarks age fast and many results are preprints |
| Specifications, standards and regulation | 86 | Protocol specifications (MCP, OpenTelemetry, RFCs), security standards and taxonomies (OWASP, NIST) and legal texts (the EU AI Act, the GDPR, sector rules) | Authoritative on what the text says at its version or date; protocol revisions are dated, and applying a legal text to a specific business belongs to its lawyers |
| Vendor documentation and engineering reports | 177 | Model-provider and framework documentation, engineering posts, guides and research write-ups published by companies about their own systems | Documentation describes the product as offered when it was read; an engineering post is documented experience about that company's system at that date, not evidence of effectiveness elsewhere |
| Incidents, cases and decisions | 43 | Incident reports, postmortems, vulnerability records, tribunal decisions and press reports of documented agent failures | Records what happened in one case at one date; press reports are secondary, often founder-sourced, and the entry says so where the chapter did |
| Design examples from the author's interview project | 12 | The author's system-design interview designs (`system-design-interviews/data/book/<design>/interview.json`) | Design examples, not deployed systems: they illustrate a design move and its reasoning, and are never evidence of how a system behaves in production |

**Dates.** Each entry gives the **publication year as the citing chapter gave it**. Where a paper appeared first as a preprint and later at a venue, the entry keeps both years; where chapters gave different years, it keeps the most specific citation. "n.d., accessed 2026-10" marks a page that carries no date and was read in October 2026, when the research for this book was done.

**Volatility.** Agentic systems move faster than most of the fields a reader is used to. **142 entries are marked *living page***: documentation, pricing, model lists, protocol pages and framework guides that change without notice. Treat what they support as true **as of October 2026**, and check the page again before relying on a limit, a price, a default or a feature. Protocol specifications such as the Model Context Protocol are cited at a dated revision; a later revision may change the detail but rarely the architectural point. Research results on agents describe the models of their date: a benchmark score or a failure rate from 2024 is a record of that generation, not of today's models, and the chapters use such numbers as indicators of a mechanism, not as rates to plan with.

**Design examples are not deployed systems.** The last topic lists the author's system-design interview designs. The chapters use them, always labeled, to show a design move and its reasoning; they were never operated in production, so nothing in them is evidence of production behavior. Ostrell, the company the book follows, is fictional, and no source in this register describes it.

The chapters also rely on the book's own research briefs and digests, written in October 2026 to collect and check the facts the chapters use. Those are working notes, not public evidence, and are not listed; wherever a brief's fact matters, the chapter cites the public source behind it. Links to the companion books are cross-references, not external evidence, and are not listed either. Plain-language definitions of the terms used in the entries are in [[glossary]].

## Topic Index

Identifiers run in topic order, so each topic is one contiguous block in this first edition. A source sits under the topic whose chapters cite it most, and its **Cited in** line lists every page that uses it. A few rules override that count: legal texts sit together under oversight and regulation, research on prompt injection under security, papers that define a benchmark under evaluation, isolation mechanisms under sandboxes, and protocol specifications under tools.
