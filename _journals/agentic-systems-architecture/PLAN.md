# Book Plan and Chapter Contracts

**Architecting Agentic Systems**

*How software architects, senior engineers and technical leaders decide where a model may decide, what it may touch, what it remembers, how its actions are made durable, reversible or approved, and how evidence earns it more autonomy.*

**Status:** first edition in progress · **Created:** 2026-10-28

## Positioning

The reader is an experienced software architect, principal or staff engineer, or technical leader who must design, review, operate or fund systems in which a language model plans and acts over several steps: support and sales agents, back-office agents in finance, legal and HR, research agents, coding agents and harnesses, and agents in safety- or rights-critical services. The reader knows distributed systems, APIs, data platforms and security basics. The reader does not need an introduction to transformers; they need a way to make and defend architectural decisions.

The central idea: **an agentic system is an ordinary distributed system with one unusual component — a model that proposes the next action, and whose proposals vary — and the architecture's job is to bound that component.** Every architectural decision in the book is a decision about where that boundary sits: which choices the model may make and which stay in deterministic code; which tools, data and credentials it can reach; what state it sees and what survives a crash; which actions are idempotent, reversible, compensable or require a human; and which evidence (evaluations, traces, incident history) justifies widening or narrowing its autonomy.

Three consequences run through the book:

- **Autonomy is a budget, not a property.** It is granted per action class, sized by the consequence and reversibility of failure, and earned with evidence. The same platform runs a coding agent that may rewrite a file freely inside a sandbox, a support agent that may grant a €250 credit, and a benefits-casework agent that may only draft.
- **The model is the least reliable component, so the rest must be more reliable than usual.** Durable state, idempotent tools, typed contracts, policy enforcement outside the model, and evidence logs are what make a variable component safe to run.
- **Evaluation is part of the architecture, not a phase.** The system must produce the traces and datasets that tell you whether a change made it better, and those results feed deployment gates and autonomy tiers.

The book is **evidence-led and vendor-neutral**. It draws on original research papers, official specifications (Model Context Protocol, Agent2Agent, OpenTelemetry GenAI conventions, OAuth), official vendor documentation, engineering reports from teams that built and operated agentic systems, incident analyses and court and regulator decisions, and regulation (EU AI Act, GDPR, sector rules). Every substantive technical claim carries a nearby citation. Durable principles are separated from framework-, model- and version-specific details, which are dated.

The book draws selectively on the author's system-design interview project (`system-design-interviews/data/book/`): `agentic-platform-foundations`, `agentic-support-platform`, `agentic-sales-platform`, `agentic-marketing-platform`, `agentic-finance-platform`, `agentic-legal-platform`, `agentic-hr-platform`, `agentic-research-platform`, `agentic-healthcare-platform`, `agentic-public-benefits-platform`, `ai-coding-cli` and `ai-coding-harness`. ## Comparison discipline: "Across Domains"

Every chapter in Parts II–X has a section titled **"Across Domains: How the Stakes Change the Design"** with a comparison table (columns: Coding agents · Customer operations (support, sales, marketing) · Back office (finance, legal, HR) · High-stakes decisions (healthcare, public benefits) — merge columns only when genuinely identical) and a short judgement on what the platform can keep common and what must be domain policy. Where the domains genuinely do not differ for the topic, say so in one paragraph rather than inventing differences.
