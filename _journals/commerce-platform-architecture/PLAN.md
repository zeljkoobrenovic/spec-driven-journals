# ORDERED — Book Plan and Chapter Contracts

**ORDERED: Architecting Commerce Platforms**

*How commerce systems fit together, why the architectural decisions behind them matter, and how those decisions evolve as a business grows — following one order from discovery to delivery and back.*

**Status:** first edition in progress · **Created:** 2026-11-07 · **Research cut-off:** 7 November 2026

## Positioning

The reader is **new to commerce architecture**: an engineer, product manager, analyst, consultant or leader who has just joined a retailer, a brand, a wholesaler, a marketplace seller, a commerce software vendor or an agency, and who needs to understand how the systems behind selling fit together before they can make or judge decisions about them. The reader is assumed to be generally software-literate (knows roughly what an API, a database and a queue are) but not to know commerce vocabulary (SKU, ATP, OMS, WMS, PSP, EDI, PIM, 3DS, punchout) or the architectural vocabulary the book uses (bounded context, system of record, idempotency, eventual consistency, multi-tenancy). Every such term is explained in plain language the first time it appears in each chapter.

The book takes the perspective of an **architect who advises a growing omnichannel merchant** and speaks in the first person when giving judgement. It is **platform-neutral**: Shopify, commercetools, Shopware, Adobe Commerce, SAP, Salesforce, Amazon, Zalando, Katapull and others appear as dated, sourced examples, never as the answer.

The central idea: **a commerce platform is a promise-keeping machine.** It shows a product, promises a price, a delivery and a return, takes the money, and then has to keep every promise across a dozen systems owned by different teams and vendors: catalog, pricing, inventory, checkout, payments, orders, warehouse, carriers, stores, marketplaces, EDI, ERP, data. Nearly every commerce failure is a promise that one system made and another could not keep. Architecture is the discipline of deciding which system may make which promise, how the others learn about it, and what happens when they disagree. The right answer changes with the size and shape of the business, which is why the book follows one merchant through its growth and compares every decision across four scale stages.

The book is **evidence-led**: company-specific statements are documented facts with a source and a publication date; everything else is labeled as industry practice, reference-architecture inference, recommendation, illustration (the fictional merchant) or assumption. It distinguishes, at the end of every chapter, what is **established** (the field agrees), what is **context-dependent** (right in some situations, wrong in others, and it says which) and what is **the author's synthesis**.

## Provenance

The book starts from the author's private journal *FEDERATED* (a commerce-architecture analysis written for a group of specialist commerce software labels, 1 November 2026), whose general parts — the commerce layers, the eight principles, the Shopify lessons, the agentic-commerce tutorial and the fourteen failure patterns — are generalized here into a platform-neutral treatment. Nothing confidential from that journal is used; only its public, dated sources, re-verified for this book. Three Productscape domain models in the author's sibling repository serve as research maps (the `merchant-commerce-platform` model inspired by Shopify, the `online-retail-marketplace` model inspired by Amazon, and the nine `katapull-group` models of specialist commerce products); they are **models, not descriptions of any company**, and the Domain Coverage page maps their bricks to chapters. Verified research briefs live in `_research/brief-*.md`.

## Running Example

**Kestrel & Pine** is fictional: an outdoor apparel and equipment brand from Arnhem that sells online in three countries, through six stores, to 1,100 wholesale accounts and on three marketplaces, and that is in the middle of growing out of its first architecture. ## Teaching discipline

Every chapter in Parts II–VIII answers, in named sections, the five questions the book exists for: **what problem this solves and why it matters** (business first), **how the components work together**, **which choices are available**, **what trade-offs they introduce**, and **when the approach fits and when it becomes limiting**. The last of these is a required section titled **"Across Scale Stages: When It Fits and When It Limits"** with a table across the four stages defined in the ledger (Starting, Growing, Omnichannel, Group). Every chapter ends with **"What to Take Away"**, split into *Established*, *Depends on context* and *The author's synthesis*.

The recurring order is **Eva's jacket** (ledger): each chapter says, in one short orientation paragraph near the top, where in the order's journey it sits.
