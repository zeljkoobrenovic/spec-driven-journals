## PART 1: Checkout

### 1.1 Checkout as an orchestration

Checkout is the short window between "add to cart" and "order confirmed" in which the platform must turn a draft (the cart) into a set of promises it can keep: a price, a tax amount, an availability, a delivery option and price, and a payment. No single system owns all of those, so checkout is an orchestration over several services, with a time limit set by the shopper's patience and by the PSP's authorization validity [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT].

The sequence is visible in vendor documentation:

- commercetools Checkout creates the Payment and an authorization Transaction, "sets the `state` of the Transaction to `Pending` and creates an Order" before the PSP responds; the outcome arrives later by notification, and the documentation warns "You should start fulfilling an Order only if the Payment contains a Transaction" with a successful state. If a cart fails to convert into an order after authorization, "it is your responsibility" to detect it (via Subscriptions) and cancel the payment or re-create the order [VERIFIED: https://docs.commercetools.com/checkout/payments-lifecycle.md, n.d., accessed 2026-11].
- Stripe's Checkout fulfilment guide: "You can't rely on triggering fulfillment only from your checkout landing page, because it's not guaranteed customers visit that page. For example, a customer can pay successfully and then lose their internet connection before your landing page loads." The fulfilment function must "Correctly handle being called multiple times with the same Checkout Session ID", check `payment_status` and "Record fulfillment status" [VERIFIED: https://docs.stripe.com/checkout/fulfillment.md?ui=full-page, n.d., accessed 2026-11].
- Shopify separates checkout from the theme: a "checkout and accounts editor" customises the checkout, thank-you, order-status and customer-account pages, and "Advanced checkout customization features are available only on the Shopify Plus plan"; the Checkout Branding API is Plus-only [VERIFIED: https://help.shopify.com/en/manual/checkout-settings/checkout-extensibility, n.d., accessed 2026-11].

| Checkout step | What it must call | Promise it makes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Identity (guest or account) | Customer/identity service | "We know who to deliver to and bill" |
| Address | Address validation, tax determination (ship-to drives VAT/sales tax) | "This is where it goes and what tax applies" |
| Shipping options | Shipping-rate service / carrier rules, availability | "Delivered by X for Y" |
| Price review | Pricing, promotions, tax engine | "You pay exactly this" |
| Payment | PSP (authorization, SCA), fraud screening | "Money is reserved" |
| Confirmation | Order creation, inventory reservation, notifications | "The order exists and is being kept" |

### 1.2 Abandonment and usability figures (Baymard Institute)

- Baymard's running list of cart-abandonment studies gives an average documented rate of **70.22%**, "the plain, unweighted mean of every study listed": 50 studies published 2006–2025, with individual rates between 55% and 84%; page updated 28 October 2026 [VERIFIED: https://baymard.com/lists/cart-abandonment-rate, published 2026-10-28].
- Reasons for abandonment among US shoppers who were not "just browsing" (42% said they were): extra costs too high 40%, slow delivery 20%, distrust of the site with card details 19%, account creation required 18%, long or complicated checkout 17%, website errors 17%, unsatisfactory returns policy 13%, total cost not visible upfront 12%, card declined 10%, too few payment methods 9% [VERIFIED: same page]. The percentages are multi-select and sum to well over 100% [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT].
- Baymard claims a **35.26%** conversion uplift is available to large sites from fixing solvable checkout issues and extrapolates "$260 billion" of recoverable orders across US and EU e-commerce; it acknowledges the gains "won't come easy" [VERIFIED: same page]. **Methodology caveat**: the 70% figure mixes studies with different definitions of "cart" and "abandonment" (some count a product-page add, some a checkout start), different industries and different years; the uplift is Baymard's own estimate from its usability testing, not a controlled experiment [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT].
- Baymard's checkout benchmark: the average checkout had **11.3 form fields** in 2024 (11.8 in 2021, 12.7 in 2019) and 5.1 steps counted from the cart to the order review step inclusive; "most sites need only 8 form fields in total for a checkout flow" [VERIFIED: https://baymard.com/blog/checkout-flow-average-form-fields, published 2024-06-26].

### 1.3 Guest vs account, express wallets

- Forced account creation is the fourth-largest stated reason to abandon (18%) [VERIFIED: Baymard list, 2026-10-28]. The architectural consequence: identity must be optional at checkout and attachable afterwards; an order needs a customer *reference*, not a customer *account* [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT].
- Apple Pay is positioned as an express path: "Customers can complete their purchase from a product page with a single tap, without the need to add to a cart or fill out a form", and the merchant "don't receive your customer's actual credit or debit card numbers, so you aren't handling sensitive data in your systems" [VERIFIED: https://developer.apple.com/apple-pay/, n.d., accessed 2026-11]. Apple's implementation overview adds that "Safari supports two JavaScript APIs" for web acceptance and recommends using "an Apple Pay SDK from a payment service provider" [VERIFIED: https://developer.apple.com/apple-pay/implementation/, n.d., accessed 2026-11].
- Google Pay for web: the payment sheet lists methods saved to the Google account; "Google Pay securely returns a payment token for that method to your website", the backend "sends the payment token to the payment service provider" to execute the payment; a direct (non-gateway) integration is "exclusively available to merchants who are Payments Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI DSS) compliant" [VERIFIED: https://developers.google.com/pay/api/web/overview, n.d., accessed 2026-11].
- Express wallets therefore skip the address and shipping steps by supplying them from the wallet, and skip card entry by supplying a token; the checkout orchestration still has to run pricing, tax and shipping-rate calls, now in a callback from the wallet sheet instead of from a form [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT].

### 1.4 How protected checkout is: Shopify's checkout.liquid timeline

Shopify's history is the best-documented example of a platform taking checkout customisation *away* from merchants and replacing it with a sandboxed extension model:

- 13 February 2023: Shopify announced that `checkout.liquid` (the Plus-only theme file that let merchants edit the checkout's HTML) was deprecated for the Information, Shipping and Payment pages as of **13 August 2024**; checkouts not upgraded "will automatically revert to a standard, uncustomized Shopify Checkout". The replacement, checkout extensibility, was described as "app-based, upgrade-safe, higher-converting, and integrated with Shop Pay" [VERIFIED: https://changelog.shopify.com/posts/the-checkout-liquid-theme-file-is-being-deprecated, published 2023-02-13].
- The Thank-you and Order-status pages followed: "`checkout.liquid` and additional scripts were sunset for the Thank you and Order status pages on **August 28, 2025**" for Plus stores; script tags on those pages are sunset for non-Plus stores on **26 August 2026** [VERIFIED: https://shopify.dev/docs/storefronts/themes/architecture/layouts/checkout-liquid, n.d., accessed 2026-11]. The help centre confirms "As of August 28, 2025, the additional scripts section in the Checkout settings is view-only" and "August 26, 2026 was the deadline for stores on a non-Plus Shopify subscription plan to upgrade" their Thank-you and Order-status pages [VERIFIED: https://help.shopify.com/en/manual/checkout-settings/checkout-extensibility/checkout-upgrade, n.d., accessed 2026-11] ⚠.

**For newcomers.** *Cart*: a draft of an order that the shopper can still change; it reserves nothing and promises nothing. *Checkout*: the orchestrated sequence that turns the cart into an order by calling pricing, tax, availability, shipping and payment. *Express wallet*: Apple Pay, Google Pay, Shop Pay, PayPal — a stored identity, address and payment token that lets the shopper skip the forms.

**Architecture implications** [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT]. Checkout is the one place where every promise-making system is called in sequence under a clock, so it should be thin: it composes calls and holds a short-lived state machine, but it does not own prices, tax rules, stock or payment state. **Starting**: use the hosted platform's checkout as is; its protection is a feature, not a limitation. **Growing**: customise through sanctioned extension points (apps, functions, UI extensions) and never by patching checkout markup; the Shopify dates show what happens to unsanctioned customisations. **Omnichannel**: checkout becomes a channel-independent capability (web, app, POS, B2B portal) that calls the same pricing, tax and availability services; an order must be creatable *before* the PSP's final answer arrives and reconcilable afterwards (commercetools' flow). **Group**: several storefronts share one checkout service with per-brand configuration; the contract between checkout and the order system (idempotent "create order from cart X with payment Y") becomes a governed API.

## PART 2: Payment service providers and the flow of money

### 2.1 What a PSP does

A payment service provider (PSP) is the merchant's single counterparty for taking money: it presents payment methods, routes each to the right network or bank (card schemes, iDEAL, Bancontact, SEPA, PayPal, Klarna), handles authentication (3-D Secure), screens for fraud, holds the merchant's balance, pays out in batches and reports on everything. Most PSPs that serve European merchants are also acquirers or partner with one; the merchant sees one API and one settlement stream [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT]. The documentation of Adyen, Stripe and Mollie describes the same four-stage money flow, which the rest of this part follows: **authorization → capture → settlement → payout**, with **refund** and **chargeback** flowing backwards.

### 2.2 Authorization, capture, settlement, payout

- Adyen: "The authorization reserves the funds on the shopper's bank account." "By default, payments are captured automatically without a delay, immediately after authorization of the payment request." A delay can be set in the Customer Area or per payment ("The setting in the API request for an individual payment overrides the setting in the Customer Area"). Manual capture "lets you settle funds on fulfillment, when an order is shipped"; the capture result arrives in a CAPTURE webhook. With a single partial capture "Any unclaimed amount that is left over after partially capturing a payment is automatically cancelled"; multiple partial captures must be enabled by Adyen Support [VERIFIED: https://docs.adyen.com/online-payments/capture/, n.d., accessed 2026-11].
- Adyen's authorization validity: "Adyen expires (pre-)authorization requests automatically after 28 days" by default, but scheme rules are shorter — Mastercard 7 days (final auth) or 30 days (pre-auth), Visa 5 days for merchant-initiated and 10 days for card-not-present customer-initiated transactions, American Express 7 days; "Final authorizations cannot be adjusted" [VERIFIED: https://docs.adyen.com/online-payments/adjust-authorisation/, n.d., accessed 2026-11].
- Stripe: card-not-present authorization windows are Visa 7 days (CIT) / 5 days (MIT), Mastercard, Amex and Discover 7 days; "You need to capture the funds before the authorization expires. If the authorization expires before you capture the funds, the funds are released and the payment status changes to `canceled`." A partial capture "automatically releases the remaining amount" and for most payments "you can only perform one capture on an authorized payment"; Klarna must be captured "by midnight of the 28th calendar day", PayPal holds 10 days (extended to 20); iDEAL and ACH do not support separate authorization and capture [VERIFIED: https://docs.stripe.com/payments/place-a-hold-on-a-payment-method, n.d., accessed 2026-11].
- Mollie: manual capture is available for cards, Klarna, Billie, Riverty, Vipps, MobilePay, PayPal (beta) and Billink; authorizations expire after 30 days (Mastercard), 7 days (Visa, Amex), 28 days (Klarna, Billie, Billink), 30 days (Riverty), 29 days (PayPal); "It is not possible to extend the authorization period"; automatic delayed capture is "for up to 7 days after the authorization" (cards only) [VERIFIED: https://docs.mollie.com/docs/place-a-hold-for-a-payment.md, n.d., accessed 2026-11].
- Mollie's iDEAL entry: "Instant charge", auto-capture only, partial and multiple partial refunds, refund validity 365 days, chargeback risk "No", EUR only, session timeout 15 minutes, no recurring (but usable as the first payment of a recurring series) [VERIFIED: https://docs.mollie.com/docs/ideal.md, n.d., accessed 2026-11].
- Settlement timing and payouts are separate. Stripe: initial settlement timing is 7 calendar days in most EU/EEA countries and 2 business days in the US, changing to 3 business days by default in most EU countries; "Choosing a payout schedule doesn't change how long it takes for your pending balance to become available. It only controls when payouts are sent"; a negative balance leads to "a payout that *debits* your bank account" [VERIFIED: https://docs.stripe.com/payouts, n.d., accessed 2026-11].
- Adyen: "Sales day payout is the default payout model" — "You get all the funds from a particular sales day in a single payout batch, with a fixed payout delay"; the default delay is 2 business days for standard currencies acquired in the matching region and 3 otherwise; frequency is daily; Friday–Sunday sales pay out on Tuesday; "Pass-through payout is the only available payout model in Africa, Brazil, and India" [VERIFIED: https://docs.adyen.com/account/sales-day-payout.md, n.d., accessed 2026-11].
- Mollie: a settlement is a payout of everything that affected the available balance "between the current and the last settlement": revenue (payments, captures, chargeback reversals, returned refunds), deductions (refunds, chargebacks) and costs (method fees, chargeback fees, refund fees), with a Settlements API that returns both aggregated totals and the individual payments, refunds, chargebacks and captures per settlement [VERIFIED: https://docs.mollie.com/docs/settlement-report.md, n.d., accessed 2026-11].

| Stage | Who acts | What the merchant's order system should record |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Authorization | Issuer approves, PSP reserves | PSP reference, amount, method, expiry (`capture_before`/`captureBefore`), 3DS result |
| Capture | Merchant (or PSP automatically) | Captured amount, capture reference, link to shipment |
| Settlement | Scheme/bank moves funds to PSP | Nothing yet (PSP's concern) |
| Payout | PSP pays merchant's bank | Batch/payout id, net amount, fees — reconciled by finance, not by the order system |

### 2.3 Refunds, cancels and reversals

- Adyen: "You can only refund a payment after it has been captured" — uncaptured payments are *cancelled*; a *reversal* is for "cases where you don't know whether a payment was captured". "The refund process is asynchronous": a REFUND webhook with `success: true` means the request went to the scheme, and "Although rare, a refund can fail after you received" it (REFUND_FAILED), sometimes days later; for iDEAL, Bancontact and bank transfers a REFUNDED_REVERSED event means the funds came back because the shopper's account was invalid; "It can take up to 40 business days before the funds are returned to the shopper's account" depending on method; across partial refunds "the sum of the refund amounts cannot be more than the captured amount" [VERIFIED: https://docs.adyen.com/online-payments/refund/, n.d., accessed 2026-11].
- Stripe: "Stripe's processing fees from the original transaction aren't returned"; refunds "can only be sent back to the original payment method"; the customer "sees the refund as a credit approximately 5-10 business days later"; refunds shortly after the charge appear as a *reversal* in which "the original charge drops off the customer's statement"; a failed refund returns the money to the merchant's balance, which "can take up to 30 days"; for bank-debit methods (SEPA, ACH, Bacs) "there's a risk of double refund" if the merchant refunds while the bank also disputes [VERIFIED: https://docs.stripe.com/refunds, n.d., accessed 2026-11].
- Mollie: refund statuses are queued ("due to a lack of balance"), pending, processing ("Cancelation is no longer possible"), refunded, failed, canceled; "Mollie blocks duplicate refunds made in a short time frame"; after a connection error "do not retry automatically. Check the payment's refunds through the API" first [VERIFIED: https://docs.mollie.com/docs/refunds.md, n.d., accessed 2026-11].
- Stripe's cost advice: businesses that refund often close to the sale should use manual capture, "canceling payments before they're captured, or by reducing your captured amount rather than processing a refund" [VERIFIED: https://docs.stripe.com/refunds, n.d., accessed 2026-11].

### 2.4 Chargebacks (disputes)

- Stripe: a dispute "occurs when a cardholder questions your payment with their card issuer"; the issuer's dispute "immediately reverses the payment", pulling the amount plus "one or more network dispute fees" from Stripe, which "debits your balance for the payment amount and dispute fee" [VERIFIED: https://docs.stripe.com/disputes, n.d., accessed 2026-11].
- Adyen's stages and webhooks: Notification of Fraud ("This is not a dispute, and no money is withdrawn"), Request for Information (being phased out by the schemes), Notification of Chargeback (start of the defence period), Chargeback ("The disputed amount is withdrawn from your account"), Information Supplied (defence forwarded, "no longer possible to change these documents"), Chargeback Reversed, pre-arbitration, scheme arbitration (fee "up to USD 600"), Second Chargeback (final, no defence). "Depending on the payment method, the chargeback debit happens a few days after you receive the NoC, or after you defend the dispute." Adyen auto-defends a dispute "that was already refunded" or "a fraudulent chargeback for which there was a liability shift" [VERIFIED: https://docs.adyen.com/risk-management/understanding-disputes/dispute-process-and-flow, n.d., accessed 2026-11].
- Cardholders "can dispute a charge up to 120 days after a payment was made (and sometimes even later)", so dispute rates for recent months keep changing [VERIFIED: https://docs.stripe.com/disputes/measuring, n.d., accessed 2026-11]. Thresholds are in PART 6.

### 2.6 Card network economics: interchange and scheme fees

- The EU Interchange Fee Regulation (EU) 2015/751 caps per-transaction interchange at **0.2%** for consumer debit cards (Article 3) and **0.3%** for consumer credit cards (Article 4); Member States may set lower domestic caps; the caps applied from **9 December 2015**. Article 12 requires the payee's PSP to tell the payee, per transaction, the reference, the amount and "the amount of any charges for the payment transaction, indicating separately the merchant service charge and the amount of the interchange fee" [VERIFIED: http://publications.europa.eu/resource/celex/32015R0751, published 2015-05-19 (OJ L 123)] **(evidence age: 11 years; still in force)**.
- Adyen's price list separates a fixed processing fee ("$0.13" per transaction) from a method fee: Visa/Mastercard and Bancontact "$0.13 + Interchange++ + 0.60%", iDEAL "$0.13 + €0.22", SEPA Direct Debit "$0.13 + €0.27", PayPal "direct contract with PayPal + management service fee"; "Interchange fees are variable, our Interchange++ pricing passes these fees directly to you"; "The fees outlined above are indicative" [VERIFIED: https://www.adyen.com/pricing, n.d., accessed 2026-11] ⚠.
- Stripe's refund page mentions that "IC+" users "might see a difference in cost between reversals and refunds because reversals usually incur lower network fees" [VERIFIED: https://docs.stripe.com/refunds, n.d., accessed 2026-11].
- Interchange++ (interchange + scheme fee + acquirer markup, each itemised) versus blended pricing (one rate) is the main commercial choice; blended is simpler to book, IC++ is cheaper at volume and makes the fee lines in the ledger more numerous [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT].

**For newcomers.** *PSP*: the provider that takes the shopper's money through many methods and pays the merchant one net amount. *Authorization vs capture*: authorization reserves the money on the shopper's card or account; capture tells the bank to actually move it, usually when the goods ship. *Settlement vs payout*: settlement is the network moving money to the PSP; payout is the PSP moving the net amount, after fees, refunds and chargebacks, to the merchant's bank, usually in daily batches. *Chargeback*: the cardholder's bank reverses a payment through the card network; the merchant may defend it with evidence. *Interchange*: the fee the merchant's bank pays the cardholder's bank on every card payment, capped in the EU at 0.2% (debit) and 0.3% (credit).

**Architecture implications** [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT]. The order system must model payment as a *state machine with external truth*: authorized (with expiry), captured (amount may differ), refunded (possibly partially, possibly failed later), disputed. Never store "paid: true". **Starting**: one PSP, automatic capture, hosted payment page; refunds from the platform admin only. **Growing**: capture on shipment (ties money to fulfilment, saves refund fees), one owner for refunds (the Double Refund in the ledger happened because two systems could both refund), payment method mix per country. **Omnichannel**: separate terminal provider for stores and PSP for online, so payments *reconciliation* needs two feeds; B2B on invoice means "payment" can be a 30-day receivable, not a PSP event. **Group**: PSP orchestration (several PSPs behind one internal payment service) becomes worth its complexity only when volume, countries or resilience demand it.

## PART 3: Strong customer authentication and the regulatory calendar

### 3.1 PSD2 and the RTS on SCA

- PSD2, Directive (EU) 2015/2366, Article 4(30): "'strong customer authentication' means an authentication based on the use of two or more elements categorised as knowledge (something only the user knows), possession (something only the user possesses) and inherence (something the user is) that are independent". Article 97(1) requires SCA where the payer "initiates an electronic payment transaction", and Article 97(2) requires, for remote electronic payments, SCA "that includes elements which dynamically link the transaction to a specific amount and a specific payee". Member States had to apply the directive from **13 January 2018** (Article 115) [VERIFIED: http://publications.europa.eu/resource/celex/32015L2366, published 2015-12-23 (OJ L 337)] **(evidence age: 11 years; being replaced by PSD3/PSR, see 3.4)**.
- The regulatory technical standards, Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2018/389 (27 November 2017), applied from **14 September 2019** (Article 38) and set the exemptions [VERIFIED: http://publications.europa.eu/resource/celex/32018R0389, published 2018-03-13 (OJ L 69)]:
  - Article 11 contactless at POS: up to EUR 50 per transaction with cumulative limits;
  - Article 13 trusted beneficiaries (payee on a list the payer created with SCA);
  - Article 14 recurring transactions: SCA "when a payer creates, amends, or initiates for the first time, a series of recurring transactions with the same amount and with the same payee", not for subsequent ones;
  - Article 16 low-value remote transactions: amount "does not exceed EUR 30" and cumulative amount since the last SCA "does not exceed EUR 100" or no more than five consecutive transactions;
  - Article 17 secure corporate payment processes;
  - Article 18 transaction risk analysis (TRA), with the Annex's reference fraud rates: for remote card payments **0.01% up to EUR 500, 0.06% up to EUR 250, 0.13% up to EUR 100** (credit transfers 0.005% / 0.01% / 0.015%); a PSP using TRA must have its model "audited ... at a minimum on a yearly basis".
- The exemption is the PSP's or issuer's to claim, not the merchant's: Stripe notes that "banks can still request that the customer complete authentication" even for low-risk transactions and that "Exemptions aren't guaranteed, and off-session payments might still require authentication by the bank" [VERIFIED: https://docs.stripe.com/strong-customer-authentication, n.d., accessed 2026-11].

### 3.2 3-D Secure 2 and the liability shift

- EMVCo: "EMV 3-D Secure (EMV 3DS) helps payment card issuers and merchants around the world prevent card-not-present (CNP) fraud"; low-risk transactions let "consumers simply click 'Buy' and the payment is approved", higher-risk ones require "a challenge, such as a one-time passcode"; "EMVCo recommends that v2.2 (or higher) should be considered to access the optimum functionality"; draft v2.4.0 specifications were published 4 November 2026 [VERIFIED: https://www.emvco.com/emv-technologies/3-d-secure/, n.d., accessed 2026-11] ⚠.
- Liability shift: "If a cardholder disputes a 3DS payment as fraudulent, the liability typically shifts from you to the card issuer. If the card issuer applies exemptions, the payment isn't authenticated through 3D Secure, and liability shift doesn't apply" [VERIFIED: https://docs.stripe.com/strong-customer-authentication, n.d., accessed 2026-11]. The trade-off is explicit: an exemption buys conversion at the price of fraud liability [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT].
- SCA "in effect as of September 14, 2019" applies if the business is in the EEA, serves EEA customers and accepts cards; "Integrations that aren't SCA-ready ... might see high rates of declines from banks that enforce SCA" [VERIFIED: same Stripe page].

### 3.3 Merchant-initiated transactions (MIT)

- Stripe: "An MIT is a transaction that you initiate without direct participation of your customer, based on a prior agreement with that customer authorizing you to store and use their credentials"; the merchant must keep "a record of each customer's agreement" covering frequency, how the amount is determined and the cancellation policy; "When a card's brand changes, you can't charge it for any MITs until you get a new cardholder agreement" [VERIFIED: https://docs.stripe.com/payments/cits-and-mits, n.d., accessed 2026-11].
- For SCA, "Stripe marks subsequent off-session payments as a merchant-initiated transaction (MIT). These transactions require an agreement (also known as a *mandate*)"; the first on-session payment must be authenticated and flagged `setup_future_usage=off_session` [VERIFIED: https://docs.stripe.com/strong-customer-authentication, n.d., accessed 2026-11]. MITs matter for commerce beyond subscriptions: split shipments captured later, pre-orders, and "charge on dispatch" all rely on an SCA-authenticated first interaction [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT].

### 3.4 PSD3 and the Payment Services Regulation (status, November 2026) ⚠

- "On 27 November 2025, the European Parliament and the Council of the EU announced that they have reached a provisional political agreement" on PSD3 and the PSR. Headline content: online platforms must remove reported fraudulent financial advertising or face liability to reimbursing PSPs; "Merchants must ensure their trading name matches the name on customers' bank statements"; open-banking access obstacles are prohibited; surcharging on non-consumer cards "would continue to be permitted". "There is an 18-month implementation period (although the Council proposed to extend this to 24 months)", so "the earliest PSD3/PSR is likely to become applicable is H2 of 2027", possibly "early 2028" [WIDELY REPORTED: https://www.hlc.com/en/publications/final-texts-for-psd3-and-psr-awaited-as-european-parliament-and-council-of-eu-announce-provisional, 2025-12-15].
- "On 23 April 2026, the Council of the EU published final compromise texts" (ST-8222-2026 for PSD3, ST-8221-2026 for the PSR), to be "formally adopted by the Council and the Parliament" before publication in the Official Journal [WIDELY REPORTED: https://www.algoodbody.com/insights-publications/psd-3-psr-final-compromise-texts-published, 2026-04-24]. Whether the plenary vote, Council adoption and OJ publication have happened by 7 November 2026 could not be verified in this session; writers should check EUR-Lex before stating that PSD3/PSR "is adopted" [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT].

### 3.5 Instant Payments Regulation (EU) 2024/886

- Regulation (EU) 2024/886 of 13 March 2024 (OJ 19 March 2024) requires euro-area PSPs to offer *receiving* instant euro credit transfers by **9 January 2025** and *sending* them by **9 October 2025**; PSPs in non-euro Member States by 9 January 2027 (receive) and 9 July 2027 (send). Charges for instant transfers "shall not be higher than the charges ... in respect of ... other credit transfers of corresponding type", and the payee-verification service (Article 5c, the IBAN-name check) "shall be provided to all PSUs free of charge", by 9 January 2025 for euro-area PSPs [VERIFIED: http://publications.europa.eu/resource/celex/32024R0886, published 2024-03-19 (OJ L 2024/886)].
- Consequence for commerce: instant credit transfer becomes a plausible checkout method (and a cheaper refund rail) across the euro area, and the ECB already records instant payments at 5% of online payments [VERIFIED: ECB SPACE 2024, as above]; iDEAL's successor products and the European Payments Initiative's Wero ride on it [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT] ⚠.

**For newcomers.** *SCA*: the EU rule that an online payment must be confirmed with two of three factors (something you know, have, are) unless an exemption applies. *3-D Secure 2*: the card-network protocol that performs SCA, either silently ("frictionless") or with a challenge in the bank's app. *Exemption*: a reason not to challenge (low value, low-risk acquirer, trusted merchant, merchant-initiated), claimed by the PSP and granted or refused by the issuer. *MIT*: a payment the merchant triggers later under an agreement, such as charging on dispatch or a subscription.

**Architecture implications** [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT]. Authentication is an *asynchronous detour* in checkout: the shopper leaves for the bank's app and may never come back, so the payment state must survive a page reload and the order must not be created twice when they do. **Starting**: the hosted payment page handles 3DS; nothing to build. **Growing**: store the PSP's authentication result and liability-shift flag on the order so support can see why a dispute was lost; capture on dispatch needs an MIT-capable first authorization. **Omnichannel**: terminals do SCA by chip-and-PIN and contactless limits, online by 3DS; a unified "payments" view must not assume one authentication model. **Group**: regulatory-calendar ownership (PSD3/PSR, IPR, 3DS versions) becomes a platform-team responsibility with a tracked list, because every storefront inherits the consequences.

## PART 4: Idempotency, webhooks, status models and reconciliation

### 4.1 Idempotency keys

- Stripe: "The API supports idempotency for safely retrying requests without accidentally performing the same operation twice." The client sends an `Idempotency-Key` header; "Stripe saves the status code and body of the first request made with a given idempotency key after endpoint execution begins. Retries with the same key return the saved response, including `500` errors." Keys are "up to 255 characters long"; "we suggest using V4 UUIDs"; "Avoid using sensitive data (for example, email addresses or personal identifiers) as idempotency keys"; keys may be pruned "after they're at least 24 hours old"; "The idempotency layer compares incoming parameters to those of the original request and errors if they're not the same"; keys apply to POST only [VERIFIED: https://docs.stripe.com/api/idempotent_requests, n.d., accessed 2026-11].
- The same discipline is needed on the merchant's side: an order-creation call from checkout, a refund request from a returns portal and a capture request from the WMS should all carry a key derived from the business event (order id + action), so a retry after a timeout cannot create a second order, refund or capture. Mollie's refund advice ("do not retry automatically. Check the payment's refunds through the API") is the manual version of the same rule [VERIFIED: https://docs.mollie.com/docs/refunds.md, n.d., accessed 2026-11; synthesis ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT].

### 4.2 Webhooks and why orders depend on them

- Stripe: "Stripe attempts to deliver events to your destination for up to three days with an exponential back off in live mode"; "Stripe doesn't guarantee the delivery of events in the order that they're generated"; "Don't use `created` to determine event order or whether you've already processed an event. Track event IDs to identify duplicate deliveries instead"; "Webhook endpoints might occasionally receive the same event more than once"; the endpoint "must quickly return a successful status code (2xx) before any complex logic" and should "process incoming events with an asynchronous queue"; every event is signed (`Stripe-Signature`, HMAC-SHA256 with a timestamp, default tolerance 5 minutes) and "Without verification, an attacker could send fake webhook events to your endpoint to trigger actions like fulfilling orders" [VERIFIED: https://docs.stripe.com/webhooks, n.d., accessed 2026-11].
- Mollie's design makes the payload useless to a forger: the webhook carries "a single POST-parameter called `id`" and "Since the status is not transmitted in the webhook, fake calls to your webhook will never result in orders being processed without being actually paid"; "When Mollie calls your webhook, you should fetch the latest status and process it if the status was changed"; retries: "In total we will call your webhook 10 times with an increasing interval" (at 0, 1, 3, 7, 15, 31 minutes, 1, 2, 4 and 26 hours) and "If after the 10th call we still do not get a `200 OK` response (which is after 26 hours), we will stop trying"; calls "time out after 15 seconds" [VERIFIED: https://docs.mollie.com/docs/webhooks, n.d., accessed 2026-11]. Mollie's newer best-practice page adds HMAC verification with "a timing-safe comparison method" and re-checking payloads against the Webhook Events API because "an attacker who gets access to the secret may be able to generate the right signature" [VERIFIED: https://docs.mollie.com/reference/webhooks-best-practices.md, n.d., accessed 2026-11].
- Adyen: webhooks "contain a snapshot of the resource"; for methods such as iDEAL they deliver "the final outcome as soon as it is available"; for disputes, reports and verification updates "Webhooks are the only way to be automatically notified"; the endpoint must "accept webhooks that you receive with a 2xx HTTP status code" [VERIFIED: https://docs.adyen.com/development-resources/webhooks/, n.d., accessed 2026-11].
- Why orders depend on them: Stripe's fulfilment guide states "Webhooks are required for fulfillment ... Automatic fulfillment with webhooks is required if you sell subscriptions or accept payment methods with delayed success notification, because their subsequent state changes only after the Checkout Session completes", while the landing-page redirect is for immediacy ("webhooks can sometimes be delayed") [VERIFIED: https://docs.stripe.com/checkout/fulfillment.md?ui=full-page, n.d., accessed 2026-11]. In commercetools' flow the order already exists in `Pending` and the webhook decides whether it is fulfilled or cancelled [VERIFIED: commercetools payments lifecycle, as above].

### 4.3 Payment status models

- Mollie's statuses: `open` (customer can still pay; no webhook), `pending` (temporary), `authorized`, `paid`, `canceled`, `expired`, `failed`; webhooks fire for all but `open`. Expiry depends on method: iDEAL 15 minutes, cards 30 minutes, PayPal 6 hours, Klarna 48 hours, bank transfer 12 days; "It is not a good idea to predict payment expiry. Best wait until your webhook is called and fetch the status as usual" [VERIFIED: https://docs.mollie.com/docs/handling-payment-status.md, n.d., accessed 2026-11].
- Stripe's PaymentIntent moves through `requires_payment_method`, `requires_confirmation`, `requires_action` (3DS), `processing`, `requires_capture`, `succeeded`, `canceled`; "A PaymentIntent can't be canceled after it has succeeded" [VERIFIED: https://docs.stripe.com/refunds, n.d., accessed 2026-11 (cancel section); status list from https://docs.stripe.com/payments/place-a-hold-on-a-payment-method, n.d.].
- An order system should map these to its *own* small vocabulary (pending, authorized, paid, failed, refunded-partially, refunded, disputed) and keep the PSP's raw status and reference beside it; the mapping is part of the PSP adapter, not of the order model [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT].

### 4.4 Payout reconciliation and reporting APIs

- Stripe's Payout reconciliation report "helps you match the payouts you receive in your bank account with the batches of payments and other transactions that they relate to"; the itemized CSV lists "every payment, refund, dispute, fee, and other balance transaction included in the payout", grouped by reporting category; "Report data follows a separate processing schedule, so a payout might arrive before its reconciliation data is ready"; the Reporting API emits `reporting.report_type.updated` events; instant payouts cannot be itemised ("You're responsible for reconciling instant payouts"); CSVs can carry the merchant's own metadata "to match Stripe transactions with records in your accounting system", and columns include `order_id`, `payment_intent_id`, `charge_id`, `automatic_payout_id`, `fee`, `gross`, `net`, `trace_id` (the bank's reference) [VERIFIED: https://docs.stripe.com/reports/payout-reconciliation, n.d., accessed 2026-11].
- Adyen's batch-level reconciliation uses the Aggregate Settlement Details Report "to find settled batches and other account movements for a specified date range", keyed by Batch Number; Net Credit is the amount "settled to your bank account after deducting bank and/or card commission fees and DCC markup"; the worked example shows batch 608 with net settlement EUR 5,847.53, commission EUR 114.27 across 31 transactions [VERIFIED: https://docs.adyen.com/reporting/settlement-reconciliation/batch-level, n.d., accessed 2026-11]. The transaction-level Settlement details report is generated per payout batch [WIDELY REPORTED: https://docs.adyen.com/essentials/settlement-details-report.md, n.d. (search snippet, page not fetched)].
- Mollie's Settlements API gives "programmatic access to your settlement data" with aggregated totals per method and cost type and the individual payments, refunds, chargebacks and captures per settlement [VERIFIED: https://docs.mollie.com/docs/settlement-report.md, n.d., accessed 2026-11].
- Stripe's reconciliation primer (Dutch edition): reconciliation is a process in which transaction data is compared with what is recorded in the books; differences "can result from timing differences, errors, or fraud" (translated); it recommends reconciling regularly ("usually monthly") while noting Stripe allows daily cash tracking, automating a large part of the process, and investigating differences immediately [VERIFIED: https://stripe.com/resources/more/payment-reconciliation-101, updated 2025-02-21; translated from Dutch].

**For newcomers.** *Idempotency key*: a unique token sent with a request so that a retry of the same request does nothing twice. *Webhook*: an HTTP call the PSP makes to the merchant's server when something happened (payment succeeded, refund failed, dispute opened); the merchant must acknowledge fast, verify the signature, and expect duplicates and disorder. *Payout reconciliation*: matching one bank deposit from the PSP to the hundreds of orders, refunds, fees and chargebacks it contains.

**Architecture implications** [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT]. Three facts drive the design: delivery is at-least-once, order is not guaranteed, and the webhook may lie (if unsigned). So: (1) persist every event id and treat re-delivery as a no-op; (2) fetch the resource from the PSP rather than trusting the payload when the decision matters (Mollie makes this mandatory); (3) acknowledge first, process from a queue; (4) run a daily reconciliation job that lists orders whose payment state has not reached a terminal state within the method's expiry window and queries the PSP, because webhooks stop retrying (26 hours at Mollie, 3 days at Stripe). **Starting**: the platform does this for you; the only reconciliation is finance matching payouts to a month. **Growing**: the first custom integration (returns portal, ERP connector) is where duplicate refunds and double fulfilment appear; one writer per money action. **Omnichannel**: payouts from the online PSP, the terminal provider, marketplaces and B2B receivables are four different reconciliation feeds; a payments ledger inside the merchant (or RECONCILED-style sub-ledger) earns its place. **Group**: PSP reporting APIs are consumed by a central finance-data pipeline, not by each brand's shop.

## PART 5: PCI DSS

### 5.1 Versions and dates

- PCI DSS v4.0 was announced on 31 March 2022 ("The next evolution of the standard - PCI DSS v4.0 - is now available") [VERIFIED: https://blog.pcisecuritystandards.org/pci-dss-v4-0-resource-hub, published 2022-03-31]. v3.2.1 retired on **31 March 2024** [WIDELY REPORTED: PCI SSC blog title "PCI DSS v3.2.1 is Retiring on 31 March 2024", linked from the resource hub; Adyen: v4.0.1 "has replaced v3.2.1"].
- v4.0.1 was published on **11 June 2024**: "There are no additional or deleted requirements in this revision"; "PCI DSS v4.0 will be retired on 31 December 2024"; "This limited revision does not impact the effective date of these new requirements", i.e. the future-dated requirements [VERIFIED: https://blog.pcisecuritystandards.org/just-published-pci-dss-v4-0-1, published 2024-06-11]. Adyen: "PCI DSS v4.0.1 was released on June 11, 2024" [VERIFIED: https://docs.adyen.com/development-resources/pci-dss-compliance-guide/, n.d., accessed 2026-11].
- The future-dated requirements, including 6.4.3, 11.6.1 and 12.3.1, took effect on **31 March 2025** [VERIFIED: https://blog.pcisecuritystandards.org/important-updates-announced-for-merchants-validating-to-self-assessment-questionnaire-a, published 2025-01-30].
- Scope: the standard applies to "Entities that store, process, or transmit cardholder data (CHD) and/or sensitive authentication data (SAD) or could impact the security of the cardholder data environment (CDE)", including "merchants, processors, acquirers, issuers, and service providers"; enforcement "is at the discretion of organizations that manage compliance programs, such as a payment brand, acquirer, or other entity" [VERIFIED: https://www.pcisecuritystandards.org/standards/pci-dss/, n.d., accessed 2026-11].

### 5.2 Scope reduction: hosted fields, iframes and SAQ types

- Stripe: a business that handles card data directly "might be required to meet more than 300 security controls in PCI DSS"; its low-risk integrations "securely collect and transmit payment information directly to Stripe without it passing through your servers, reducing your PCI obligations"; the card type, last four digits and expiry "isn't subject to PCI compliance, so you can store any of these properties"; "Payment pages must use a recent version (TLS 1.2 or above)" [VERIFIED: https://docs.stripe.com/security/guide, n.d., accessed 2026-11].
- Stripe's PCI guide maps integrations to self-assessment questionnaires: Checkout or Elements → **SAQ A**; mobile SDK (under conditions) → SAQ A; Stripe.js v2 (card data passed from the merchant's own form) → **SAQ A-EP**; Terminal → SAQ C; Dashboard manual entry → SAQ C-VT; direct API → **SAQ D**. Merchant levels: Level 1 over 6 million Visa/Mastercard transactions a year (annual Report on Compliance by a QSA), Level 2 1–6 million, Level 3 20,000–1 million; "300+ security controls" across "12 security domains" [VERIFIED: https://stripe.com/guides/pci-compliance, n.d., accessed 2026-11 (Dutch edition; translated)].
- Adyen: with encrypted/hosted solutions "you never see and never have access to unencrypted cardholder data", but the merchant remains "responsible for making sure that cardholder data is secure and protected before the data reaches Adyen" [VERIFIED: https://docs.adyen.com/development-resources/pci-dss-compliance-guide/, n.d., accessed 2026-11].
- The mechanism behind SAQ A is that the card fields are rendered inside an iframe (or a redirect) served from the PSP's domain, so the merchant's page never sees the PAN; "hosted fields" is the industry term; Stripe's Content Security Policy list shows the frames come from `js.stripe.com` / `checkout.stripe.com` [VERIFIED: https://docs.stripe.com/security/guide, n.d. (CSP section); mechanism explanation ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT].

### 5.3 Requirements 6.4.3 and 11.6.1: payment page scripts

- 6.4.3 covers "payment page scripts" (an inventory of all scripts on the payment page, authorisation and integrity assurance) and 11.6.1 "change- and tamper-detection mechanisms" for the HTTP headers and content of the payment page as received by the browser [VERIFIED: https://docs.adyen.com/development-resources/pci-dss-compliance-guide/saq-a-eligibility, n.d., accessed 2026-11 (naming); requirement content paraphrased from the standard, ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT].
- On 30 January 2025 the PCI SSC removed 6.4.3, 11.6.1 and 12.3.1 from **SAQ A** in response to feedback about "the complexity of implementing the new e-commerce security Requirements 6.4.3 and 11.6.1", replacing them with an eligibility criterion that the merchant must "confirm their site is not susceptible to attacks from scripts that could affect the merchant's e-commerce system(s)"; the change "do[es] not remove or diminish the underlying requirements within PCI DSS"; the new SAQ A applied from 31 March 2025 [VERIFIED: https://blog.pcisecuritystandards.org/important-updates-announced-for-merchants-validating-to-self-assessment-questionnaire-a, published 2025-01-30] ⚠.
- Adyen's SAQ A eligibility page: payment page elements must "originate only and directly from a PCI DSS compliant Third-Party Service Provider (TPSP) or payment processor", the merchant must confirm the site "is not susceptible to attacks from scripts", and 6.4.3 and 11.6.1 "still apply to merchants submitting SAQ D and to merchants who must present an Attestation of Compliance for onsite assessment" [VERIFIED: https://docs.adyen.com/development-resources/pci-dss-compliance-guide/saq-a-eligibility, n.d., accessed 2026-11].
- Stripe's security guide gives the underlying risk in one sentence: "Including JavaScript from other sites makes your security dependent on theirs ... If they're ever compromised, an attacker could execute arbitrary code on your page" [VERIFIED: https://docs.stripe.com/security/guide, n.d., accessed 2026-11]. This is the "Magecart" skimming class of attack that 6.4.3/11.6.1 target [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT].

**For newcomers.** *PCI DSS*: the card industry's security standard for anyone who touches card data; the merchant's acquirer or PSP decides how it is enforced. *SAQ*: the self-assessment questionnaire a smaller merchant fills in; SAQ A is the short one for merchants whose payment page is fully hosted by the PSP, SAQ D the long one for merchants that handle card numbers. *Hosted fields / iframe*: the PSP draws the card-number box inside the merchant's page from its own domain, so the merchant never sees the number.

**Architecture implications** [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT]. The rule "never store, log or transmit a card number" is a scope decision, not a security feature: it keeps the whole platform out of the cardholder-data environment. **Starting**: hosted checkout → SAQ A, nothing to build. **Growing**: custom checkout with hosted fields keeps SAQ A, but every third-party script on the payment page (analytics, chat, A/B testing, tag managers) is now a liability; keep an inventory and a Content Security Policy. **Omnichannel**: terminals bring SAQ C/P2PE and card-present rules; a unified customer-payment-method vault must be the PSP's token vault, not the merchant's. **Group**: a shared payment service that *does* touch PANs (for routing between PSPs) moves the group into SAQ D or a Report on Compliance, which is a strategic cost to be chosen deliberately.

## PART 6: Fraud screening basics

### 6.1 Card testing (enumeration)

- Stripe: "Card testing is a type of fraudulent activity where someone tries to determine whether stolen card information is valid"; attackers "use scripts to test a large amount of card information at once"; consequences are disputes, "Higher decline rates" that "damage the reputation of your business with card issuers", "Additional fees ... such as authorization fees", "Infrastructure strain", and enrolment in "Card Monitoring Programs". Symptoms: "A spike in failed or blocked payments", "A spike in requests with 402 errors", small payments "with nonsensical customer names and emails". Mitigations: a PSP-recommended integration with "rate limiters, AI models, CAPTCHA triggers", requiring login or session validation ("The easier it's for fraudulent actors to reach your payment form (for example, using guest checkout), the easier it's for them to execute card testing attacks"), rate limits per IP, velocity rules, and never exposing the secret key [VERIFIED: https://docs.stripe.com/disputes/prevention/card-testing, n.d., accessed 2026-11].
- Visa's VAMP now monitors enumeration separately: "VAMP Enumeration Ratio ... ≥ 2000 bps" (20%) and "VAMP Enumeration Transaction Count ... ≥ 300,000" per month [VERIFIED: https://corporate.visa.com/content/dam/VCOM/corporate/visa-perspectives/security-and-trust/documents/visa-acquirer-monitoring-program-fact-sheet-2025.pdf, published 2025-05 (file dated 2025-05-14)]. Stripe adds that "Visa doesn't assess fines for VAMP enumeration monitoring" [VERIFIED: https://docs.stripe.com/disputes/monitoring-programs, n.d., accessed 2026-11].

### 6.2 Dispute ratios and network thresholds

- Visa consolidated its fraud and dispute programmes into one VAMP, with "Updates to the program thresholds ... effective 1 June 2025" and an advisory period ending 30 September 2025. "VAMP Ratio = Count of [Fraud (TC40) + Disputes (TC15)] ÷ Count of Settled Transactions (TC05)", excluding disputes resolved through pre-dispute solutions and TC40 fraud qualified for Compelling Evidence 3.0. Acquirer portfolios are "Above Standard if its VAMP ratio is ≥50bps and ... Excessive if ≥70bps". Excessive-merchant thresholds: "VAMP Ratio: ≥220bps" with "Monthly count of fraud and disputes: ≥1,500" in AP, Canada, EU and US (LAC ≥150bps; CEMEA ≥220bps with ≥150 cases and ≥USD 75,000); footnote: "Excessive Merchant threshold reduced to >=150bps in AP, Canada, EU, and U.S. regions on 1 April 2026" [VERIFIED: Visa VAMP fact sheet, as above] ⚠.
- Stripe's rendering of the current merchant thresholds: non-compliant at VAMP count 5 and ratio **0.5%**; excessive at count 1,500 and ratio **1.5%** (2.2% in CEMEA); "The same fraudulent transaction might appear in both the TC40 and TC15 reports. Any transaction that appears in both reports will be counted twice"; early fraud warnings count even when the payment was refunded; Visa identifies accounts by statement descriptor and acquirer, so one business can be several Visa "accounts" [VERIFIED: https://docs.stripe.com/disputes/monitoring-programs, n.d., accessed 2026-11] ⚠.
- Mastercard's Excessive Chargeback Program: ECM at 100–299 chargebacks and a 1.5–2.99% rate, HECM at 300+ and 3%, with fines starting in month two (USD 1,000) and rising to USD 100,000 (ECM) or USD 200,000 (HECM) after 19 months; Mastercard's rate divides this month's chargebacks by *last* month's transactions, Visa uses the same month [VERIFIED: same Stripe page].
- "The credit card processing industry standard recognizes dispute activity above 0.75% as excessive"; Stripe distinguishes *dispute activity* (disputes received this period ÷ payments this period, the networks' measure) from *dispute rate* (disputes on this period's payments) [VERIFIED: https://docs.stripe.com/disputes/measuring, n.d., accessed 2026-11].
- Stripe's prevention advice that touches architecture: "Consider using separate authorization and capture in combination with review rules. Issuers are required to report possible fraud for a captured payment, even if it gets refunded, but aren't required to report it for a payment authorization" [VERIFIED: https://docs.stripe.com/disputes/monitoring-programs, n.d., accessed 2026-11].

### 6.3 PSP risk tools

- PSPs expose rule engines over the signals they see: Stripe Radar offers "custom rules" including "velocity rules", built-in "bank checks" such as CVC, and expects the integration to send IP address, email, name and billing address because "the more data your integration provides, the more successful card testing prevention can be" [VERIFIED: https://docs.stripe.com/disputes/prevention/card-testing, n.d., accessed 2026-11]. Adyen's dispute flow includes automatic defence of refunded or liability-shifted chargebacks [VERIFIED: Adyen dispute process page, as above]. What is scored inside these models is not public [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT].

**For newcomers.** *Card testing*: bots trying thousands of stolen card numbers against a checkout to see which still work; it costs the merchant authorization fees and reputation even when every attempt is declined. *Dispute ratio*: disputes plus reported fraud divided by transactions in a month; above about 0.5–1.5% the card networks fine the merchant through its PSP. *Early fraud warning (TC40)*: the issuer's report that a payment was fraudulent, which counts against the merchant even without a chargeback.

**Architecture implications** [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT]. Fraud is a ratio, so defence belongs *before* authorization (bot and velocity controls at the edge, CAPTCHA on guest checkout, rate limits on the payment endpoint) and *between* authorization and capture (review queue; cancel instead of refund). **Starting**: rely on the PSP's defaults; watch the decline rate. **Growing**: the ledger's Card-Testing Night (48,000 attempts, €2,900 in fees, account paused 40 minutes) is the typical first incident; add edge rate limits and a kill switch that can disable guest card payments without a deploy. **Omnichannel**: fraud signals from stores (returns abuse, gift-card draining) and online need one customer view; the PSP sees only its own channel. **Group**: a shared risk service feeding PSP rules across brands, with the statement-descriptor convention managed centrally because Visa aggregates by it.

## PART 7: Tax

### 7.1 VAT rates and the EU framework

- EU rules: the standard VAT rate "must be no less than 15%, but there is no maximum" (Article 97 of Directive 2006/112/EC); since the April 2022 reform (Directive (EU) 2022/542) Member States may apply up to two reduced rates "as low as 5%" for up to 24 Annex III categories, plus one super-reduced rate below 5% and one zero rate for up to seven basic-needs categories [VERIFIED: https://taxation-customs.ec.europa.eu/taxation/vat/vat-rates_en, n.d., accessed 2026-11].
- **Netherlands**: 21% general tariff ("applies to all other products and services"), 9% low tariff (food, medicines, books including e-books, newspapers), 0% for exports and intra-EU supplies; "From 1 January 2026, the VAT rate for overnight accommodation is 21%" [VERIFIED: https://business.gov.nl/regulation/vat-rates-exemptions/, n.d., accessed 2026-11].
- **Germany**: § 12(1) UStG "Die Steuer beträgt für jeden steuerpflichtigen Umsatz 19 Prozent der Bemessungsgrundlage" (the tax is 19% of the taxable base for every taxable transaction); § 12(2) "Die Steuer ermäßigt sich auf sieben Prozent" (reduced to 7%) for listed supplies [VERIFIED: https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/ustg_1980/__12.html, consolidated text, accessed 2026-11; translated from German].
- **Belgium**: "The standard VAT rate is 21%"; reduced rates of 12% (restaurant services excluding beverages, social housing; "as of 1 March 2026 the reduced rate of 6% has been increased to 12%" for hotels and camping) and 6% (food, pharmaceuticals, passenger transport); exports and intra-Community supplies are "VAT exempt with credit ('zero-rated')" [WIDELY REPORTED: https://taxsummaries.pwc.com/belgium/corporate/other-taxes, reviewed 2026-09-10] ⚠.

### 7.2 Cross-border VAT: the July 2021 e-commerce package

- B2B goods within the EU: with a valid customer VAT number "you do not charge VAT"; the buyer declares and pays VAT at its own country's rate "as if you had sold the goods yourself"; VAT numbers are checked in VIES. B2B services: the seller "don't usually need to charge" VAT and the customer accounts for it "using the reverse charge procedure" [VERIFIED: https://europa.eu/youreurope/business/taxation/vat/cross-border-vat/index_en.htm, n.d., accessed 2026-11].
- B2C distance sales: "A VAT threshold of EUR 10 000 applies to distance sales for customers in the EU"; above it the seller charges the customer's country's VAT and may use the One Stop Shop to "register in one EU country for the declaration and payment of VAT on all distance sales of goods" [VERIFIED: same page]. The OSS page: the MOSS "has been extended to become a One Stop Shop (OSS) as from 1 July 2021"; the threshold applies while the supplier is established in one Member State and cross-border B2C sales do "not exceed EUR 10 000 (without VAT) in the current and the preceding calendar year"; "The VAT return is submitted quarterly in the non-Union and in the Union scheme"; the import scheme (IOSS) covers "goods in consignments of an intrinsic value not exceeding EUR 150" with monthly returns and an EU intermediary for non-EU sellers [VERIFIED: https://vat-one-stop-shop.ec.europa.eu/one-stop-shop_en, n.d., accessed 2026-11].
- Consequence: a Dutch merchant selling €235 to a Belgian consumer charges Belgian VAT (21%) once past €10,000 of EU-wide cross-border B2C sales, reports it in the Dutch OSS return, and must therefore know the *destination* rate per product category at checkout; the same jacket sold to a Belgian wholesale customer with a VAT number carries 0% with the reverse-charge mention [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT, applying the rules above].

### 7.3 US sales tax after Wayfair, economic nexus and tax engines

- *South Dakota v. Wayfair, Inc.*, decided **21 June 2018**, 5–4, opinion by Justice Kennedy: "the physical presence rule of *Quill* is unsound and incorrect"; South Dakota's law applied to sellers that "deliver more than $100,000 of goods or services into the State or engage in 200 or more separate transactions" annually [VERIFIED: https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/17-494, published 2018-06-21] **(evidence age: 8 years; the ruling, not a system)**.
- The Streamlined Sales Tax Governing Board: after *Wayfair* "states can require sellers to collect and remit sales or use tax on sales delivered to locations within their state"; sellers must "Review the state's remote seller threshold chart and check with each state"; 23 full member states and one associate member (Tennessee) [VERIFIED: https://www.streamlinedsalestax.org/for-businesses/remote-seller-faqs, n.d., accessed 2026-11]. Thresholds vary by state and several have dropped the transaction-count test; marketplace-facilitator laws make the marketplace, not the seller, collect [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT] ⚠.
- Tax engines: Avalara claims AvaTax "continuously calculates line-item taxes in under 10 milliseconds on average" over "more than 900,000 tax rules across 12,000+ U.S. sales and use tax jurisdictions and 190+ countries", with "more than 1,400 signed partner integrations" and "Continuous economic nexus tracking" [VERIFIED: https://www.avalara.com/us/en/products/calculations.html, n.d., accessed 2026-11; vendor claims]. TaxJar's `POST /v2/taxes` "Calculate sales tax for an order" takes from/to addresses, amount, shipping, `nexus_addresses` and `line_items` with `product_tax_code`, and returns `amount_to_collect`, `rate`, `has_nexus` and a `breakdown` by state, county, city and special district; "In origin-based states, sales tax should be collected based on where you, the seller, are located"; every calculation "results in a 'transaction'" for billing [VERIFIED: https://developers.taxjar.com/api/reference/, n.d., accessed 2026-11]. Vertex (O Series) is the third widely used engine, strong in ERP-attached enterprise use; its product pages could not be fetched [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT].
- The engine pattern: a stateless *calculate* call at cart/checkout (an estimate that is not recorded) and a *committed* document at order/invoice time that feeds returns and filing; Avalara's developer guide distinguishes these as SalesOrder vs SalesInvoice document types, but the pages did not render in this session [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT].

### 7.4 Tax-inclusive vs exclusive pricing; calculation at checkout vs invoice

- Stripe Tax: "Tax-exclusive prices are common in the US and Canada and for B2B sales in other countries"; "Tax-inclusive prices are common for B2C sales in many markets outside the US. When set to inclusive, the amount your customer pays remains constant, regardless of the tax amount"; with reverse charge "If no tax applies, the tax-inclusive price is the unit price. If the tax amount is positive, the unit price is lower"; the "Automatic" default is "exclusive pricing for USD and CAD and inclusive pricing for all other currencies"; once set, "You can't change `tax_behavior` after it's been set" [VERIFIED: https://docs.stripe.com/tax/products-prices-tax-codes-tax-behavior, n.d., accessed 2026-11].
- Shipping-fee taxation follows either the "Proportional Allocation Method" (shipping split across items' rates) or the "Highest Tax Rate Method" [VERIFIED: same page]. Discounts raise the same allocation question across items with different rates, which is why discount allocation (chapter 08) is a tax problem as much as a promotions problem [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT].
- Checkout vs invoice: the tax shown at checkout is a promise computed from the ship-to address, the product's tax category and the customer's status; the legally binding amount is the one on the invoice, computed when the supply happens (dispatch) — if the address, the basket (split shipment, out-of-stock line) or the rate changed in between, the invoice may differ from the checkout figure, and the order system must either re-run the engine at invoicing or freeze the checkout result as the tax basis and reconcile differences [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT].

**For newcomers.** *VAT*: Europe's consumption tax, charged at every sale and reclaimed by business buyers, so a B2C price includes it and a B2B invoice shows it separately. *OSS (One Stop Shop)*: the EU scheme (since July 2021) that lets a merchant report VAT owed to every EU country in one quarterly return at home, once its cross-border B2C sales exceed €10,000 a year. *Reverse charge*: for B2B sales to another EU country the seller charges 0% and the buyer books the VAT itself. *Economic nexus*: in the US, the obligation to collect a state's sales tax once sales into that state pass a threshold (South Dakota: $100,000 or 200 transactions), regardless of physical presence. *Tax engine*: a service (Avalara, Vertex, TaxJar) that knows the rates and rules and answers "how much tax on this line, shipped there, to this customer".

**Architecture implications** [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT]. Tax is determined by data the commerce platform owns (ship-to, product category, customer type, VAT number) and rules it should not own (rates, thresholds, nexus). **Starting**: the hosted platform's built-in tax with inclusive EU prices; one country, one rate table. **Growing**: the €10,000 OSS threshold is crossed early; destination rates per product category must be configured; B2B needs VIES validation and reverse-charge invoices. **Omnichannel**: stores (point-of-sale VAT of the store's country), marketplaces (which may collect VAT as deemed supplier), wholesale and web all feed one VAT return; a tax engine or a disciplined rate service behind every channel stops four implementations of the same rule. **Group**: US expansion brings nexus tracking and exclusive pricing; the pricing model (inclusive vs exclusive) must be a property of the market, not of the product.

## PART 8: Invoicing and accounting

### 8.1 What an invoice must contain

- **Netherlands** (Belastingdienst): supplier's name, address and VAT identification number; customer's name and address; "invoice number and invoice date"; date of supply; quantity and type of goods or nature of services; per rate or exemption the unit price excluding VAT, reductions, the VAT rate, the amount excluding VAT and the VAT amount in euros; "Your VAT identification number must also be included on the invoice in cases of reverse-charging" and for intra-EU supplies; whether a special scheme applies [VERIFIED: https://www.belastingdienst.nl/wps/wcm/connect/bldcontenten/belastingdienst/business/vat/vat_in_the_netherlands/vat_administration/invoice_requirements, n.d., accessed 2026-11].
- **Germany** (§ 14 UStG): an invoice is "jedes Dokument, mit dem über eine Lieferung oder sonstige Leistung abgerechnet wird" (any document that settles a supply or service); an e-invoice is one "in einem strukturierten elektronischen Format" following the European standard under Directive 2014/55/EU (EN 16931); for B2B supplies between parties resident in Germany the invoice must be an e-invoice; a B2B invoice must be issued "innerhalb von sechs Monaten nach Ausführung der Leistung" (within six months of the supply). Mandatory contents (§ 14(4)): full names and addresses, tax number or VAT ID, "das Ausstellungsdatum" (issue date), "eine fortlaufende Nummer mit einer oder mehreren Zahlenreihen" (a consecutive number, unique, from one or more number ranges), quantity and type, time of supply, consideration broken down by rate, rate and tax amount, the self-billing word "Gutschrift" where applicable. Authenticity, integrity and legibility must be ensured, e.g. by qualified signature or EDI [VERIFIED: https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/ustg_1980/__14.html, consolidated text, accessed 2026-11; translated from German].
- The common core across both: a unique, sequential number; dates of issue and of supply; parties and VAT IDs; lines with net, rate and tax; and a reverse-charge or exemption mention where applicable. "Sequential" means gap-free within a series, which is why an invoice number is assigned by the system of record at the moment of posting, never pre-allocated by checkout [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT].

### 8.2 Immutability and credit notes

- Odoo (as a representative ERP): "Odoo initially creates invoices in Draft status"; "On confirmation, Odoo assigns each invoice a unique number from a defined sequence"; "Once confirmed, an invoice can no longer be updated"; posted entries can be locked by hash ("data inalterability") [VERIFIED: https://www.odoo.com/documentation/18.0/applications/finance/accounting/customer_invoices.html, 2024/2025 (18.0 docs), accessed 2026-11].
- "Issuing a credit/debit note is the only legal method for canceling, refunding, or modifying a validated invoice"; a credit note "generates a reverse entry that cancels out the journal items from the original invoice"; options are *Reverse* (draft credit note prefilled, allowing a partial refund) and *Reverse and Create invoice* (validated credit note reconciled with the original plus a new draft) [VERIFIED: https://www.odoo.com/documentation/18.0/applications/finance/accounting/customer_invoices/credit_notes.html, 18.0 docs, accessed 2026-11].
- Hence the commerce rule: a return never edits the invoice; it produces a credit note that references it, and a refund that references both; the three documents reconcile to zero [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT].

### 8.3 Retention periods

- **Germany**: § 14b(1) UStG requires the business to keep a copy of every invoice issued and every invoice received "acht Jahre" (eight years) [VERIFIED: https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/ustg_1980/__14b.html, consolidated text, accessed 2026-11]. § 147(3) AO: books, records, inventories and annual accounts "zehn Jahre" (ten years); accounting vouchers (*Buchungsbelege*) "acht Jahre" (eight years); other documents including commercial letters "sechs Jahre" (six years); periods run from the end of the calendar year of creation and are extended while the documents matter for an open assessment [VERIFIED: https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/ao_1977/__147.html, consolidated text, accessed 2026-11; translated from German]. The reduction of the voucher period from ten to eight years was made by the fourth Bürokratieentlastungsgesetz in 2025; the consolidated text shows only the current eight years [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT].
- **Netherlands**: the fiscal retention obligation (*bewaarplicht*) is seven years for the basic records (general ledger, debtors and creditors, stock, purchase and sales records, payroll) and ten years for records on immovable property; digital storage is permitted if the records remain accessible and verifiable within a reasonable time [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT — widely published by the Belastingdienst and business.gov.nl, but both pages returned 404 in this session; verify before quoting].
- For a commerce platform this means invoice PDFs, their structured data and the order and payment references behind them must survive platform migrations for 7–10 years, which is one reason the ERP, not the shop, is the invoice's system of record [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT].

### 8.4 E-invoicing at a glance

- Peppol "began in 2008 as a large-scale pilot", finalised in 2012 when OpenPeppol, "a non-profit international association established under Belgian law", took over; "At the heart of Peppol is the four-corner model" (buyer and supplier each connect through their own accredited provider); "Peppol is not a portal, or a provider of exchange services. Peppol is an enabler"; the Peppol Business Interoperability Specifications (Peppol BIS) standardise orders and invoices [VERIFIED: https://peppol.org/about/, n.d., accessed 2026-11].
- Germany's § 14 UStG already defines the e-invoice by reference to EN 16931 and mandates it for domestic B2B supplies (receipt obligation since 2025; issuing obligations phase in) [VERIFIED: § 14 UStG, as above; phasing per § 27 UStG, ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT]. Belgian B2B e-invoicing from 1 January 2026 and the Dutch position are covered in the `b2b` brief (brief-b2b-edi-erp).

### 8.5 Revenue recognition basics

- IFRS 15 "recognises revenue to depict the transfer of promised goods or services to the customer" in an amount reflecting the consideration expected; the five steps are "identify the contract(s) with a customer", "identify the performance obligations in the contract", "determine the transaction price", "allocate the transaction price to each performance obligation" and "recognise revenue when a performance obligation is satisfied"; issued May 2014, "effective for annual reporting periods beginning on or after 1 January 2018" [VERIFIED: https://www.ifrs.org/issued-standards/list-of-standards/ifrs-15-revenue-from-contracts-with-customers/, n.d., accessed 2026-11] **(evidence age: the standard is 12 years old and current)**.
- For physical goods the obligation is satisfied when control transfers (typically delivery or dispatch under the agreed terms), not when the order is placed or paid; a right of return creates a refund liability and a return asset [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT, standard application of IFRS 15.B20–B27].
- Gift cards and breakage (ASC 606-10-55-46 to 55-49, equivalent to IFRS 15.B44–B47): the sale of a gift card is a contract liability, not revenue; "Entities may recognize a breakage amount if they expect that customers will not exercise all their contractual rights" and "Breakage revenue would then be recognized in proportion to the actual performance that the entity provides"; otherwise "recognize revenue on breakage amounts when the likelihood of the customer exercising their remaining rights becomes remote"; where unredeemed amounts must be remitted to the state, "instead of recognizing revenue, the entity should recognize a liability for amounts payable to the government" [WIDELY REPORTED: https://www.revenuehub.org/article/unexercised-rights, n.d. (accessed 2026-11); IFRS mapping ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT].

### 8.6 What the ERP posts for an order, and month-end

- A confirmed customer invoice generates one journal entry, and payment is recorded when "its accounting entry is matched with a bank transaction" [VERIFIED: Odoo customer invoices, as above]. The entries behind a typical D2C order, with PSP settlement, are [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT, standard bookkeeping]:

| Event | Debit | Credit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Invoice (dispatch) | Accounts receivable (gross) | Revenue (net), VAT payable |
| Gift card sold | Bank/PSP receivable | Gift-card liability (no revenue, no VAT until redemption in NL/DE single-purpose rules aside) |
| PSP payout received | Bank (net), Payment fees (expense) | PSP receivable (gross of fees) |
| Refund after credit note | Revenue (reversal), VAT payable (reversal) | Accounts receivable / PSP receivable |
| Chargeback | Dispute expense or receivable, dispute fee | PSP receivable |

- Month-end reconciliation is a three-way match: the order system's invoices and credit notes, the PSP's settlement/payout reports (gross, fees, refunds, chargebacks per batch) and the bank statement (one line per payout, identified by the PSP's trace or batch reference). Stripe's `trace_id` column ("a unique identifier generated by bank for the Stripe payouts") and Adyen's batch number are the join keys [VERIFIED: Stripe payout reconciliation report and Adyen batch-level pages, as above; three-way framing ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT].
- Typical mismatch causes: timing (captured in March, paid out in April), fees netted inside the payout, refunds and chargebacks deducted from a later batch than the sale, reserves, currency conversion, failed refunds returning after 30 days, and orders refunded twice (the ledger's Double Refund) [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT, drawing on the Stripe and Adyen refund and dispute pages above].

**For newcomers.** *Invoice*: the legal document that states what was sold, to whom, for how much and with how much VAT; it gets a gap-free sequential number and can never be changed once issued. *Credit note*: the invoice's mirror image, issued to cancel or reduce it after a return or an error. *Revenue recognition*: the accounting rule for *when* a sale counts as income, generally when the goods are delivered, not when the order or the payment happens. *Reconciliation*: proving that orders, PSP payouts and the bank agree, line by line, every month.

**Architecture implications** [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT]. The commerce platform makes promises; the ERP keeps the books. The handoff is a small set of documents with stable identifiers: order → invoice (on dispatch, with the tax basis), return → credit note, payment events → PSP receivable, payout report → bank. **Starting**: the hosted platform's order export into an accounting package; invoices generated by the shop are acceptable if numbering is gap-free and immutable. **Growing**: an ERP or accounting system becomes the invoice's system of record; the connector must be idempotent per order and per return, and must never post from the webhook directly. **Omnichannel**: invoice generation moves out of the shop entirely (stores, EDI customers, marketplaces and web all invoice from one numbering authority per legal entity); Peppol e-invoices for Belgian B2B customers come from the ERP. **Group**: one finance data pipeline consumes PSP reporting APIs and store settlement files; the "order-to-ledger" contract is versioned and tested like any other integration, and RECONCILED's sub-ledger pattern applies.
