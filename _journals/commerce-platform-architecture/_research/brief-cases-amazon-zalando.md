# Research brief: Two public cases, Amazon and Zalando

Prepared 7 November 2026 for chapter writers of *ORDERED: Architecting Commerce Platforms*. The brief feeds chapters 26 (API contracts and versioning), 29 (architecture styles), 42 (replatforming), 47 (Case: Amazon) and 48 (Case: Zalando). It is platform-neutral: Amazon and Zalando are dated, sourced examples, not models to copy. Everything about how either company works *inside* is limited to what the company itself has published; the rest is marked as judgement.

## How to read this brief

Every substantive claim carries one tag:

- **[VERIFIED: url, published YYYY-MM(-DD)]**: the page was fetched in this session (7 November 2026) and supports the claim. Paraphrases stay close to the source and quoted text is quoted. The date is the source's publication date. Undated pages are marked "n.d., accessed 2026-11".
- **[WIDELY REPORTED: url, YYYY]**: from a reputable secondary source (trade press, conference write-up, syndicated press release) that was fetched, or from a primary page that could not be fetched but whose content is consistently reported.
- **[ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT]**: industry knowledge or the analyst's inference. Every statement about how a proprietary system works beyond what the company has described falls here.

**Evidence age matters.** Much of the architectural evidence is old by design: Amazon's service decomposition was described in 2006–2007 and Zalando's microservices move in 2015–2017. These sources describe systems that have certainly changed since; they are flagged **(evidence age)** and should be presented in the book as *history*, not as a description of today's systems. Business figures are from 2025–2026 filings.

**Research limitation.** The session's shared web-search quota ran out near the end; all later work used direct fetches. Several primary pages blocked automated fetching: the ACM Queue text of Werner Vogels' 2006 interview (Cloudflare), the ACM Digital Library copy, Fast Company's 2004 "two-pizza" article, Amazon's 2005 Prime press release (404 at two guessed URLs), Business Wire and Nasdaq copies of Amazon's February 2026 delivery-speed release (403/timeout), and McKinsey's 2018 Eric Bowman interview (timeouts). Amazon's current fulfillment-centre count is not in the 10-K (which reports square footage), so it is not given.

## PART A: Amazon

### A1. From a two-tier monolith to hundreds of services (2001–2007)

The primary account is Vogels' June 2006 interview with Jim Gray in ACM Queue ("A Conversation with Werner Vogels"). Its text could not be fetched this session (see limitation); the claims below come from the 2007 High Scalability digest of that interview and from Amazon's own Dynamo paper.

- In the interview as digested, Amazon's front end was a "two-tier monolith" and "In 2001 it became clear that the front-end application couldn't scale anymore." Amazon moved "to a fully-distributed, decentralized, services platform" that "grew into hundreds of services and a number of application servers that aggregate the information from the services." A page request touched "between 100-150 services" [WIDELY REPORTED: http://highscalability.com/amazon-architecture, 2007 (published 2007-09-18), digesting the 2006 ACM Queue interview] **(evidence age: 19 years)**.
- The same digest carries the two cultural slogans attributed to Vogels: "You build it, you run it" and teams "called two pizza teams" [WIDELY REPORTED: http://highscalability.com/amazon-architecture, 2007] **(evidence age)**.
- Amazon's own 2007 paper states the architecture in its first paragraph: "Amazon uses a highly decentralized, loosely coupled, service oriented architecture consisting of hundreds of services." Services have "stringent latency requirements which are in general measured at the 99.9th percentile of the distribution" [VERIFIED: https://www.allthingsdistributed.com/files/amazon-dynamo-sosp2007.pdf, published 2007-10 (SOSP'07, 14–17 October 2007)] **(evidence age)**.
- The internal name of the monolith ("Obidos") appears in secondary retellings but not in any page fetched this session; do not use it as a sourced fact [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT].
- Vogels' later AWS retrospective says the management-API lesson "we had already learned from our experiences with Amazon retail", and that AWS was built as "a collection of primitives and tools" rather than one framework [VERIFIED: https://www.allthingsdistributed.com/2016/03/10-lessons-from-10-years-of-aws.html, published 2016-03-11].

**For newcomers.** *Monolith*: one deployable application that contains all the shop's logic and usually one shared database. *Service-oriented architecture (SOA)*: the shop is split into many separately deployed services, each owning one function (cart, pricing, orders) and talking to the others over the network. *Tail latency (99.9th percentile)*: the response time that only one request in a thousand exceeds; Amazon measured services on it because a page that calls 100+ services is only as fast as its slowest dependency.

**Architecture implications** [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT]. The documented sequence is monolith → scaling wall (2001) → services (hundreds by 2006–2007), with the split driven by scale and by team count, not by fashion. A page that aggregates 100–150 services is only workable with disciplined latency budgets, which is why the 99.9th-percentile SLA and the "always writeable" cart (A2) appear in the same paper. For the book's stages: Starting and Growing merchants are on the *left* of that timeline (one platform, one database) and nothing in Amazon's public record argues for decomposing before the scaling wall; Omnichannel merchants meet the same aggregation problem at a smaller scale (a product page assembled from PIM, pricing, stock and reviews) and inherit the lesson about tail latency; Group-stage organisations are the only ones for which "hundreds of services" is a realistic shape.

### A2. The cart as a highly available write path: Dynamo (2007) → DynamoDB (2012, 2022)

- The Dynamo paper motivates its design with the cart: "customers should be able to view and add items to their shopping cart even if disks are failing, network routes are flapping, or data centers are being destroyed by tornados." Dynamo targets "an 'always writeable' data store (i.e., a data store that is highly available for writes)"; "the shopping cart service must allow customers to add and remove items from their shopping cart even amidst network and server failures," which "forces us to push the complexity of conflict resolution to the reads" [VERIFIED: https://www.allthingsdistributed.com/files/amazon-dynamo-sosp2007.pdf, published 2007-10] **(evidence age)**.
- The paper's consistency rule for carts: "the shopping cart application requires that an 'Add to Cart' operation can never be forgotten or rejected"; if the latest version is unavailable, a change to an older version "is still meaningful and should be preserved" but "shouldn't supersede the currently unavailable state of the cart"; divergent versions are merged (an add is never lost, a delete can resurface) [VERIFIED: same paper, 2007-10].
- Scale in 2006–2007: the Shopping Cart Service "served tens of millions requests that resulted in well over 3 million checkouts in a single day", and the session-state service "handled hundreds of thousands of concurrently active sessions" [VERIFIED: same paper, 2007-10] **(evidence age)**.
- Vogels' 2007 blog post adds that Dynamo was internal and "not exposed directly as a web service", though related technology powered parts of AWS such as S3 [VERIFIED: https://www.allthingsdistributed.com/2007/10/amazons_dynamo.html, published 2007-10-02].
- The 2012 DynamoDB announcement explains why the internal system did not spread: Dynamo was "originally built to serve the needs of the shopping cart" but "did nothing to reduce the operational complexity of running large database systems"; engineers "strongly preferred simplicity to fine-grained control" and chose managed services such as S3 and SimpleDB. DynamoDB combined Dynamo's incremental scaling and predictable performance with a managed service [VERIFIED: https://www.allthingsdistributed.com/2012/01/amazon-dynamodb.html, published 2012-01-18].
- The 2022 USENIX ATC paper ("Amazon DynamoDB: A Scalable, Predictably Performant, and Fully Managed NoSQL Database Service", Elhemali et al., July 2022) reports that during the 66-hour Prime Day 2021 event Amazon systems made trillions of API calls to DynamoDB, "peaking at 89.2 million requests per second" with single-digit-millisecond latency, and discusses fairness, partition traffic imbalance and automated operations as the hard problems of running it [VERIFIED: https://www.usenix.org/conference/atc22/presentation/elhemali, published 2022-07].

**For newcomers.** *Always writeable*: the store accepts a write even when parts of the system are down, and sorts out conflicts later, because refusing an "add to cart" costs a sale. *Eventual consistency*: different copies of the data may disagree for a short time and converge later. *Managed service*: a database run by a provider so the team using it does not operate servers, replication or failover.

**Architecture implications** [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT]. The cart is the one place in a commerce system where availability beats consistency by design: a lost add-to-cart is a lost order, while a briefly stale cart is harmless. The paper's merge rule (never lose an add; tolerate a resurrected delete) is a business decision encoded in a data structure, which is the book's "which system may make which promise" idea in miniature. The 2012 lesson is as important as the 2007 one: Amazon's own teams would not adopt a correct but operationally heavy store, so the second generation was a managed service. For Starting and Growing merchants, the hosted platform's cart already behaves this way; Omnichannel merchants who build their own cart or headless front end should buy a managed highly available store rather than replicate Dynamo; Group-stage platforms designing a cart service inherit the whole trade-off, including conflict merging across devices and channels.

### A3. Teams, ownership and the "API mandate"

- Amazon's own DevOps guidance quotes Bezos: "We try to create teams that are no larger than can be fed by two pizzas. We call that the two-pizza team rule," and ties it to ownership: the team should see the process "as an end-to-end venture" and not treat production "as black boxes where they have no visibility" [VERIFIED: https://docs.aws.amazon.com/whitepapers/latest/introduction-devops-aws/two-pizza-teams.html, n.d., accessed 2026-11].
- The phrase is commonly dated to a 2004 Fast Company profile of Bezos ("Inside the Mind of Jeff Bezos"); that page returned 403 this session, so the 2004 dating is secondary [WIDELY REPORTED: https://www.fastcompany.com/50106/inside-mind-jeff-bezos, 2004, not fetched].
- The "API mandate" exists in public only as Steve Yegge's recollection in his 2011 "Google Platforms Rant" (a Google+ post preserved as a gist dated 12 October 2011). Yegge dates it "back around 2002 I think, plus or minus a year" and lists: "All teams will henceforth expose their data and functionality through service interfaces"; "Teams must communicate with each other through these interfaces"; no other interprocess communication, "no direct reads of another team's data store"; technology "doesn't matter"; and "All service interfaces, without exception, must be designed from the ground up to be externalizable" [VERIFIED: https://gist.github.com/chitchcock/1281611, published 2011-10-12] **(evidence age; single first-hand recollection, never confirmed by Amazon in any page fetched)**. Present it as widely reported, not as an Amazon document.
- What Amazon *has* confirmed in its own words is the outcome: in 2006 Vogels wrote that Amazon "continues on the path of opening up all of its services for customers and partners to use," and that with Fulfillment by Amazon "we are now also opening up our fulfillment network for everyone to use" [VERIFIED: https://www.allthingsdistributed.com/2006/09/fulfilling.html, published 2006-09-19].

**For newcomers.** *Two-pizza team*: a team small enough that two pizzas feed it (roughly five to ten people), owning one service end to end. *Externalizable interface*: an API designed so that it could be offered to outside developers, which forces clean contracts even when only internal teams use it. *You build it, you run it*: the team that writes a service also operates it in production.

**Architecture implications** [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT]. The mandate (as recalled) is the strongest public statement of the rule that *the contract is the boundary*: no shared databases, no back doors, one way in. The book's chapter 26 can use it as the origin story of API-first governance, with Zalando's guidelines (B3) as the written, verifiable version. For Starting and Growing merchants the rule applies to vendors, not teams: integrations should go through the platform's documented API, never through a direct database read of the ERP. At Omnichannel scale the rule becomes an internal one (the OMS exposes availability; nobody reads its tables). At Group scale, team-per-service ownership and the "externalizable" test are what make shared services possible.

### A4. The business: Prime (2005), Fulfillment by Amazon (2006), the marketplace share

- Amazon Prime launched in February 2005 at $79 a year for unlimited two-day shipping; the 2005 press release could not be fetched this session [WIDELY REPORTED: multiple timelines, 2005]. Amazon's 2024 shareholder letter confirms the starting scope: "Prime two-day delivery began with one million products and now covers over 300 million items, with tens of millions available in one day or better" [VERIFIED: https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/company-news/amazon-ceo-andy-jassy-2024-letter-to-shareholders, page undated; letter published 2025-04 per press coverage].
- Fulfillment by Amazon and WebStore by Amazon launched as beta services on 19 September 2006 (Seattle, Business Wire), giving small and medium-sized businesses "access to Amazon's order fulfillment, customer service, customer shipping offers, and underlying website technology"; third-party items marked "Fulfilled by Amazon" could qualify for Prime and Free Super Saver Shipping [VERIFIED: https://press.aboutamazon.com/2006/9/amazon-launches-new-services-to-help-small-and-medium-sized-businesses-enhance-their-customer-offerings-by-accessing-amazons-order-fulfillment-customer-service-and-website-functionality, published 2006-09-19].
- Bezos' 2018 shareholder letter gives the third-party share of physical gross merchandise sales by year: 3% (1999), 17% (2002), 28% (2005), 34% (2010), 42% (2012), 51% (2015), 58% (2018), with the line "Third-party sellers are kicking our first party butt. Badly." It credits "Fulfillment by Amazon and the Prime membership program" as the two investments that "meaningfully improved the customer experience of buying from independent sellers" [VERIFIED: https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/company-news/2018-letter-to-shareholders, page undated; letter dated 2019-04].
- Amazon's 2022 Small Business Empowerment Report states: "More than 60% of sales in Amazon's store are from independent sellers" [VERIFIED: https://aboutamazon.com/news/small-business/amazon-2022-small-business-empowerment-report, n.d. (PDF dated 2023-05-31), accessed 2026-11]. The 2024 report repeats "more than 60% of the sales in Amazon's store", and adds that independent sellers "have shipped more than 80 billion items through Fulfillment by Amazon since its 2006 launch", that Multi-Channel Fulfillment "helped more than 300,000 sellers worldwide fulfill orders from their other sales channels" in the past year, and that over 55,000 sellers exceeded $1 million in sales in 2024 [VERIFIED: https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/small-business/amazon-2024-small-business-empowerment-report, n.d. (published 2025-05 per coverage), accessed 2026-11] ⚠ (the unit share is a moving figure; third-party trackers put paid units at 60–62% through 2024–2026 [WIDELY REPORTED: https://www.marketplacepulse.com/articles/amazon-steers-third-party-seller-share-to-all-time-high, 2025–2026]).
- The FY2025 10-K defines the model: "We offer programs that enable sellers to sell their products in our stores, and fulfill orders using our services. We are not the seller of record in these transactions." Amazon earns "fixed fees, a percentage of sales, per-unit activity fees, interest, or some combination thereof" from sellers, and designs its stores "to enable hundreds of millions of unique products to be sold by us and by third parties" [VERIFIED: https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1018724/000101872426000004/amzn-20251231.htm, filed 2026-02-06].
- Scale, FY2025: total net sales $716.9 billion (North America $426.3bn, International $161.9bn, AWS $128.7bn); fulfillment expense $109.1 billion; approximately 1,576,000 full-time and part-time employees at 31 December 2025; 615 North America and 8 International physical stores [VERIFIED: same 10-K, filed 2026-02-06].

**For newcomers.** *Marketplace (3P) vs retail (1P)*: in 1P Amazon buys stock and is the seller of record; in 3P an independent seller is the seller of record and Amazon takes fees. *Fulfillment by Amazon (FBA)*: the seller sends stock to Amazon's warehouses and Amazon picks, packs, ships and handles returns. *Seller of record*: the legal party that sells to the consumer and carries the obligations (invoice, VAT, consumer rights).

**Architecture implications** [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT]. The table in the 2018 letter is the clearest dated evidence that a retailer became a platform *through* two operational services, Prime (the promise) and FBA (the fulfillment network opened to others), not through a storefront feature. Architecturally, FBA means one inventory and fulfillment system that serves two seller-of-record models with different legal and financial flows; that is the same split a Group-stage merchant faces when it runs retail and marketplace models side by side (Zalando's 32% partner share, B5). For Omnichannel merchants the practical lesson is that "fulfillment as a platform" starts with being able to fulfil *someone else's* order from the same stock pool with the same promise.

### A5. The promise and the network: 2024–2026

- 2024 letter: faster delivery is "primarily due to our regionalization redesign of our fulfillment network," new placement algorithms and same-day fulfillment centres; Amazon delivers "over a billion packages each year to customers in 13,000 zip codes spanning 1.2 million square miles" and Same-Day/Overnight expanded "to dozens of smaller cities and towns" [VERIFIED: https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/company-news/amazon-ceo-andy-jassy-2024-letter-to-shareholders, page undated; published 2025-04].
- 2025 letter (released 9 April 2026 per press coverage): over one million robots in fulfillment centres; "more than 85 Same Day Fulfillment Centers" carrying the top 90,000 SKUs across the US; over $4 billion committed to the rural delivery network; same-day units "more than 500 million in 2026 so far"; average monthly same-day customers in rural areas "nearly doubled in 2025"; grocery "over $150 billion in gross sales in 2025"; "Amazon Now" delivering thousands of items within 20 minutes in India; drones meant to use same-day centres "as a building block" [VERIFIED: https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/company-news/amazon-ceo-andy-jassy-2025-letter-to-shareholders, page undated; released 2026-04-09 per coverage] ⚠.
- Amazon's 3 February 2026 press release (syndicated on Business Wire and Nasdaq, neither fetchable this session) reports over 13 billion items delivered same or next day globally in 2025, over 8 billion in the US (up over 30%), a 70% year-on-year rise in items delivered in under a day, and expansion of same/next-day reach to over 4,000 smaller cities, towns and rural areas across 44 states [WIDELY REPORTED: https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20260203913728/en/, 2026, and https://distributionstrategy.com/2026/02/amazon-sets-prime-delivery-speed-record-in-2025-with-13-billion-same-or-next-day-items/, 2026] ⚠.
- Properties in the 10-K: North America "fulfillment, data centers, and other" space of 474.1 million square feet leased and 45.8 million owned; International 185.8 million leased and 20.7 million owned (thousands of square feet in the filing) [VERIFIED: 10-K FY2025, filed 2026-02-06]. The 10-K does not state a count of fulfillment centres.
- The 10-K's own framing of Prime: shipping offers and Prime benefits are "effective worldwide marketing tools" that Amazon intends "to continue offering them indefinitely" [VERIFIED: 10-K FY2025, filed 2026-02-06].

**For newcomers.** *Regionalization*: holding stock in the region where it will be ordered so most orders ship from nearby, which cuts cost and time. *Placement algorithm*: the logic that decides which warehouse should hold which SKU and in what quantity. *Delivery promise*: the date (or hour) shown at checkout, which becomes a commitment the fulfillment network must keep.

**Architecture implications** [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT]. Amazon's public account makes the promise, not the catalog, the product: the investment goes into network shape (regions, same-day centres with a curated SKU set), placement and the promise calculation. For the book, this is the argument that the promise engine (which date can I show?) is a first-class system with inputs from inventory, network and carrier capacity. Starting and Growing merchants rent the promise from a carrier or 3PL; Omnichannel merchants must compute it themselves across warehouse, stores and 3PL; Group-stage merchants shape the network to the promise, as Amazon does. Note the limit: only Amazon's scale justifies 85 single-purpose same-day centres; the transferable idea is the curated SKU subset per fast node.

### A6. Buy with Prime, Shop Direct, Rufus / Alexa for Shopping, Buy for Me, and third-party agents

- **Buy with Prime** lets merchants offer Prime delivery and Amazon checkout on their own sites, with Amazon handling "the payment, processing, storage, picking, packing, and shipping" plus customer service and returns; integrations exist for Shopify (bundled with Multi-Channel Fulfillment), Salesforce Commerce Cloud, a widget for other platforms and an API; Amazon cites a 16% average lift in revenue per visitor from A/B tests across 167 merchants (July 2023–June 2024) [VERIFIED: https://buywithprime.amazon.com/, n.d., accessed 2026-11] ⚠. Its 2022 launch date is not stated on the page [WIDELY REPORTED: 2022].
- **Rufus** was launched as a beta "generative AI-powered expert shopping assistant" in the US mobile app, trained on "Amazon's extensive product catalog, customer reviews, community Q&As, and information from across the web"; the page notes that on 13 May 2026 Rufus was renamed "Alexa for Shopping" [VERIFIED: https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/retail/amazon-rufus, page undated (launch 2024-02 per coverage), accessed 2026-11] ⚠. Amazon reports "over 250 million shoppers have used Rufus this year", monthly users up 140–149% and interactions up 210% year on year, and that users "are 60%+ more likely to convert"; Rufus "helped over 300 million customers in 2025" [VERIFIED: https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/retail/amazon-agentic-ai-gen-ai-shopping and https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/retail/alexa-for-shopping-ai-assistant, n.d. (2026), accessed 2026-11] ⚠.
- **Alexa for Shopping** (2026) combines Rufus and Alexa+ and adds agentic actions: auto-buy when an item reaches a target price (requests "stay active for six months or until you cancel", with a free 24-hour cancellation window), price history, reorders and scheduled recurring purchases, and Buy for Me checkout [VERIFIED: https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/retail/alexa-for-shopping-ai-assistant and https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/retail/amazon-rufus-ai-assistant-personalized-shopping-features, n.d. (2026), accessed 2026-11] ⚠.
- **Buy for Me** (beta announced April 2025): for items Amazon does not sell, the app shows "Shop brand sites directly"; the customer confirms address, taxes, shipping and payment on Amazon's checkout page, then "Amazon completes the purchase on the brand's site"; it runs on Amazon Bedrock with "Amazon Nova and Anthropic's Claude" models; Amazon passes "encrypted name, address, and payment details" to the brand site and "cannot see customers' previous or separate orders from other brands' sites"; the brand "handles delivery, returns, exchanges, and customer service"; brands choose whether to participate; "The customer is in control of the AI agent acting on their behalf" [VERIFIED: https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/retail/amazon-shopping-app-buy-for-me-brands, n.d., accessed 2026-11; date and "no commission currently" from https://www.digitalcommerce360.com/2025/04/08/amazon-buy-for-me-agentic-ai-shopping-tool/, published 2025-04-08] ⚠. The catalog grew "from 65K products at launch to over half a million" [VERIFIED: https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/retail/amazon-agentic-ai-gen-ai-shopping, n.d. (2026)] and later to "tens of millions" [VERIFIED: https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/retail/amazon-shop-direct-external-stores, n.d. (2026)] ⚠.
- **Shop Direct** (launched 2025, merchant feeds added 2026): products from other stores appear in Amazon search; "Shop Direct now includes over 100 million products from more than 400,000 merchants"; merchants sync catalog, price and inventory through existing feeds from Feedonomics, Salsify or CEDCommerce, with an Amazon merchant-direct feed "coming soon" [VERIFIED: https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/retail/amazon-shop-direct-external-stores, n.d. (2026), accessed 2026-11] ⚠.
- **Stance on third-party agents.** Amazon's statement on Perplexity's Comet: "third-party applications that offer to make purchases on behalf of customers from other businesses should operate openly" and "respect service provider decisions whether or not to participate"; agentic apps "have the same obligations" as food-delivery apps or online travel agencies; Amazon "repeatedly" asked Perplexity to remove Amazon from Comet, citing a "significantly degraded shopping and customer service experience"; the cease-and-desist letter is dated 31 October 2025 [VERIFIED: https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/company-news/amazon-perplexity-comet-statement, n.d. (letter 2025-10-31), accessed 2026-11]. Amazon sued in Northern California in early November 2025 alleging computer-fraud violations and "disguising Comet as a Google Chrome browser"; Perplexity called it a "threat to user choice" [VERIFIED: https://www.retaildive.com/news/amazon-sues-perplexity-ai-shopping-agents/804871/, published 2025-11-06] ⚠ (case status not checked).

**For newcomers.** *Agentic commerce*: software that does not only recommend but acts, placing an order on the customer's behalf. *Merchant of record for an agent purchase*: in Buy for Me the brand's site remains the seller; Amazon is the agent passing the customer's details. *Product feed*: a structured file (catalog, price, stock) a merchant publishes so other channels can list its products.

**Architecture implications** [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT]. Two patterns are visible. First, Amazon exports its promise-keeping machinery to other storefronts (Buy with Prime, Multi-Channel Fulfillment) and imports other storefronts' catalogs into its own surface (Shop Direct feeds, Buy for Me), so the merchant's checkout and fulfillment become interchangeable modules behind a dominant demand surface. Second, Amazon's rule for agents, *identify yourself and respect the provider's choice to participate*, is the same rule every merchant will need for bots on its own site: a documented, authenticated agent channel (feeds, APIs, a checkout protocol) plus a way to refuse unidentified automation. For Starting merchants the realistic move is a clean product feed and standard checkout; for Growing and Omnichannel merchants, feed quality and an explicit agent policy; for Group-stage platforms, an agent identity and consent model (compare Zalando's 2026 broker in B6).

### A7. A nuance: Prime Video's 2023 "microservices to monolith" post

- In March 2023 the Prime Video Video Quality Analysis team described replacing a serverless, distributed design (Step Functions orchestrating Lambda functions, intermediate results in S3, SNS for notifications) for its audio/video monitoring service with a single application process on ECS/EC2 because "running the infrastructure at a high scale was very expensive" (S3 read/write volume, state-transition costs and account limits) and the design "supported only around 5% of the expected load"; data moved in memory instead of S3; operational costs fell by 90%. The team's own framing: the "distributed approach wasn't bringing a lot of benefits in our specific use case" [VERIFIED: https://www.infoq.com/news/2023/05/prime-ec2-ecs-saves-costs/, published 2023-05-03, summarising the March 2023 post]. The original URL (primevideotech.com) now redirects to a generic Amazon page.

**For newcomers.** *Serverless*: functions run on demand with no servers to manage, billed per invocation and per state transition. *Orchestration service*: a managed workflow engine (here AWS Step Functions) that calls each step in turn and stores state between steps.

**Architecture implications** [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT]. The post is often quoted as "Amazon abandons microservices"; the source supports a narrower claim: *one* internal tool with a high-volume, tightly coupled pipeline was cheaper as one process. It is a cost-and-coupling argument for sizing a boundary to the data flow across it, which the book can place next to Zalando's 2025 event-to-API reversal (B4). It is not evidence about Amazon.com's retail architecture.

## PART B: Zalando

### B1. "Jimmy", Radical Agility and the microservices move (2015–2016)

- In March 2015 Zalando's technology leadership introduced "Radical Agility", built on autonomy, mastery and purpose (after Daniel Pink's *Drive*) and on trust, with small autonomous teams owning a topic end to end; the 2016 trade reporting gives teams of "three to seven people" [WIDELY REPORTED: https://diginomica.com/zalando-embraces-radical-agility-in-its-bold-platform-play, 2016, and https://www.infoq.com/articles/zalando-apis-delivery-radical-agility, 2016] **(evidence age)**.
- Zalando's own engineering blog, July 2015: VP Engineering Eric Bowman at AWS Summit Berlin 2015: "We think of teams as a unit of trust"; "we've tried to create the context where people can make mistakes and it's not fatal"; STUPS presented as Zalando's open-source platform-as-a-service on AWS for many autonomous teams under audit requirements [VERIFIED: https://engineering.zalando.com/posts/2015/07/radical-agility-on-aws-video.html, published 2015-07-07] **(evidence age)**.
- The monolith was called "Jimmy": "all of the Shop teams shared the same code base" and teams "couldn't move quickly because of Jimmy's slow startup time and our overly complicated deployment processes." The Spearheads team (seven engineers and a designer, formed May 2015) began rebuilding the Fashion Store, 15 country sites serving over 16 million active customers, as microservices owned by autonomous teams that own "programming, quality assurance, deployment, and operations"; it chose a Go-based router, Skipper, which it open-sourced [VERIFIED: https://engineering.zalando.com/posts/2015/10/from-jimmy-to-microservices-rebuilding-zalandos-fashion-store.html, published 2015-10-16] **(evidence age)**.
- Rodrigue Schaefer's talk "From Monolith to Microservices at Zalando" (MicroXchange, Berlin, 4–5 February 2016) covered microservices for front end and back end across "our 85+ engineering teams", with STUPS as "our open source platform as a service for using AWS and Docker" [VERIFIED: https://engineering.zalando.com/posts/2016/02/from-monolith-to-microservices-video.html, published 2016-02-10].
- STUPS: development began late 2014; publicly released spring 2015, available for production to every team in May 2015; "a convenient and audit-compliant Platform-as-a-Service" on AWS; "Our employees get nearly administrative access to their AWS accounts with almost no restrictions," with near-real-time audit notifications so that "Auditors need to know that we're complying with the regulations" (PCI, SOX, German law) [VERIFIED: https://www.infoq.com/news/2016/04/zalando-stups-aws, published 2016-04-04]. The STUPS site lists its components (Senza for CloudFormation deployment, Taupage base AMI, Pier One Docker registry, mint secrets, fullstop. audit reporting, Kio application registry and others) [VERIFIED: https://stups.io/, n.d., accessed 2026-11].
- Organisation in December 2016: "about 110 teams"; Tech "has more than 1400 employees, largely software engineers and data scientists" across six hubs in Germany, Ireland and Finland; "each team has its own AWS account"; applications "managed as Software-as-a-Service based on microservice architecture" [VERIFIED: https://www.infoq.com/articles/zalando-apis-delivery-radical-agility, published 2016-12-24] **(evidence age)**.
- One year into the shop rebuild (October 2016) the effort was renamed Project Mosaic and the task force became Team Pathfinder: "Team Pathfinder enables team autonomy by providing a platform to deliver web content"; components Skipper, Tailor, Shaker, Innkeeper, Instaskip; header and footer fragments live, PDP and wish-list fragments going live; the author describes a shift "from prototyping to performance optimization" and toward "stable components that other teams can depend on" [VERIFIED: https://engineering.zalando.com/posts/2016/10/jimmy-to-microservices-the-journey-one-year-later.html, published 2016-10-05].
- A Dortmund team's lessons (April 2016): a first cut of two services became eight after modelling boundaries ("Define your Microservices properly and know your boundaries"), "Integrate early, integrate often", and weekly goals after an upfront plan failed [VERIFIED: https://engineering.zalando.com/posts/2016/04/four-lessons-with-microservices.html, published 2016-04-27].
- The business-intelligence view (September 2015): the monolith's "consolidation of data in a single place makes business intelligence fairly simple," whereas "with each service owning its data, data is spread over a significantly larger number of cloud-based microservices" (article and order data across eight PostgreSQL databases); Zalando deployed Kafka and ZooKeeper on AWS "to make our event data available for downstream systems" and planned an S3 data lake [VERIFIED: https://engineering.zalando.com/posts/2015/09/data-integration-in-a-world-of-microservices.html, published 2015-09-21].

**For newcomers.** *Autonomous team*: a team that chooses its own tools and process and owns its services in production. *Platform-as-a-service (internal)*: tooling that gives every team the same safe way to deploy to the cloud. *Front-end fragments (micro front ends)*: a page assembled from pieces owned by different teams, each deployed separately.

**Architecture implications** [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT]. Zalando's documented sequence mirrors Amazon's with a thirteen-year lag and a stated organisational motive: the monolith blocked team autonomy (startup time, shared code base, deployment), and the fix was organisational first (teams, AWS accounts, STUPS) and architectural second. The 2015 BI post is the earliest honest statement of the cost: once every service owns its data, analytics and reconciliation need an event stream, which is why Nakadi (B3) exists. For the book's stages: Starting and Growing merchants are below the team count at which this makes sense; Omnichannel merchants meet the *data* problem (a dozen systems, no single place to query) before they meet the team problem, and should take the event-stream lesson without the service count; Group stage is where team-sized modularity is the real question.

### B3. API governance: the API Guild, the guidelines, the event bus, the Tech Radar

- The API Guild formed in March 2015 as an informal group (about 20 members) that sets standards and reviews APIs as "a sounding board and review resource, not an approval board, to avoid bottlenecks"; members spend about 10% of their week on it. "API First" is a key engineering principle because Zalando's "open platform strategy relies on public APIs used by external partners": "We cannot afford to break our APIs"; "Ideally, all Zalando APIs should look like the same author created them" [VERIFIED: https://engineering.zalando.com/posts/2015/09/on-apis-and-the-zalando-api-guild.html, published 2015-09-03].
- By December 2016 the guidelines were open-sourced and receiving external contributions; API First means designing "the API outside of code first" in OpenAPI (Swagger) with a lightweight review; "We see API definitions as a source truth, part of a contract between a service provider and consumers" [VERIFIED: https://www.infoq.com/articles/zalando-apis-delivery-radical-agility, published 2016-12-24].
- The current guidelines (RFC 2119 MUST/SHOULD/MAY) say teams "must define APIs first, before coding its implementation, using OpenAPI as specification language"; "Treat your API as product and act like a product owner"; and cite Postel's law [VERIFIED: https://opensource.zalando.com/restful-api-guidelines/, n.d., accessed 2026-11].
- Compatibility rules: #106 "MUST not break backward compatibility" ("Change APIs, but keep all consumers running"); #107 "SHOULD prefer compatible extensions"; #108 "MUST prepare clients to accept compatible API extensions" ("Be tolerant with unknown fields in the payload"); #113 "SHOULD avoid versioning" ("do so in a compatible way and avoid generating additional API versions"); #114 "MUST use media type versioning"; #115 "MUST not use URL versioning" [VERIFIED: https://raw.githubusercontent.com/zalando/restful-api-guidelines/main/chapters/compatibility.adoc, n.d., accessed 2026-11].
- Deprecation rules: #185 "MUST obtain approval of clients before API shut down" ("all clients have given their consent on a sunset date"); #186 "MUST collect external partner consent on deprecation time span"; #187 "MUST reflect deprecation in API specifications" (`deprecated: true` plus sunset date and migration guidance); #188 "MUST monitor usage of deprecated API scheduled for sunset"; #189 "SHOULD add Deprecation and Sunset header to responses"; #191 "MUST not start using deprecated APIs" [VERIFIED: https://raw.githubusercontent.com/zalando/restful-api-guidelines/main/chapters/deprecation.adoc, n.d., accessed 2026-11].
- The Tech Radar was created in 2016 "to inspire and support teams to pick the best technologies for new projects", maintained by a Technologist Guild of about 25 senior technologists with ring assignments by vote; "Teams must use the Tech Radar as one input source for their technology decisions" [VERIFIED: https://engineering.zalando.com/posts/2016/05/zalando-tech-radar.html, published 2016-05-17]. The public radar defines ADOPT ("high confidence in to serve our purpose, also in large scale"), TRIAL, ASSESS and HOLD ("not recommended to be used for new projects") [VERIFIED: https://opensource.zalando.com/tech-radar/, n.d., accessed 2026-11].
- Nakadi's design, in its README: producers register an event type with a schema before publishing; events are validated against the schema; partitioning by random, hash or user-defined keys; the REST abstraction exists so teams do not depend directly on a broker [VERIFIED: https://github.com/zalando/nakadi, n.d., accessed 2026-11]. Zalando's 2017 "third-generation microservices" post describes the intent: a move "toward an asynchronous, event-driven style" where services "process incoming events and data from other services" and data streaming becomes "a central part of the service fabric" [VERIFIED: https://engineering.zalando.com/posts/2017/05/platform-engineering-and-third-generation-microservices-in-dublin.html, published 2017-05-26].

**For newcomers.** *API First*: the interface is designed and reviewed (as an OpenAPI document) before any code is written. *Tolerant reader*: a client that ignores fields it does not know, so the provider can add fields without breaking it. *Sunset*: the announced date after which an old API version stops working; Zalando requires client consent before it. *Event bus*: a shared channel on which services publish what happened (order placed, stock changed) and others subscribe.

**Architecture implications** [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT]. The guidelines are the most complete public, maintained, dated specimen of API governance from a large retailer and should carry chapter 26: avoid versions by extending compatibly; when you must version, do it in the media type, not the URL; deprecate only with consent, a sunset date, headers and usage monitoring. The organisational detail matters as much as the rules: a guild that reviews but does not approve, and a radar that is "one input" rather than a mandate (until 2020, see B4). For Starting and Growing merchants the same rules apply in reverse, as what to demand from vendors (dated deprecations, additive change, Sunset headers). Omnichannel merchants writing their first internal APIs can adopt the compatibility chapter wholesale. Group-stage platforms need the deprecation chapter, because external partners depend on them.

### B4. What changed later: the return to discipline (2018–2026)

No source was found in which Zalando announces the end of "Radical Agility" or a 2019 tech reorganisation under that name; the shift is visible in Zalando's own posts and in business decisions.

- 2018, Bowman (then VP Digital Foundation, leading a 500-person group): "Our biggest challenge was that we treated autonomy like an input to teams"; unaligned teams were like "air molecules in a balloon: moving fast but not running together as a group"; the monolithic tech organisation "had too many customers for us to prioritize and align well"; autonomy became "an aspiration and a reward, not a gift"; "a set of principles and systems scales better than a set of prescriptions"; engineering teams were embedded across the company as self-contained units with OKRs (adopted 2015) and company-wide "Global Projects" such as GDPR [VERIFIED: https://www.infoq.com/articles/scaled-autonomy-zalando/, published 2018-09-30]. Bowman left for TomTom in 2019 [WIDELY REPORTED: LinkedIn/alphalist profiles, 2019–2023].
- 2018, agile coaching: "As Agile Coaches we value principles more than frameworks"; six principles replace a single prescribed method [VERIFIED: https://engineering.zalando.com/posts/2018/08/agile-principles-over-frameworks.html, published 2018-08-02].
- 2020, technology selection: with "1,200+ developers" and "4,000+ applications", Zalando "redefined the ring semantics" of the radar, formalised Technology Selection Principles, gave a Principal Engineering community the power to decide, required Engineering Head sponsorship for TRIAL technologies, and dropped Clojure, Haskell and Rust ("not create new applications in those languages moving forward") in favour of Kotlin and TypeScript, because "A broad freedom of choice leads to increased difficulties in supporting software long-term" [VERIFIED: https://engineering.zalando.com/posts/2020/07/technology-choices-at-zalando-tech-radar-update.html, published 2020-07-15].
- 2023, developer platform: "Sunrise", built on Backstage since 2021, replaced "more than 100 disconnected interfaces" with one portal holding "over 40k registered entities (applications, teams, and users)" and 27 integrated tools, with old tools shut down [VERIFIED: https://engineering.zalando.com/posts/2023/08/sunrise-zalandos-developer-platform-based-on-backstage.html, published 2023-08-03].
- 2025, boundary reversal: the event-driven Price and Stock update pipeline "became a bottleneck, introducing delays and scaling challenges"; "over 90% of each payload unchanged" was reprocessed; during Cyber Week events "could be delayed by up to 30 minutes"; teams that could not consume the streams used other sources, giving "divergent versions of product data"; it was replaced by a synchronous low-latency Product Read API (PRAPI) [VERIFIED: https://engineering.zalando.com/posts/2025/03/event-driven-to-api.html, published 2025-03-07].
- 2023, business: on 21 February 2023 the co-CEOs announced the removal of "several hundred overhead roles across many of our teams" (about 5% of roughly 17,000 employees), sparing logistics, customer care and outlets [VERIFIED: https://www.retailgazette.co.uk/blog/2023/02/zalando-to-axe-hundreds-of-jobs-amid-economic-slowdown/, published 2023-02-21]. The stated reason that parts of the company had "expanded too much" is reported by several outlets but was not in the page fetched [WIDELY REPORTED: 2023].
- 2026, platform consolidation: Zalando is discontinuing Connected Retail and folding its partners into the standard partner programme; the Connected Retail technology "will be discontinued by June 30, 2027"; from September 2026 all partners pay the same standard marketplace fees (with temporary reductions for retailers); "We are not saying goodbye to brick and mortar stores; we are simply updating the technology they use to connect with us" [VERIFIED: https://fashionunited.com/news/business/zalando-consolidates-partnership-models-connected-retail-to-be-discontinued/2026042371971, published 2026-04-23] ⚠.
- Headcount today: "more than 17,000 direct employees", "Around 3,000 employees work in our tech departments", "more than 7,000 employees work in our fulfilment centres" [VERIFIED: https://corporate.zalando.com/sites/default/files/media-download/zalando-se_annual-report-2025_eng_261003_s.pdf, published 2026-03].

**For newcomers.** *Paved road / internal developer platform*: one supported way to build, deploy and find services, so teams do not each solve the same problem. *Technology radar with teeth*: a list of allowed technologies that teams must follow, not only consult. *Synchronous read API*: callers ask for the current price or stock and get an answer now, instead of rebuilding their own copy from a stream of change events.

**Architecture implications** [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT]. The evidence supports a specific story, not "microservices failed": autonomy without alignment produced too many technologies (fixed 2020), too many tools (fixed 2023) and, in one critical path, a stream-based integration that could not keep up at peak and bred divergent copies of product data (fixed 2025). Each fix narrows a choice. The 2025 case is the most useful for the book: events are right for "tell everyone what happened", wrong as the only way to learn "what is the price now" when 90% of the payload is noise and consumers fall 30 minutes behind on the busiest day. That is the book's reconciliation argument in Zalando's words. For Omnichannel merchants: keep a system of record with a read API for price and stock, and use events for propagation, not as a substitute for it. For Group stage: expect to spend a platform team on consolidation a few years after decentralising.

### B5. The platform strategy: Partner Program, Connected Retail, ZFS and ZEOS, ABOUT YOU, 2025 figures

- The Partner Program launched in 2011 (brands sell on Zalando as seller of record via the zDirect portal); by June 2021 "more than 600 partners, representing over 2,000 brands" used zDirect, about a quarter of GMV came from partners, with a target of 40% by 2023 and a long-term goal of half; Zalando Fulfillment Solutions (ZFS) and Zalando Shipping Solutions gave partners access to 16 European markets, with "more than 2,300 market expansions" in 18 months [VERIFIED: https://corporate.zalando.com/en/node/9401, published 2021-06-24] **(evidence age for the targets)**.
- Connected Retail (launched 2018) connects physical stores' stock to Zalando; carriers (DHL in Germany, PostNL in the Netherlands) collect from the store and returns go back to it; Zalando supplies content, payment, customer care and an account manager; "more than 1,500 brick-and-mortar stores in Germany and the Netherlands" in March 2020, when commissions were waived for April–May 2020 and payouts moved to weekly [VERIFIED: https://corporate.zalando.com/en/company/zalandos-connected-retail-program-offers-support-physical-stores, published 2020-03-31]; "more than 2,600 connected store partners" in January 2021, the launch year 2018 stated [VERIFIED: https://corporate.zalando.com/en/convenience/stronger-together-connecting-online-and-offline-retail, published 2021-01-25]; "over 3,000 active stores" in nine markets by February 2021, with C&A as first vertical retailer and Hermes as a second carrier [VERIFIED: https://corporate.zalando.com/en/newsroom/news-stories/zalando-onboards-ca-connected-retail-and-expands-program-austria, published 2021-02-15]. Discontinuation by 30 June 2027: see B4 ⚠.
- ZEOS ("Zalando Ecommerce Operating System"): a multi-channel fulfilment pilot ran in 2022 and moved under the ZEOS brand as ZEOS Fulfillment in October 2023, letting brands fulfil Zalando, own-shop and other-platform orders from a shared inventory pool with one carrier connection and one dashboard, open to players "whether or not they sell through Zalando" [VERIFIED: https://channelx.world/2023/10/zeos-zalando-ecommerce-operating-system/, published 2023-10-26].
- ZEOS today: "By the end of 2025, ZEOS Fulfilment served 25 markets, with over 600 partners selling across 18 sales channels (including Zalando), covering around 85% of the European marketplace volume"; the B2B segment comprises "ZEOS Fulfilment including Zalando Fulfilment Solutions [ZFS] and multi-channel fulfilment [MCF], the enterprise digital commerce platform SCAYLE, the platform integration solution Tradebyte"; a ZEOS Shopify app opened MCF to Shopify's European merchants; "330 online shops were powered by SCAYLE" by end-2025; partnerships named: NEXT, Marks & Spencer, TikTok Shop (via Tradebyte), DEICHMANN and Netto Marken-Discount (SCAYLE) [VERIFIED: Zalando annual report 2025, published 2026-03]. Zalando's December 2025 strategy note lists ZEOS One (merchant portal), a Replenishment Engine, integrations with ChannelEngine, Tradebyte and Rithum, and "over 60" live merchants on fulfilment at that point (the annual report's 600 counts partners across all B2B services; the two figures measure different things) [VERIFIED: https://corporate.zalando.com/en/node/10922, published 2025-12-10] ⚠. The ZEOS site positions logistics, fulfilment, returns, content, replenishment and capital as modular services [VERIFIED: https://www.zeos.eu/, n.d., accessed 2026-11].
- ABOUT YOU: intention announced 11 December 2024; transaction completed 11 July 2025 with Zalando holding 91.45% of the share capital; dual-brand B2C with shared logistics and payment infrastructure; in B2B "ZEOS, Tradebyte, and SCAYLE will combine logistics, software, and service offerings"; targeted Group EBIT synergies "around €100 million per year" [VERIFIED: https://corporate.zalando.com/en/investor-relations/zalando-and-about-you-successfully-complete-transaction, published 2025-07-11]. The squeeze-out took effect on registration in Hamburg on 6 November 2025 at EUR 6.50 per share, with delisting from the Frankfurt Stock Exchange [VERIFIED: https://corporate.aboutyou.de/en/newsroom/press-releases/about-you-and-zalando-complete-final-step-of-their-strategic-combination, published 2025-11-06].
- FY2025 (fact sheet dated 12 March 2026): GMV EUR 17,560.2m (+14.7%), revenue EUR 12,346.1m (+16.8%), adjusted EBIT EUR 590.7m (4.8% margin), active customers 62.0m (+19.7%), orders 278.6m (+11.0%), 4.8 orders per active customer, average basket EUR 62.8, B2C revenue EUR 11,278.7m, B2B revenue EUR 1,091.3m (+14.6%, adjusted EBIT EUR 53.9m, more than doubled), Retail Media +42.4%; 2026 guidance GMV EUR 19.7–20.6bn, pro-forma growth 5–10% [VERIFIED: https://corporate.zalando.com/sites/default/files/media-download/zalando-se_fy-2025_fact-sheet.pdf, published 2026-03-12] ⚠. Reported growth includes ABOUT YOU; pro-forma growth is far lower. Partner business share of group GMV "decreased to 32.1% (2024: 34.2%)" because ABOUT YOU "currently operates with a lower partner business share"; average employees 16,582 [VERIFIED: annual report 2025, published 2026-03].

**For newcomers.** *Partner programme / marketplace model*: the brand is the seller of record and Zalando takes a commission, as opposed to Zalando's wholesale ("retail") model where it buys the stock. *Ship-from-store*: a store's shelf stock is sold online and shipped from the store. *Logistics as a service*: a retailer's warehouses, carriers and software offered to other sellers, for orders on and off its own platform.

**Architecture implications** [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT]. Zalando's platform story is Amazon's in European form and on a European timetable: marketplace (2011) → ship-from-store (2018) → fulfilment for partners (ZFS) → fulfilment for anyone, any channel (ZEOS, 2023) → owning the shop software too (SCAYLE, 2025). The architectural consequence is one stock pool serving several seller-of-record models and several demand channels, which is exactly the Omnichannel-stage problem in the book (own site, stores, marketplaces, wholesale) at larger scale. Two cautions from the evidence: Connected Retail shows that a separately built channel technology gets consolidated once the general partner programme can do the job; and the 32% partner share (down from 34%) shows that a merger imports the acquired company's model mix, not only its revenue.

### B6. AI at Zalando

- 19 April 2023: Zalando announced a beta "fashion assistant powered by ChatGPT" for selected customers in Germany, Austria, Ireland and the UK (English and German), letting customers describe what they want in their own words and refine results in conversation [VERIFIED: https://corporate.zalando.com/en/node/10422, published 2023-04-19].
- 2025: "the Zalando Assistant ... was used by over 6 million customers"; an "AI-powered discovery feed" replaced the home feed; Zalando sees "the emerging channel of agentic commerce as a great opportunity" and notes "the rise of AI agents as digital gatekeepers" in its market outlook [VERIFIED: annual report 2025, published 2026-03] ⚠.
- September 2026, engineering: Zalando runs an internal agentic platform on Kubernetes (kagent, agentgateway, kro) and open-sourced an MIT-licensed Agentic Identity Broker that "lets agents act on behalf of users without ever holding provider tokens"; "Delegated access is the intersection of both the user and agent permissions"; consent "should name a business capability ... instead of presenting a raw scope string"; stronger approval (CIBA) planned for high-risk transactions because users rubber-stamp approvals [VERIFIED: https://engineering.zalando.com/posts/2026/09/agentic-platform-open-sourcing-agentic-identity-broker.html, published 2026-09-25] ⚠.

**For newcomers.** *Delegated access*: a user allows an agent to act with a subset of the user's permissions. *Token exchange*: the broker swaps the agent's identity for a short-lived credential scoped to one tool call, so the agent never holds the user's real credentials.

**Architecture implications** [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT]. Zalando's assistant is a search and discovery feature (the 2023 release is about finding products in natural language); the 2026 broker is the infrastructure side of the same question Amazon answered with a lawsuit: who is this agent, for whom does it act, and what may it do. For the book's AI chapter, pair them: Amazon states the policy for *external* agents; Zalando publishes the identity model for *internal* agents. Starting and Growing merchants will meet both only through their platform's features; Omnichannel and Group merchants need the consent and identity model before they let agents place or change orders.

### B7. What Zalando teaches, and where the evidence stops

[ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT throughout.]

- **Team-sized modularity**: the service count follows the team count (85+ teams in 2016, 110 by December 2016, 4,000+ applications by 2020). The evidence says the split worked for autonomy and then needed alignment (2018), technology limits (2020), a paved road (2023) and, in one case, a merged boundary (2025).
- **API governance** is the durable export: a reviewed-not-approved guild, API-first in OpenAPI, compatibility over versioning, consent-based deprecation. The repository is still active in 2026.
- **Event buses** are necessary once data is distributed (2015 BI post) and insufficient as the only source of current state (2025). Nakadi itself was archived in 2024 for lack of external use.
- **A retailer becoming a platform** proceeds through logistics: marketplace, then ship-from-store, then fulfilment for others, then software; consolidation follows (Connected Retail into the partner programme, 2026–2027).
- **Where the evidence stops**: Zalando has not published its current service inventory, its order or pricing architecture, or the internals of ZEOS; the "2019 reorganisation" exists in this brief only as a sequence of blog posts and a leadership change; the ABOUT YOU integration is one year old and its pro-forma growth (5–10% guided) is the honest measure of the core business.

| Platform move | Prime + FBA, third-party share 3% → 58% (A4) | Partner Program → Connected Retail → ZFS → ZEOS + SCAYLE, 32% partner GMV (B5) |
| Agents | Policy for external agents; own assistant and Buy for Me (A6) | Assistant for discovery; identity broker for delegated agents (B6) |
| Migration style visible in public | Incremental: services carved out of the monolith over years; FBA opened an existing network (A1, A4) | Incremental: shop rebuilt fragment by fragment behind a router (B1); channel technology retired with a 14-month notice (B4) |

The replatforming chapter can use the last row: neither company describes a big-bang cutover. Zalando's shop migration ran header and footer first, then product pages, behind Skipper (2015–2016); its Connected Retail wind-down gives partners from April 2026 to June 2027. Amazon's public record shows capabilities (fulfilment, Prime, checkout) being opened to others one at a time rather than systems being replaced at once.
