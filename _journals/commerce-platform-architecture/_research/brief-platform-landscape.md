# Research brief: The commerce platform landscape in November 2026

Prepared 7 November 2026 for chapter writers of *ORDERED: Architecting Commerce Platforms*. The brief is platform-neutral. It feeds chapters 03 (capability map), 05 (platform landscape), 23 (ERP integration), 29 (architecture styles), 31 (extensibility), 41 (build, buy or assemble), 42 (replatforming), 43 (scaling stages) and 49 (the composable movement case). Vendors get space in proportion to the public, dated evidence about them, not in proportion to their market importance: Shopify, Commerce.com and Lightspeed are listed companies and publish figures; most others publish product pages only.

## How to read this brief

Every substantive claim carries one tag:

- **[VERIFIED: url, published YYYY-MM(-DD)]**: the page was fetched in this session (7 November 2026) and supports the claim. Paraphrases stay close to the source; quoted text is quoted. The date is the source's publication date, not the fetch date. Undated product pages are marked "n.d., accessed 2026-11".
- **[WIDELY REPORTED: url, YYYY]**: from a reputable secondary source (trade press, financial press, encyclopaedia, a vendor's claim relayed by press) rather than a primary document fetched in this session.
- **[ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT]**: industry knowledge or the analyst's inference. Every statement about how a vendor's proprietary system works internally falls here unless the vendor has described it. Chapter writers should re-verify these claims or present them explicitly as reference-architecture judgement.

**Evidence age matters.** Product names, plans and prices in this market change every year; several vendors have renamed themselves since 2024 (BigCommerce → Commerce.com, plentymarkets → PlentyONE, ChannelAdvisor + CommerceHub → Rithum, Magento Commerce → Adobe Commerce). A source older than about three years is flagged **(evidence age)**. Pricing pages are geolocated: Shopify, Stripe and Squarespace served EUR pages to this session, BigCommerce and Wix served USD pages. Quote the currency with the figure.

**Research limitation.** Several primary sites refused automated fetching (HTTP 403 or bot checks): sap.com, netsuite.com, businesswire.com, builtwith.com, nasdaq.com and the Commerce.com investor PDF. As a result:

- **BuiltWith** store counts could not be fetched; usage shares rest on W3Techs alone, which measures websites, not stores or revenue.

## PART 1: Platform categories

### 1.1 The map

| Category | What the merchant buys | Who runs it | Typical pricing | Named examples in this brief |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Hosted SaaS platform | A multi-tenant storefront, cart, checkout, admin and app store | The vendor | Subscription tier plus payment and transaction fees | Shopify, BigCommerce (Commerce.com), Wix, Squarespace, Lightspeed eCom, CCV Shop |
| Enterprise suite | A licensed or cloud commerce engine with B2B/B2C, OMS and personalization modules | Vendor cloud, partner-implemented | Negotiated; often GMV- or order-linked | SAP Commerce Cloud, Salesforce Commerce Cloud (Agentforce Commerce), Adobe Commerce |
| Open source and forks | Source code to host and extend; paid editions and support optional | The merchant or its agency | Free core; paid tiers (subscription or GMV-based) | Magento Open Source, Mage-OS, WooCommerce, Shopware, PrestaShop |
| Composable / headless vendor | API-only commerce services; the merchant assembles storefront and surrounding systems | Vendor cloud, merchant-built front end | Subscription with GMV or order caps | commercetools, Elastic Path, Medusa, Saleor |
| ERP-centred commerce | A storefront bolted to an ERP that already owns stock, orders and finance | Vendor cloud or on-premise | Per user or per order plus ERP licence | Odoo, JTL, PlentyONE, Microsoft Business Central (with Shopify connector), NetSuite SuiteCommerce |
| Specialist layers | One capability each: OMS, PIM, WMS, returns, search, channel integration, iPaaS, payments | Vendor cloud | Subscription, per order, per request, per transaction | Part 2 |
| Specialist groups | Several specialist products under one owner, sold as a connected set | Group | Per product | Katapull (NL) |

[ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT] The table is the analyst's synthesis; categories overlap (Shopware sells SaaS, PaaS and self-hosted; Adobe sells on-premises, PaaS and SaaS; Kibo sells OMS and a full platform).

**For newcomers.** A *hosted (SaaS) platform* is commerce software the vendor runs for many merchants at once; the merchant configures it and installs apps but never touches servers. A *suite* is one vendor's bundle of many commerce capabilities designed to work together. *Headless* means the storefront (what shoppers see) is separated from the commerce engine (catalog, cart, orders) and talks to it only through APIs. *GMV* (gross merchandise value) is the total value of orders placed through a platform, before refunds and before the vendor's own revenue is taken out.

**Architecture implications.** [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT] The category decides where the integration burden lands. A SaaS platform keeps the core in one tenant and pushes variation to apps and webhooks; a suite keeps the integration inside one vendor's data model; composable moves the integration burden to the merchant. For the four scale stages: Starting almost always means hosted SaaS or an ERP-centred package; Growing adds a first OMS-like, PIM-like or channel tool around it; Omnichannel is where the joins (OMS, PIM, WMS, channel integration) become the architecture; Group is where suite-vs-composable and shared-platform questions become real.

**For newcomers.** A *transaction fee* is a percentage the platform charges on each order when the merchant does not use the platform's own payment service; it is separate from the card processing fee. *Plus / Enterprise* tiers are the same product with higher limits, lower transaction fees and a negotiated contract. *ARR* (annual recurring revenue) is subscription revenue annualised; it excludes payment revenue.

**Architecture implications.** [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT] Hosted platforms monetise payments: a merchant who brings its own PSP pays a transaction fee on Shopify (0.2–2%) and BigCommerce (0.6–2% for non-embedded gateways). That fee is an architectural constraint on the payments layer (chapter 13) and on exit cost (chapter 41). The Agentic plan and Feedonomics ACE show the newest surface, AI assistants, being priced as a channel; it fits the Growing and Omnichannel stages as one more channel integration rather than a new platform.

### 1.3 Enterprise suites

**SAP Commerce Cloud.** The open-source Spartacus project is "a lean, Angular-based JavaScript storefront for SAP Commerce Cloud" that talks to the platform only through its REST API; from version 5.0 "SAP Commerce Cloud, composable storefront" is the official name of SAP's published release of the Spartacus libraries, included in the Commerce Cloud licence; the open-source code is Apache-2.0 and "provided as-is with no official lines of support"; from release 2211.19 the storefront versioning aligned with SAP Commerce Cloud [VERIFIED: https://github.com/SAP/spartacus, n.d., accessed 2026-11]. An October 2026 analyst note on SAP Connect 2026 reports an MCP server for Commerce Cloud storefronts, a "SAP Commerce Cloud, cloud ERP edition" available now for midsize organisations wanting ERP-connected commerce, Joule agents inside Order Management due Q1 2027, and warns buyers to "separate products that are available now from forward-looking demonstrations"; it scores Commerce Cloud below category averages for ease of data integration [WIDELY REPORTED: https://www.softwarereviews.com/vendor-technology-notes/sap-s-cx-vision-for-its-autonomous-enterprise-gets-product-general-availability-timeline-at-connect-2026, 2026] ⚠. [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT] SAP's product page could not be fetched; writers should not quote feature lists for SAP Commerce beyond the above.

**Salesforce Commerce Cloud / Agentforce Commerce.** On 6 July 2026 Salesforce announced "its biggest Agentforce Commerce release yet": one platform across B2C, B2B, point of sale and order management, with three generally available agents: a Shopper Agent (discovery to checkout on the brand's storefront), a Buyer Agent (B2B buyers in WhatsApp and SMS) and a Merchant Agent (back-office work in plain language); the agents are "natively connected to catalog, inventory, and orders", with native integrations to ChatGPT and Google Search AI Mode and a Gemini app integration "in the coming months"; the release cites market statistics (AI influenced 20% of global online sales in the 2025 holiday season, $262 billion) rather than product metrics [VERIFIED: https://www.salesforce.com/ap/news/press-releases/2026/07/06/as-ai-agents-transform-commerce-salesforce-unleashes-its-biggest-agentforce-commerce-release-yet/, published 2026-07-06] ⚠. The pricing page shows "Contact for pricing" for every commerce edition in all six currencies; the editions are Commerce Cloud B2C Premium (100 sites, 3,500 price books, full order management), B2B Growth Edition (6 storefronts, Order Management Lite), Salesforce Order Management Growth (up to 50 locations), Retail Cloud Point-of-Sale ("Starting at 25 Stores") and Salesforce Payments with "Bring Your Own Adyen account" [VERIFIED: https://www.salesforce.com/commerce/pricing/, n.d., accessed 2026-11]. [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT] Salesforce Commerce Cloud is widely described in the trade as priced on a percentage of GMV (a competitor's comparison page states "1–3% of GMV" [VERIFIED: https://woocommerce.com/pricing/, n.d., accessed 2026-11]); treat that as an industry belief, not a Salesforce statement.

**Adobe Commerce.** Adobe's information centre (last updated 21 October 2026) lists three deployment options: "Adobe Commerce on-premises" (the merchant or its host runs everything), "Adobe Commerce on cloud infrastructure" (platform-as-a-service: Adobe manages the hosted platform, the merchant manages application-level maintenance, custom code and configuration) and "Adobe Commerce as a Cloud Service" (fully managed SaaS: Adobe manages the core application, infrastructure and updates). **Adobe Commerce Optimizer** is a SaaS for storefront, catalog, product discovery and merchandising that "can work alongside an existing Adobe Commerce or third-party commerce backend", so the transaction system need not be replaced; Cloud Service and Optimizer "don't currently integrate" [VERIFIED: https://experienceleague.adobe.com/en/docs/commerce/information-center/explore-platforms-and-services/platform-and-services, published 2026-10-21] ⚠. The Cloud Service runs on "Adobe-managed SaaS infrastructure", delivers feature and security updates continuously with 30 days to evaluate them in sandbox, uses the headless "Commerce Storefront" on Edge Delivery Services (the classic Luma storefront is not supported) and extends through App Builder, API Mesh, Events and Webhooks [VERIFIED: https://experienceleague.adobe.com/en/docs/commerce/cloud-service/overview, published 2026-10-20]. Its release notes start in November 2025 and by September 2026 it "now contains all changes from Adobe Commerce version 2.4.9" [VERIFIED: https://experienceleague.adobe.com/en/docs/commerce/cloud-service/release-notes, published 2026-11]. Partner and agency reports place the launch of both the Cloud Service and Optimizer in June 2025 after an Adobe Summit announcement in March 2025; no Adobe press release was found [WIDELY REPORTED: https://www.rightpoint.com/thought/article/demystifying-adobe-commerce-as-a-cloud-service, 2025]. History: Adobe announced in May 2018 that it would buy Magento for $1.68 billion, closing 19 June 2018; Magento Open Source is distributed under the Open Software License; Magento Commerce was renamed Adobe Commerce in April 2021 [WIDELY REPORTED: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magento, 2026].

**For newcomers.** *On-premises / PaaS / SaaS* are three answers to "who runs the servers and who applies upgrades": the merchant, the vendor for the platform only, or the vendor for everything. A *Magic Quadrant* is Gartner's annual vendor chart; "Leader", "Challenger", "Visionary" and "Niche" are its four boxes, and vendors pay to reprint it, so always read the report date and vendor count. An *agent* in the 2026 vendor sense is software that acts for a shopper, buyer or merchant through conversation and APIs.

**Architecture implications.** [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT] Two of the three suites now sell a layer that sits *beside* an existing backend (Adobe Commerce Optimizer; Salesforce's agents connecting to catalog, inventory and orders) rather than a rip-and-replace. For a Group-stage merchant that is the realistic path: keep the transaction engine, add discovery and agent layers through APIs. The PaaS deployment of Adobe Commerce is the clearest public example of split responsibility (vendor runs infrastructure, merchant owns code), the arrangement that creates the Forked Platform pattern when the merchant's customisations block upgrades (chapter 31).

**For newcomers.** A *fork* is a copy of an open-source codebase maintained separately; a *distribution* (Mage-OS's term) keeps compatibility with the upstream and adds patches on top. *Open core* means the base is open source and paid editions add proprietary modules (Shopware Rise and above; Medusa's Enterprise Edition). *Fair use* clauses turn "free" software into a paid plan above a revenue threshold.

**Architecture implications.** [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT] Open source moves hosting, upgrades and security patches onto the merchant or its agency: the Mage-OS security port of an Adobe patch shows the dependency chain (Adobe → distribution → agency → merchant) a Growing-stage merchant on Magento lives with. Shopware's GMV-linked pricing and €1M fair-use line mean "open source" is free only below the Growing stage. Shopware Nexus is a platform vendor absorbing the integration layer; chapter 23's hub debate should treat it as "commerce-as-hub" sold by the commerce vendor.

### 1.5 Composable and headless vendors

**For newcomers.** *Composable* means buying commerce as separate services (catalog, cart, checkout, search, content) from one or several vendors and assembling them, rather than taking one suite. *API-only* means there is no vendor storefront at all; the merchant builds or buys one. **Architecture implications.** [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT] The 2025–2026 turn is visible in the vendors' own words: commercetools' AgenticLift and Adobe's Optimizer are both "beside your existing stack" products; Elastic Path sells an iPaaS inside the commerce platform. Composable is being resold as *layers* that attach to a monolith, which is what an Omnichannel-stage merchant can actually absorb. The GMV-capped subscriptions (Saleor, Shopware, BigCommerce) make TCO a function of growth, which chapter 41 should model explicitly.

**For newcomers.** An *ERP* (enterprise resource planning system) keeps the books: purchasing, stock valuation, invoices, the general ledger. *ERP-centred commerce* makes the ERP the system of record for products, stock and orders and treats the webshop as a front end. A *connector* is a vendor-built integration between two named products, maintained by the vendor.

**Architecture implications.** [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT] At the Starting and Growing stages the ERP-centred suite avoids the integration problem by making the ERP the hub; at the Omnichannel stage the same choice becomes the bottleneck when stores, marketplaces and a WMS each need stock in near real time and the ERP's batch cadence cannot serve them (chapter 23). The Business Central connector's token mechanics (90-day refresh, automatic rotation) are a small, documented example of what "a connector as a product" actually maintains.

## PART 3: The MACH Alliance and the composable turn

### 3.1 Origin and growth

- On 24 June 2020 EPAM announced it had joined "the newly formed MACH Alliance" as a founding member with commercetools, Contentstack and Valtech; MACH stands for "Microservices, API-first, Cloud-Native, and Headless"; the group described itself as "a group of independent tech companies dedicated to advocating for open, best-of-breed technology ecosystems" [VERIFIED: https://s202.q4cdn.com/436759741/files/doc_news/2020/06/24/12951.pdf, published 2020-06-24] **(evidence age: founding document, still the authoritative origin)**.
- On 13 December 2023 the Alliance passed 100 members (new joiners included Mirakl, inRiver and Salsify); it then defined the goal as ecosystems that are "Microservices based, API-first, Cloud-native SaaS and Headless"; its 2023 survey of 500 tech leaders found 85% had increased the share of MACH infrastructure in the previous 12 months; the release itself gives two inconsistent applicant figures (over 200 with about half admitted; 225 with about 45%) [VERIFIED: https://machalliance.org/newsroom/mach-alliance-surpasses-100-members-as-it-closes-out-milestone-2023, published 2023-12-13].

### 3.2 The principles as stated now

- On 5 August 2025 the Alliance announced a move to "end-user leadership" by September 2026 (an appointed end-user president, two end-user ambassadors on the board) and listed five guiding principles: Composable, Connected, Incremental, Open and Autonomous; it did not restate the acronym [VERIFIED: https://www.silicon.co.uk/press-release/mach-alliance-advances-end-user-leadership-model-to-guide-enterprise-ai-architecture-evolution, published 2025-08-05].
- The Alliance's principles page today states **three** principles and no longer defines Microservices, API-first, Cloud-native or Headless: "Open is about what the system is made of — and whether humans and agents can see it, trust it, and verify it"; "Composable means capabilities you can assemble, replace, and evolve independently"; "Connected means designed to be optimized in an ecosystem — not in isolation"; the Alliance "certifies ISVs, system integrators, and enablers against these principles" [VERIFIED: https://machalliance.org/mach-principles/, n.d., accessed 2026-11] ⚠. The explainer page likewise says "Three principles." and gives no reason for the change [VERIFIED: https://machalliance.org/mach-explained, n.d., accessed 2026-11].
- [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT] The acronym has outlived its expansion: between 2023 ("Microservices based, API-first, Cloud-native SaaS and Headless"), August 2025 (five principles) and 2026 (three: Open, Composable, Connected) the Alliance has dropped microservices and headless as stated principles and reframed around AI agents. Chapter 49 should quote the three dated formulations side by side.

### 3.3 The Alliance's own survey data

- The MACH Alliance Enterprise Technology Report (18 February 2026; 600 enterprise technology decision-makers in seven markets) reports that 78% of organisations with fully scaled MACH technology see clear ROI from AI versus 13% in early planning; 98% of mature composable adopters can support AI at scale versus 33% of early-stage ones; **37% cite integration complexity as a primary concern** and 45% flag data privacy and security; 89% say standards and certifications for AI in composable environments are missing [VERIFIED: https://machalliance.org/insights-hub/mach-alliance-research-6x-more-organizations-achieve-ai-roi-with-a-composable-foundation, published 2026-02-18]. [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT] The survey is commissioned by a vendor alliance and self-selects for composable adopters; its usable finding for the book is the one against interest: integration complexity remains the top architectural concern among its own constituency.

**For newcomers.** *MACH* is an acronym coined in 2020 for Microservices, API-first, Cloud-native SaaS and Headless; the *MACH Alliance* is the vendor association that promotes it. *Agentic commerce* is the 2025–2026 term for selling through AI assistants (ChatGPT, Gemini, Copilot) that browse catalogs and complete checkouts on a shopper's behalf. *MCP* (Model Context Protocol) is an open protocol by which an AI assistant calls a system's functions; vendors now advertise "MCP coverage".

**Architecture implications.** [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT] For chapter 29, composable is a style whose own champions now sell it incrementally. For the scale stages: Starting and Growing merchants should read "composable" as a vendor category, not an instruction; Omnichannel merchants can adopt it per capability (search, OMS, PIM) around a kept core; only Group-stage merchants with platform teams should consider an API-only core. The Alliance's own 37% integration-complexity figure is the cost line the composable promise omitted.

**For newcomers.** *TTM* means trailing twelve months. *Overage* is the charge above an included quota. A *blended rate* is one card fee for all card types; *interchange* is the part of the card fee set by the card schemes and paid to the issuing bank.

**Architecture implications.** [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT] Pricing models encode the vendor's architecture bet: GMV-based pricing assumes the vendor owns checkout; per-order pricing assumes the vendor sees every order (WMS, channel tools); per-request pricing assumes the vendor sits in the request path (search). A merchant moving from Growing to Omnichannel typically crosses three thresholds at once (platform GMV tier, PSP volume pricing, per-order quotas), which is why chapter 43 should list pricing thresholds as a transition signal.

## PART 6: How to read vendor claims

| Claimed unit | What it usually counts | Dated example | Trap |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **GMV** | Order value through the platform, gross of refunds and before the vendor's cut | Shopify $378.44B in 2025 [WIDELY REPORTED: 2026]; commercetools "$100B run-rate" [VERIFIED: 2026-04-22]; Commerce.com $8.8B in Q2 2026 [VERIFIED: 2026-08-06]; Mirakl $14.6B in 2025 [VERIFIED: 2026-11] | Run-rate vs full year; "GMV from new customers" is a bookings proxy; BigCommerce's plan GMV is "gross order value minus 10%" [VERIFIED: 2026-11], a different definition from its reported GMV |
| **GTV / GPV** | Lightspeed's gross transaction volume (all sales through its POS and eCom) vs gross payments volume (the part it processes) | GTV $22.9B in Q4 FY2026 [VERIFIED: 2026-05-21]; GPV $8.8B in Q3 FY2025 [VERIFIED: 2025-02-06] | GTV counts volume the vendor does not monetise through payments |
| **Merchants / stores / sellers / shops** | Installations, trials, or paying accounts, rarely stated | WooCommerce "4 million stores" [VERIFIED: 2026-11]; PrestaShop "250,000 merchants" (vendor) vs "300,000 shops" (Wikipedia) [VERIFIED/WIDELY REPORTED: 2026]; Shopware "more than 50,000 online sellers" [WIDELY REPORTED: 2025]; Shopify "Millions of businesses in 175+ countries" [VERIFIED: 2026-08-05] | A merchant may run several stores; free and dormant installs inflate open-source counts; W3Techs counts websites, not merchants |
| **Customers / brands / users / enterprises** | Contracting companies (customers), end-user seats (users) or marketing-chosen "brands" | commercetools "500+ enterprises" [VERIFIED: 2026-04-22]; Katapull "4,500+ customers", "27,500+ users" [VERIFIED: 2026-11]; PlentyONE "Over 55,000 users" [VERIFIED: 2026-11]; Loop "5,000+ brands" [VERIFIED: 2026-11]; inRiver "1,600+ brands" [VERIFIED: 2026-11] | "Users" exceeds "customers" by a factor of 5–10; "brands" can count subsidiaries of one customer |
| **ARR / revenue** | Audited (listed companies) or self-reported (private) | Commerce.com ARR $360.5M [VERIFIED: 2026-08-06]; commercetools ARR "far beyond $100 million" as of July 2024 [WIDELY REPORTED: 2025] | ARR excludes payment revenue; for Shopify and Lightspeed the larger revenue line is payments |
| **Implementations / go-lives** | Projects, not customers | Fluent "150+ implementations" [VERIFIED: 2026-11] | One customer, several countries, several implementations |
| **Marketplaces / channels / integrations / apps** | Connector listings, not active connections | ChannelEngine "over 1,300 marketplaces" [VERIFIED: 2026-11]; Rithum "600+" [VERIFIED: 2026-11]; PlentyONE "150+ marketplaces", "500+ integrations" [VERIFIED: 2026-11]; Katapull "140+ marketplaces" [VERIFIED: 2026-11]; Workato "17,000+ apps" [VERIFIED: 2026-11] | A listing proves a connector exists, not that it is maintained, bidirectional or used |
| **Survey percentages** | Respondents of a commissioned survey | MACH "78% ... clear ROI from AI" [VERIFIED: 2026-02-18]; Salesforce "AI influenced 20% of global online sales" [VERIFIED: 2026-07-06] | Sample is chosen by the vendor; "influenced" is undefined |
| **Analyst placements** | A position on a chart for a fee-free evaluation that vendors pay to reprint | Gartner 2025 MQ, 19 vendors [VERIFIED: 2025-11-06]; Forrester Q3 2026, 10 vendors [VERIFIED: 2026-07-22] | Vendor count and inclusion criteria change yearly; absence from a chart is not a verdict |
| **Performance claims** | Vendor-measured, no methodology | Elastic Path "100% uptime over the last two years" [VERIFIED: 2026-11]; Shopware Nexus "cut integration costs by 40%" [VERIFIED: 2026-06-10]; Bloomreach "Boosts RPV by 20% or More" [VERIFIED: 2026-11] | Unaudited; treat as marketing unless a case study with a baseline is attached |

**For newcomers.** *GMV* is sales volume through the platform; *revenue* is what the vendor keeps; a vendor's revenue is typically 1–4% of its GMV [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT: Shopify's 2025 ratio is $11.56B / $378.44B, about 3.1%]. *ARR* is annualised subscription revenue. A *merchant* is a company that sells; a *store* is one storefront, and one merchant may have many.

**Architecture implications.** [ASSUMPTION/JUDGEMENT] The units matter because architecture decisions are made from them: a PIM or OMS vendor quoting "implementations" is telling you about project cost; a platform quoting GMV is telling you about its payment and fee leverage; a channel tool quoting "1,300 marketplaces" is telling you about connector breadth, not depth. Chapter 05 should teach the reader to ask for the unit, the date and the definition before comparing any two vendors.
