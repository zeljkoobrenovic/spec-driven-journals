A security policy in my organization states **what must be achieved** — the how lives in standards and procedures. The language is mandatory or it is not policy: must, will, shall, and do, never should, try, and mostly, with exceptions documented. Every policy is a governed document, the set is managed deliberately, and policies are living documents reviewed at least annually. The test the set is held to: any employee can find the current approved version of the policy that applies to them and understand what it requires — and nobody can find an obsolete one.

**What changes**

* **Policy and implementation split cleanly.** Policies state outcomes and boundaries tied to a security objective; tool names, configurations, and step-by-step instructions move to standards and procedures — so the mandate stays durable while the technology churns.
* **The language becomes binding.** Mandatory requirements use must, will, shall, and do; ambiguous terms are banned for anything mandatory; permitted exceptions are documented rather than whispered; legalese is out, short clear statements are in.
* **Every policy becomes a governed document.** Version number, effective date, revision history, named owner, named approver, executive sign-off where required, explicit purpose and scope, and references to related documents and regulatory requirements.
* **Coverage becomes a decision, not an accident.** The set is walked against the chapter's full list — acceptable use through equipment disposal, third-party responsibilities, and industry-specific policies — and each entry is deliberately adopted or deliberately declined.
* **The set gets a lifecycle.** Separate manageable documents, framework-aligned, centrally stored under revision control, formally approved, communicated to affected personnel, backed up — with physical copies of critical policies — and reviewed at least annually and after significant change, with obsolete versions archived.

**What it costs**

* Real governance overhead per document — owners, approvers, revision records, and re-approval on every change — accepted because an unowned policy is a rumor.
* An annual review cycle that consumes leadership attention even in years when the answer is "still true, re-dated."
* Splitting policy from procedure means maintaining two document layers instead of one — the price of mandates that survive tool swaps.

**What we are not doing**

* Not writing the how here — configuration baselines and step-by-step instructions are [[standards-and-procedures]].
* Not defining the substance of each named policy — incident response and disaster recovery get their operating content in [[incident-response]] and [[disaster-recovery]].
* Not treating communication as education — making policies known happens here; making them lived is [[user-education]].
