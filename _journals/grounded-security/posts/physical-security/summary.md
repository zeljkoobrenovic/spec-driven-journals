Physical access is a security boundary of the same rank as the network, and my organization defends it the same way — **in layers, with revocation as disciplined as granting**. Secure areas sit behind real controls and highly sensitive areas behind more than one; badges, keys, and codes are recovered or changed the day someone leaves; media is tracked like the data it carries; visitors and contractors are identified, escorted, and time-boxed; and every employee is trained that politeness does not override procedure. The test the estate is held to: no single failed control — a held door, a cloned badge, a confident pretext — is enough to reach sensitive systems or data.

**What changes**

* **Access becomes layered and maintained, not just installed.** Highly sensitive areas get more than one control; keypads, badges, and readers are audited on a schedule; departures trigger same-day badge recovery, prompt permission removal, and changed shared codes; role changes trigger access reviews. Shared PINs stop guarding anything highly sensitive. Racks lock, rack keys are centrally controlled and logged, and remote-office equipment gets secured enclosures.
* **Surveillance becomes evidence, not decoration.** Cameras cover entrances and high-risk rooms, positioned to capture faces and correlate badge use with the person using the badge, protected from tampering — and footage is actually reviewed and correlated with badge logs when investigating.
* **Media gets a chain of custody.** Removable and backup media is secured, locked away, labeled by classification, moved with approval and secure couriers, and every transfer is recorded; storage locations are inspected periodically.
* **The perimeter extends to people.** Visitors sign in and out, are escorted, and need verified purpose and employee approval before touching anything; contractors show verified ID, are vetted, and work within policy-defined, area-limited access; badges visibly differ by type, expire with the visit, and are disabled promptly when lost.
* **The human layer is trained against the classic plays.** Tailgating, badge cloning, malicious USB drops, and pretexting each get explicit training and controls — individual authentication at secure doors, second factors where cloning risk warrants it, restricted media ports, and verify-before-admitting as the standing reflex.
* **Physical security runs as a program.** Information security and facilities coordinate it, penetration tests include it, policies are reviewed, new threats identified, and gaps corrected — defense-in-depth by design.

**What it costs**

* Escorting, sign-in, and verification add friction to every visit — accepted deliberately, because helpfulness at the door is the attack surface.
* Badge audits, camera reviews, media records, and periodic control testing are standing work, not a one-time fit-out.
* Training against tailgating and pretexting must recur; this control decays fastest.

**What we are not doing**

* Not putting biometric locks on every door — controls are proportionate to what each area holds.
* Not banning removable media — governing it with classification, tracking, and approval.
* Not treating this as facilities' problem alone — the program is jointly owned, and the broader training curriculum lives in [[user-education]].
