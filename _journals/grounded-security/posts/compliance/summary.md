My organization treats compliance as the floor, never the program. **Compliance standards are minimum requirements** — an organization can be fully compliant and still be weak — so the map layers stay straight: laws and regulations bind us, compliance standards operationalize them, and security frameworks are the instruments we choose to actually build with. For every regulated data type we hold, we can name the regulation that governs it and the enforcement behind it. The test the posture is held to: being compliant is never mistaken for being secure.

**What changes**

* **Every regulated data type gets a named regulation.** Student records map to FERPA, nonpublic personal information to GLBA, ePHI to HIPAA, cardholder data to PCI DSS, financial-reporting controls to SOX — each with its enforcement reality, from banking regulators and HHS to the card brands' power to switch off processing.
* **Frameworks are chosen by the job, not collected.** CIS to harden systems, CSA's Cloud Controls Matrix for cloud mapped to compliance standards, COSO and COBIT for the board-and-auditor layer, ISO 27001 for a certifiable ISMS, MITRE ATT&CK for adversary behavior, NIST CSF — Core, Profiles, Tiers — for the program's common risk language.
* **Regulated industries are read structurally.** Finance's standing attack list on legacy systems, government's sensitive data behind slow approvals (CMMC, DoD Impact Levels, FedRAMP on NIST SP 800-53, FISMA), healthcare's unpatchable devices and HITRUST's harmonization of HIPAA, NIST, ISO, and PCI DSS — the regulation exists where the risk concentrates.
* **The literacy stays with the executive.** Compliance officers coordinate, but the chapter's final review is written in the first person — I can explain, match, and distinguish — because category confusion at the top becomes control failure at the bottom.
* **Audit evidence becomes a by-product.** With the mappings and frameworks operationalized, an audit is retrieval from a real program, not an annual evidence factory.

**What it costs**

* Real study: the regulation map and framework shelf must be genuinely learned at the leadership level, not delegated away.
* Double bookkeeping by design: tracking the gap between compliant and secure means funding controls no assessor asked for.
* Scope discipline: keeping regulated data mapped requires standing effort with asset inventory, not a pre-audit sprint.

**What we are not doing**

* Not treating compliance as optional — the floor is mandatory; it is just not the building.
* Not building the security program here — that is [[security-program]]; the documents that face the audit are [[policies]] and [[standards-and-procedures]].
* Not issuing legal opinions — counsel interprets statutes; this record owns the executive map, with data location owned by [[asset-management]].
