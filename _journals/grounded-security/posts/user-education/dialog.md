## Users as a Control

**Ben:** Let me open with the industry's dirty secret: security awareness training is the control everyone runs and nobody believes in. Click rates barely move, the modules are despised, and the whole thing exists so an auditor can tick a box. Why write a record defending it?

**Ana:** Because the thing you just described — the annual compliance module — is the record's first anti-pattern, not its subject. The chapter's claim is different: your people are a security control, and controls get engineered, not lectured. The person who receives the phish sees it before any analyst does. A workforce trained to recognize suspicious requests, with a one-click reporting path, is a detection sensor at every mailbox — the widest coverage of any control in the estate. The record is about engineering that sensor properly.

**Ben:** Engineering how? Every vendor says "ongoing program" too.

**Ana:** Start with what the record refuses: a training catalog. One or two clear, achievable objectives — not twelve. Tailored to our threat landscape, not the vendor's scenario library. With executive support and cultural fit, because a program the organization resents is a program the organization routes around. And then the discipline most programs skip entirely: the baseline.

## The Baseline

**Ben:** Right, the baseline. Measuring what users know before training them. Isn't that just an extra assessment nobody asked for — a delay before the real work?

**Ana:** It's the difference between engineering and ritual. A program launched without measuring current behavior can never demonstrate improvement — only activity. So before launch: measure current knowledge and behavior, run realistic exercises to find *our* common weaknesses, assess how users actually react to phishing and unusual requests today, and set measurable targets against those results. Every claim the program makes afterward — reporting up, clicks down — is only credible against that starting line.

**Ben:** And "realistic exercises" means phishing simulations. Which brings us to the ugliest part of this industry: the gotcha test. Fake bonus announcements. Fake layoff notices. Security teams bragging about click rates they engineered to be high.

**Ana:** The record bans that outright — it's the gotcha simulation anti-pattern. Simulations should closely resemble the threats we actually receive, because the goal is recognition, not humiliation. A test designed to maximize clicks produces two things: a scary number for a slide deck, and a workforce that resents and conceals. Both are anti-outcomes. The exercise exists to build the habit — hover, verify, report — and to measure whether the habit is forming.

## Never Punish the Report

**Ben:** Now the part executives will push back on hardest. No punishment for reporting a mistake — even after clicking a malicious link. Where's the accountability? If someone clicks every quarter, do we just keep sending them encouragement?

**Ana:** Separate two things the record is careful to separate. Reporting is never punished — that's absolute, and it's cold economics, not softness. Punish the click and the next click goes unreported; the attacker gets hours or days of quiet instead of minutes. The cheapest incident my organization will ever have is the one reported in minutes by the person it happened to. Fear teaches concealment, and concealment is the expensive failure mode.

**Ben:** And the serial clicker?

**Ana:** Willful, repeated policy violation is a management matter — it sits outside this program, and the record says so explicitly. What the program owns is making the safe behavior easy and rewarded: simple reporting, recognition, small incentives, gamification where it fits the culture, a supportive environment instead of fear or shame. You want the person who just clicked to have exactly one instinct: report it now.

**Ben:** Rewarding reporting will buy you a pile of false positives. Every newsletter becomes a ticket.

**Ana:** Accepted, and cheerfully. False positives are the price of fast detection, and triage is cheaper than dwell time. A user who reports ten harmless emails and one real phish has paid for the program. The metric the record actually worries about runs the other way — the suspicious emails *not* reported. That number is on the tracking list precisely because silence is the failure mode.

## The Exercise Tests Us Too

**Ben:** There's an incident-response section in this checklist that surprised me. Testing IR procedures during awareness exercises? That's scope creep — education program grading the response team?

**Ana:** It's the cheapest integration test the response program will ever get. Every simulation is a live rehearsal of the whole path: the user reports, the report lands somewhere, someone triages, a runbook fires. The record requires defining what users do after identifying or falling for an attack, testing the existing procedures during exercises, and feeding the gaps back into updated procedures — not just updated slides. Because here's the failure nobody plans for: the simulation succeeds, the user dutifully reports, and the report goes nowhere. The lesson that user learns is that reporting is pointless — the untested reporting path anti-pattern. That's our defect, not theirs.

**Ben:** So a failed exercise might indict the security team rather than the users.

**Ana:** Regularly does, and that's the point. The exercise tests the humans *and* the machinery — [[incident-response]] gets rehearsed, [[phishing-response]] gets its sensors calibrated, and the education program gets honest about whether the easy option is actually easy.

## Behavior, Not Completion

**Ben:** Close on the measurement question. Every awareness program produces a dashboard. What makes this one worth believing?

**Ana:** What it refuses to count as success. Completion rates measure attendance — the record calls reporting them "completion-rate theater." The honest metrics are behavioral: click rates, credential submissions, reporting rates, unreported suspicious emails — all trended against the original baseline, reviewed periodically, never judged from a single assessment. And the program itself stays alive: topics adjust to emerging threats and performance data, objectives update as the organization's maturity improves. The same curriculum two years running is its own anti-pattern.

**Ben:** What do you actually show the board?

**Ana:** Two lines. The reporting-rate trend and the click-rate trend, both against the baseline. The first question after every simulation is whether the reporting path and response procedures worked. Completion percentages appear nowhere in that conversation.

**Ben:** Then here's my concession, and it's a real one. This record isn't defending awareness training — it's replacing it. Measure before you teach, drill what's real, make reporting the easy and safe option, let every exercise test your own response machinery, and accept only one definition of success: people behaving differently than the baseline says they used to.

**Ana:** That's the record in one line. The module produces certificates; the program produces reports in minutes — and the report in minutes is the whole game.
