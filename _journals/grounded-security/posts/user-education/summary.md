My organization treats its people as a security control, and controls get engineered, not lectured. **Security awareness is an ongoing program, not a once-a-year compliance module**: one or two clear objectives tailored to our actual threat landscape, a measured baseline before launch, training built on repetition and realistic exercises, a reporting path so simple that reporting is the easy option, and a hard rule that nobody is punished for reporting a mistake — even after clicking. The test the program is held to: behavior change measured against the baseline, not course-completion rates.

**What changes**

* **The program gets a focus.** One or two clear, achievable objectives replace the training catalog, and every exercise, message, and metric points at them — with executive support and cultural fit, not just budget.
* **A baseline comes first.** Before any training runs, we measure what users currently know and do through realistic exercises — how they react to phishing, suspicious links, and unusual requests — and set measurable improvement targets against that starting line.
* **Training becomes habit-building.** Spaced, repeated, hands-on practice with examples that closely resemble the threats we actually receive, framed in each user's everyday work; interactive activities supplement the computer-based module instead of being replaced by it.
* **Reporting becomes the easy, safe option.** What to do about suspicious activity is explicit, the reporting path is one click, good behavior is rewarded — and reporting is never punished, even after the malicious link was clicked, because a click reported in minutes is the cheapest incident we will ever have.
* **Exercises test the response machinery.** Every simulation doubles as a rehearsal of the reporting path and incident-response procedures; gaps found flow back into updated procedures, and the metrics loop — sent, opened, clicked, submitted, reported — is reviewed periodically against the baseline, with topics adjusted to emerging threats.

**What it costs**

* Standing work, not a yearly event: simulations, reinforcement, and metric reviews recur indefinitely.
* Baseline discipline delays the visible launch — measuring first feels slower than shipping training on day one.
* Rewarding reporting means absorbing false positives; that noise is the accepted price of fast detection.

**What we are not doing**

* Not running gotcha simulations designed to humiliate — they teach concealment, the expensive failure mode.
* Not handling the operational response to real phish — that is [[phishing-response]], fed by the sensors this program trains.
* Not writing the rules users are trained on — that is [[policies]]; the response machinery the exercises rehearse is [[incident-response]].
