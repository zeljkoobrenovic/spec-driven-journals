Before anything in my organization earns the name **platform**, it passes a test: a platform **elevates participants** by letting them build on existing capabilities instead of starting from scratch, and it **generates value through interaction** — a platform without participants is worth little, and a product relabeled "platform" is worth nothing extra. The bar comes from the automotive industry: **harmonize the expensive, undifferentiated engineering underneath so that diversity *increases* on top** — when standardization suppresses differentiation, we have built a constraint with a fashionable name.

**What changes**

* Every initiative claiming the name answers four questions: **who are the participants, what interaction creates the value, what is harmonized underneath, and what stays variable on top**. If the answers are "one team, none, everything, nothing", it gets renamed back and funded honestly as a product or shared service.
* The five platform benefits — **enable, democratize, self-perpetuate, accelerate, avoid unnecessary constraints** — become a diagnostic, not a brochure. The last one bites hardest internally: something reusable that forces every team into the same solution is a defect, not a benefit.
* The harmonized-vs-variable line is the design decision that matters. Get it wrong one way and nothing is reused; wrong the other way and you get **badge engineering** — near-identical products with superficial differences, the platform eating the diversity it was meant to enable.
* The four technology platform types — **marketplace, base, developer, business capability** — are named explicitly so each is judged by its own economics: no marketplace flywheel expectations on a base platform, no developer-productivity metrics on a business capability platform. They combine and layer; ecosystems are fractal.
* **Access is a first-class capability.** Console, CLI, APIs, automation — how users reach the platform matters as much as what is inside it. A powerful platform behind a ticket queue is a weak platform.

**What it costs**

* Enforcing the definition costs comfortable labels: some current "platforms" lose the name, the halo, and the budget framing that came with it.
* Holding the harmonization line is ongoing design work — deciding what belongs underneath versus on top, and re-deciding as the landscape shifts, rather than standardizing by default.

**What we are not doing**

* Not writing the strategy or the design mechanics — [[platform-strategy]] governs why and where we build platforms, [[designing-platforms]] governs interfaces and abstractions.
* Not restating the platform-engineering discipline case — that is [[what-is-platform-engineering]]; this record is Hohpe's vocabulary-and-taxonomy foundation.
* Not cataloging our internal platforms — this is the test they must pass, not the inventory.
