I judge a platform organization on **four dimensions at once — aligned, trusted, managing complexity, and loved** — and none of them is adoption for adoption's sake. A platform succeeds when it makes the organization's technology easier to operate and evolve and makes its users materially better at their jobs, ideally by making difficult things feel boring.

**What changes**

* Platform reviews start from a **conjunctive test**: are the teams aligned (shared purpose, coordinated product strategy, jointly planned investments with conflicts surfaced and disagree-and-commit), trusted, managing complexity, and loved? A strong score on one dimension does not compensate for the others — weakness on any one eventually drags down the rest.
* **Trust is checked in three registers**: operationally (reliability demonstrated at the customer's scale, adoption staged so confidence is earned before critical workloads move), in major investments (stakeholder buy-in, business outcomes in every proposal, incremental checkpoints instead of a big bang, legacy maintained while replacements mature), and in delivery (self-service instead of a bottleneck, capacity for the unplanned) — backed by composable architecture with escape hatches.
* **Complexity must be removed, not relocated.** Less glue code counts only if human glue — meetings, manual coordination, tribal knowledge, escalation — went down too. The honest question is whether the organization's *total* cost of operating and evolving its technology dropped, and headcount stops being the default answer: toil is reduced before teams grow.
* **Loved gets an engineering definition**: it just works, opinionated but pierceable, recurring friction hunted down, migration part of the product rather than homework for customers.
* **Metrics must reflect reality**: voluntary adoption distinguished from mandated adoption, surveys that discover rather than validate, quantitative metrics paired with qualitative feedback — improvements in users' ability to do their jobs, not vanity numbers.

**What it costs**

* The conjunctive test is deliberately unforgiving — no single score, no compensating dimension, and evidence has to come from the customer's side, which is more work than reading the team's dashboard.
* Staged trust is slower than big-bang promises: incremental checkpoints, and legacy systems maintained while their replacements mature.
* Growth restraint — headcount requests arrive after the toil-reduction work, and existing features are periodically re-justified against their maintenance cost.

**What we are not doing**

* Not redefining what a platform *is* — that is [[four-pillars]]; this record defines how I judge whether the organization built on those pillars is succeeding.
* Not the product-discovery mechanics — those live in [[platform-as-a-product]]; discovery appears here only as one success signal among many.
* Not a metrics catalog — no KPI definitions, dashboards, or survey templates; the record fixes the dimensions and the judgment, not the instrumentation.
