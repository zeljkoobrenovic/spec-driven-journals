# Spec: What Platform Success Looks Like

> Working doc for the post in this folder. The spec drives the post; the post
> is the artifact.

## Intent

State the four-dimensional success test I hold every platform organization
to: platforms are **aligned** (shared purpose, coordinated product strategy,
jointly planned investments), **trusted** (operationally, in major
investments, and in delivery), **managing complexity** (removing it rather
than relocating it into human coordination, with controlled growth and real
product discovery), and **loved** (they just work, friction disappears,
users become more capable, and metrics reflect reality rather than vanity).
The post turns the "What Success Looks Like" chapter of Fournier & Nowland's
*Platform Engineering* into an operating principle: adoption numbers and
shipped roadmaps are not success; a platform succeeds when it makes the
organization's technology easier to operate and evolve and makes its users
materially better at their jobs — ideally by making difficult things feel
boring. The load-bearing idea: success is judged from the customer's side of
the platform, on all four dimensions at once.

## Audience

Platform leaders in my organization who need to know what they are being
judged on; the executives and stakeholders who fund platforms and need a
sharper question than "what is adoption?"; platform engineers and product
managers calibrating what "done well" means. First-person declarative.

## Success criteria

- [x] **Principle is quotable** — highlight states the four dimensions
      (aligned, trusted, managing complexity, loved), rejects adoption for
      adoption's sake, and lands on making difficult things boring.
- [x] **Alignment survives** — shared purpose with a product mindset,
      coordinated (not siloed) product strategy, deliberately consolidated
      overlaps, joint planning of major investments with surfaced conflicts,
      disagree-and-commit, and reorganization only when misalignment cost
      exceeds disruption.
- [x] **Trust survives in all three registers** — operational trust
      (demonstrated reliability, staged adoption), trust in major
      investments (stakeholder buy-in, incremental checkpoints, maintained
      legacy), and trust in delivery (not a bottleneck, self-service,
      capacity for the unplanned) — plus the architecture that preserves it
      (composable blocks, escape hatches).
- [x] **Complexity management survives** — removing rather than relocating
      complexity, less glue code and less human glue, shadow platforms as
      signals, headcount not the default answer, product discovery toward
      narrower abstractions.
- [x] **Loved survives** — "it just works," opinionated but pierceable,
      friction hunted down, easy to embrace, users made more capable, and
      metrics that reflect reality (voluntary vs. forced adoption,
      representative surveys, no vanity metrics).
- [x] **The leadership check survives** — the checklist carries the
      chapter's "Platform Leadership Is Working" section (mixed expertise,
      shared context over command-and-control, confronting trade-offs,
      transparent decisions, investment in existing systems, learning from
      friction); the article points at it in How to Read This rather than
      carrying it as a fifth dimension.
- [x] **The overall test survives** — the four closing questions appear as
      the record's operational core.
- [x] **Credit is explicit** — References name Fournier & Nowland's
      *Platform Engineering* and the "What Success Looks Like" chapter.

## Non-goals

- Not [[four-pillars]] — the pillars define what a platform *is*; this
  record defines how I judge whether the resulting organization is
  *succeeding*.
- Not [[platform-as-a-product]] — product discovery mechanics live there;
  this record uses discovery only as one success signal among many.
- Not a metrics catalog — no specific KPI definitions, dashboards, or
  survey templates; this record fixes the dimensions and the judgment, not
  the instrumentation.
