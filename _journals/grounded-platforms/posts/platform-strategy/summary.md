A platform strategy in my organization is a **real strategy or it is not approved**: derived from our business strategy and our unique context — never copied from someone else's success story — and aimed at increasing our **rate of change**, not at reaching a fixed target state. Before I sign one, it passes the **ACED test** — Alignment, Clarity, Evolution, and above all actual **Decisions** — because a strategy that makes no choices is a wish list wearing a strategy's clothes.

**What changes**

* Every platform strategy arrives as an explicit four-layer chain — **context → objectives → mechanisms → design decisions** — so business readers and engineers see the same story. The chain exists to kill the **IT hourglass**: business goals on top, technology inventory below, nothing connecting them.
* Buzzwords stop counting as mechanisms. "We will do DevOps" explains nothing; a mechanism is a testable claim about how a platform capability produces a business outcome. My first read of any strategy is the waist of the hourglass — mechanism sentences and design trade-offs before anything else.
* Strategies must describe **transformation, not optimization**: which ways of working change, which old constraints disappear — and they claim the "and": speed *and* quality through automation, speed *and* compliance through guardrails. New technology under old habits goes back.
* Governance is stated as **centralized expertise, decentralized usage**: automation and shared responsibility do the governing, not approval queues and tickets — and no all-encompassing IT pyramid that predicts every use case.
* The strategy meets reality through a **mapped landscape** (Wardley-style component evolution, refreshed with usage data) and a roadmap of **point, path, and terrain** — starting from our actual current state, naming decision points, alternative paths, and the data each future choice will need. Tactics flex; direction holds.

**What it costs**

* Making actual decisions costs consensus: a strategy everyone instantly agrees with usually decided nothing, so ACED's D will send popular documents back for rework.
* Decentralizing usage means deliberately relinquishing some central control so teams can build what we did not anticipate — uncomfortable, and non-negotiable.
* Maintaining a living landscape map and revisiting decision points is ongoing work, not a one-time approval artifact.

**What we are not doing**

* Not redefining what a platform is — that vocabulary is [[understanding-platforms]]; and not doing design mechanics, which live in [[designing-platforms]].
* Not execution — [[implementing-platforms]] and [[planning-and-delivery]] pick up where the roadmap's first decision point begins.
* Not a template for our current strategy document — this is the standard any such document must meet.
