*The working checklist behind this record. The Article tab carries the rationale and anti-patterns.*

## 1. Map Your Stakeholders

- [ ] Identify the stakeholders who can materially affect the team's success
- [ ] Assess each stakeholder's power within the organization
- [ ] Assess each stakeholder's interest in the team or platform
- [ ] Place stakeholders on a power–interest grid:
  - [ ] High power / high interest: manage closely
  - [ ] High power / low interest: keep satisfied
  - [ ] Low power / high interest: keep informed
  - [ ] Low power / low interest: monitor with minimum effort
- [ ] Identify who has the ear of the most senior leadership
- [ ] Determine which teams are considered indispensable versus "nice to have"
- [ ] Identify the loudest and most respected stakeholder groups
- [ ] Consider how much it would matter if a stakeholder strongly disliked the team
- [ ] Revisit the map as organizational priorities and relationships change

## 2. Prioritize Relationship-Building

- [ ] Spend the most relationship-management time on high-power, high-interest stakeholders
- [ ] Build relationships before a crisis or escalation occurs
- [ ] Understand each stakeholder's goals, pressures, incentives, and concerns
- [ ] Treat stakeholder disagreements as differences in perspective rather than automatically as "politics"
- [ ] Remember that stakeholders may be optimizing for a different part of the business
- [ ] Look for patterns across stakeholder complaints instead of reacting to every complaint individually

## 3. Communicate With the Right Level of Transparency

- [ ] Decide the main message stakeholders should take away before communicating
- [ ] Lead with outcomes, impact, risks, decisions, and next steps
- [ ] Avoid overwhelming stakeholders with unnecessary technical or operational detail
- [ ] Provide additional detail when a stakeholder specifically asks for it
- [ ] Adjust the level of technical detail to the audience
- [ ] Avoid inviting stakeholders to micromanage the team's day-to-day work
- [ ] Avoid sending so much information that important signals get lost
- [ ] Do not rely on engineering detail alone to resolve relationship or trust problems
- [ ] Make business impact visible rather than assuming stakeholders understand it

## 4. Use 1:1 Meetings Deliberately

- [ ] Use early 1:1s to understand stakeholders' concerns, power, interest, and motivations
- [ ] Use regular 1:1s primarily with the most important stakeholders
- [ ] Avoid creating a meeting cadence that cannot scale
- [ ] Reduce unnecessary 1:1s when the relationship is healthy and little discussion is needed
- [ ] Consider roughly monthly 1:1s for "manage closely" stakeholders
- [ ] Consider roughly quarterly 1:1s for "keep satisfied" or "keep informed" stakeholders
- [ ] Use private conversations for sensitive issues rather than relying solely on broad updates

## 5. Track Expectations and Commitments

- [ ] Take private notes after stakeholder conversations
- [ ] Record what was agreed to investigate, deliver, or follow up on
- [ ] Record dates or expected timelines where relevant
- [ ] Send a short written follow-up when an agreement could be misunderstood
- [ ] Give stakeholders the opportunity to correct your understanding
- [ ] Review outstanding commitments regularly
- [ ] Close the loop when work is completed, delayed, or changed
- [ ] Avoid making promises that are not captured anywhere

## 6. Run Effective Interlock Meetings or Advisory Boards

- [ ] Establish a regular forum for multiple stakeholders when individual 1:1s no longer scale
- [ ] Determine the desired outcome for each meeting
- [ ] Decide what information and decisions will be presented
- [ ] Decide where stakeholder input is actually needed
- [ ] Structure the meeting so stakeholders can participate without letting it devolve into an unbounded debate
- [ ] Prepare the appropriate product, engineering, and operational owners
- [ ] Use product leaders for roadmap and feature discussions
- [ ] Include engineering leaders when operational or delivery matters require technical context
- [ ] Surface disagreements between stakeholders where useful so the team is not caught between contradictory demands
- [ ] Capture decisions, commitments, owners, and next steps

## 7. Adjust Communication During Rough Patches

- [ ] Keep communication lightweight when the platform is stable and stakeholders are satisfied
- [ ] Increase communication when reliability, delivery, cost, or confidence deteriorates
- [ ] Explain what is happening without hiding significant problems
- [ ] State what is being done about the issue
- [ ] Clarify owners, timelines, risks, and expected next updates
- [ ] Supplement meetings with written updates when the situation changes quickly
- [ ] Continue increased communication until the rough patch has genuinely passed

## 8. Handle Disagreements and Requests

- [ ] Make sure you understand the stakeholder's underlying business need
- [ ] Gather enough information to understand the business impact
- [ ] Avoid assuming the existing roadmap is automatically correct
- [ ] Evaluate the value of current work against the stakeholder's request
- [ ] Explain trade-offs in terms of business impact rather than internal preferences
- [ ] Look for compromises involving scope, timing, ownership, or sequencing
- [ ] Escalate, when necessary, to individuals with broader organizational responsibility
- [ ] Avoid treating every disagreement as something that must have a single "right" answer

## 9. Say "Yes, With Compromises" When Appropriate

- [ ] Consider accommodating high-value requests that require little sacrifice
- [ ] Look for a smaller first version that solves the most important need
- [ ] Limit the scope rather than automatically rejecting the entire request
- [ ] Negotiate timing if immediate delivery is unrealistic
- [ ] Consider whether the requesting team can contribute engineering effort
- [ ] Be explicit about what is and is not included
- [ ] Explain the impact of reprioritization to other affected stakeholders
- [ ] Avoid becoming a feature factory that says yes to everything

## 10. Say "No" Without Damaging the Relationship

- [ ] Determine whether the answer is truly no or merely not yet
- [ ] If it is "not yet," explain what must happen before the request can be prioritized
- [ ] Offer a realistic future point for reconsideration where possible
- [ ] If the blocker is technical, explain the technical constraint in understandable terms
- [ ] If the request conflicts with product strategy, explain the platform's core mission and boundaries
- [ ] If the request is technically infeasible, say so clearly rather than pretending it is simply a prioritization issue
- [ ] Suggest alternatives, workarounds, or another appropriate system where possible
- [ ] Keep the conversation focused on the problem rather than on ego or who is "right"
- [ ] Ask your manager or another leader for help if you lack the authority to give a firm no

## 11. Manage Shadow Platforms Constructively

- [ ] Identify why another team wants to build its own solution
- [ ] Determine whether the driver is urgency, novel demand, poor collaboration, disagreement over operating costs, or engineers simply wanting to build
- [ ] Address "us versus them" dynamics early
- [ ] Build relationships with teams before they begin creating competing systems
- [ ] Partner with teams when they have a legitimate, urgent need the platform cannot yet satisfy
- [ ] Learn from novel solutions rather than automatically trying to stop them
- [ ] Set clear expectations about long-term ownership and support
- [ ] Decide whether successful innovations should later be absorbed into the platform
- [ ] Accept that occasionally the team may need to clean up or operationalize a useful solution built elsewhere

## 12. Prepare for Budget and Cost Pressure

- [ ] Assume that roadmaps alone will not justify the team during a downturn
- [ ] Connect platform work directly to protected business outcomes
- [ ] Identify which customers or initiatives will benefit from each major project
- [ ] Review work outside essential operational or "keep the lights on" responsibilities
- [ ] Pause speculative projects that are early, unclear, or expensive
- [ ] Shrink projects when part of the investment can still deliver meaningful value
- [ ] Build a clear business case for projects you strongly believe should continue
- [ ] Seek senior sponsorship for important initiatives where appropriate

## 13. Organize Work for Budget Discussions

- [ ] Group work into meaningful project teams rather than presenting person-by-person activities
- [ ] Use team-sized chunks that leadership can understand and evaluate
- [ ] Avoid creating a false impression of precision about individual utilization
- [ ] Identify whole initiatives that could be reduced, delayed, or removed
- [ ] Protect the teams and work that contribute most directly to critical outcomes

## 14. Arrive With Your Own Cost-Cutting Proposal

- [ ] Decide in advance what could reasonably be reduced or eliminated
- [ ] Base recommendations on customer feedback, stakeholder input, business value, and operational necessity
- [ ] Be prepared to defend the work you believe is essential
- [ ] Avoid defending every project equally
- [ ] Show that you understand the seriousness of the financial situation
- [ ] Offer concrete trade-offs rather than waiting for executives to choose cuts for you
- [ ] Use established stakeholder trust to influence where cuts are made

## 15. Final Relationship Health Check

- [ ] Do stakeholders understand what the team does and why it matters?
- [ ] Do the most powerful stakeholders trust my judgment?
- [ ] Can I identify what each important stakeholder cares about?
- [ ] Are commitments documented and followed through?
- [ ] Are important disagreements surfaced early?
- [ ] Are communication channels appropriate for each stakeholder group?
- [ ] Can I say "no" without creating unnecessary hostility?
- [ ] Can I make reasonable compromises without losing sight of the platform strategy?
- [ ] Am I communicating more actively when circumstances deteriorate?
- [ ] Can I explain the team's value in business terms?
- [ ] Do I already know what I would protect, reduce, or stop if budgets tightened?
- [ ] Are my stakeholder relationships strong enough to survive a difficult quarter?
