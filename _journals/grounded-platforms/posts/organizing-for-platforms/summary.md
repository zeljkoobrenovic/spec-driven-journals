I organize for platforms the way one runs a small product company inside the organization: a cross-functional team with an accountable leader and named ownership of technology strategy, product roadmap, engineering delivery, marketing and adoption, and support — roles that may share people but must never go unowned. Platforms fail organizationally before they fail technically, so the relationships matter as much as the boxes.

**What changes**

* Every platform we run has a named accountable leader plus an owner for each of the five functions — strategy, roadmap, delivery, marketing, support — even when two hats sit on one head. An unowned role does not disappear; it fails silently: no marketing owner means no adoption, no support owner means burned-out engineers.
* We align **east–west**: infrastructure, operations, networking, security, storage, and compute work toward common outcomes behind one cohesive interface. Users never navigate our org chart — and where collaboration is genuinely impossible, we explicitly decide to fix, wrap, or work around the dependency.
* We align **north–south**: adoption is an ongoing relationship earned through value — a recognizable identity, a clear value proposition, regular updates, real feedback channels — never a mandate rolled out once.
* Customer needs come before solutions: observed workflows, investigated bottlenecks, and distinct personas — developers, administrators, operators, end users. The engagement model is deliberately balanced across self-service, setup, consulting, community, and co-creation.
* Before any of that, we confirm a platform is needed at all. Sometimes the base platform already suffices, sometimes enablement and skills development beat another abstraction layer, and sometimes a Thinnest Viable Platform wins over the grand custom build.

**What it costs**

* Keeping the engagement portfolio in balance is deliberate, recurring work — unmanaged consulting quietly turns the platform team into a professional-services shop with a roadmap it never touches.
* Earning voluntary adoption is slower than mandating it: marketing, internal events, and feedback channels all need owners and ongoing effort.
* Once a year we re-ask the founding question — is this platform still the simplest, most valuable solution? — with retirement as an acceptable answer.

**What we are not doing**

* Not hiring and growing platform engineers as individuals — that lives in [[building-platform-teams]].
* Not the full product-management discipline for platforms — that is [[platform-as-a-product]]; here it appears only as the founding stance. Stakeholder tactics live in [[managing-stakeholders]].
* Not prescribing team sizes or reporting lines — this is an operating model, not an org-chart template.
