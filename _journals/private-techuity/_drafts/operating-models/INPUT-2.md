For someone running a PE-backed company, **the key change is that you are expected to run today’s business and deliver a defined improvement programme—with an owner who may be closely involved in both.** The relationship can extend well beyond periodic board meetings into strategy, performance reviews, senior hiring and major operational decisions. ([McKinsey & Company][1])

The important question is not simply, “How hands-on is the investor?” It is:

**Are the expectations, your decision-making authority, and the resources available to you actually aligned?**

I would look at the implications in seven areas.

## 1. You need to understand the investment plan—not just the annual budget

The *investment thesis* is the investor’s explanation of why it bought the company and how it expects to increase its value. The *value creation plan* translates that into business changes. Spencer Stuart’s research on investor–CEO relationships emphasizes that management and the sponsor should build this plan together and agree on the measures used to assess progress. ([Spencer Stuart][2])

For you, the central question is: **“What business are we expected to become under this ownership?”**

Consider the difference between these hypothetical mandates:

| Investment objective                       | What it asks of management                                                               |
| ------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Grow the existing business                 | Strengthen products, sales, customer retention and capacity.                             |
| Improve profitability                      | Change pricing, purchasing, productivity or the mix of customers and products.           |
| Build a group through acquisitions         | Evaluate targets while developing integration capability and a coherent group structure. |
| Make a founder-dependent business scalable | Delegate decisions, strengthen the leadership team and establish repeatable processes.   |

These are different leadership jobs. Being an excellent day-to-day operator does not automatically equip a team to integrate several acquisitions or redesign its commercial approach.

**My recommendation:** translate the mandate into a small number of initiatives, each with a baseline, outcome, accountable executive, investment requirement and timetable. Include what the investor has committed to provide—not just what management has committed to deliver.

For example, “increase sales productivity” is an ambition. “Redesign territory coverage, hire a sales-operations lead and improve qualified-opportunity conversion, with an agreed implementation budget” is an executable proposal.

## 2. Make your authority explicit before a disagreement tests it

PE boards can be actively involved between formal meetings, and that involvement can support faster decisions. McKinsey’s interviews with portfolio-company CEOs also illustrate that this is a meaningful adjustment for leaders accustomed to more distant boards. ([McKinsey & Company][3])

The practical issue is distinguishing **advice, approval and instruction**.

I would agree, in writing, which decisions management can make, which need consultation, and which require formal approval. Cover senior appointments, spending outside budget, pricing changes, acquisitions, restructuring and major technology projects. Check those arrangements against the company’s actual governance documents.

An operating partner also needs a clear mandate. Are they advising you, sponsoring an initiative, supplying specialist expertise, or directly leading execution? PE firms can offer functional experts, experienced advisers and networks of other portfolio-company executives, but those resources need to be integrated deliberately into how you run the company. ([McKinsey & Company][1])

A useful working rule would be:

> “We welcome direct collaboration with the operating team, but changes to company priorities or commitments need to be agreed with the accountable executive.”

That is not a ban on investor access. It prevents your functional leaders from receiving incompatible instructions.

**Accountability without the authority to make decisions is an arrangement to fix—not a leadership challenge to absorb indefinitely.**

## 3. Design the company’s operating model around the strategy

This is the part that goes beyond managing the investor relationship.

Your company’s operating model determines where decisions sit, which activities are centralized, how departments work together, and what systems and capabilities support them.

I would start with the changes needed to deliver the investment plan, rather than begin with an organization chart.

### Example: an acquisition-led group

Suppose the plan is to buy several businesses. You need to decide what kind of group you are building.

**A decentralized group** might retain local leadership, brands and customer relationships while introducing consistent financial reporting and a small number of group controls.

**An integrated business** might combine purchasing, finance, technology, sales coverage or service delivery.

Neither is automatically the right answer. The design question is: **which specific benefits require integration, and which advantages depend on local autonomy?**

For each proposed shared function, I would ask what problem it solves, what it costs to establish, who owns service quality, and what authority remains with the business-unit leader.

For example, holding local managers accountable for profit while centrally controlling their pricing, staffing and purchasing creates a mismatch unless the performance measures reflect those constraints.

The same logic applies outside acquisitions: a sales-led growth plan may need stronger commercial leadership and sales operations; a productivity plan may need better scheduling, process ownership and operational data.

**More reporting to the investor is not a substitute for making the business work better.**

## 4. Create one performance-management system—not separate “board” and “business” versions

Agreeing on reporting requirements matters because it lets management focus on information needed for decisions rather than continually producing additional reports. That is one of Spencer Stuart’s specific recommendations for the CEO–sponsor relationship. ([Spencer Stuart][2])

For a reasonably stable company, I would use the following as a starting design—not as a universal PE standard:

**Weekly management review:** near-term sales, customer or delivery problems, cash exceptions and progress on the few most important initiatives. Finish with decisions, owners and dates.

**Monthly business review:** financial results, performance against the improvement plan, an updated outlook, and choices requiring investor or board input.

**Periodic strategy review:** revisit the assumptions behind the plan, capital allocation, organizational capability and acquisition priorities. Keep this distinct from explaining last month’s results.

The crucial distinction is between:

* **Budget:** the approved financial plan.
* **Forecast:** the current evidence-based expectation.
* **Stretch target:** an ambition that needs additional actions or favourable conditions.

I would insist on keeping those separate. Otherwise, the forecast can become a statement of loyalty to the budget rather than a useful estimate.

For each important financial measure, add an operational measure that helps explain it. For instance, revenue alongside conversion and retention; margin alongside productivity and service quality; cash alongside overdue receivables and inventory.

The purpose is to know **what to change**, not merely whether a number is red or green.

## 5. Treat cash and execution capacity as constraints on the plan

### Understand the cash position personally

Where the company has acquisition debt, you need to understand repayment obligations, interest costs, liquidity and loan conditions—not leave all of that inside the finance function. McKinsey’s guidance for PE-company CFOs places particular emphasis on understanding cash flow, the balance sheet and debt covenants. ([McKinsey & Company][4])

EBITDA—earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization—is not cash available to spend. A hypothetical company could report €10 million of EBITDA while also needing cash for interest, tax, equipment, inventory and overdue customer payments.

I would ask the CFO to make three things easy for the management team to understand: the expected cash position, the downside scenario, and the triggers that require action. For a cash-constrained business, a rolling short-term cash forecast can be more immediately useful than another earnings presentation.

### Give the improvement programme actual capacity

Do not assume the existing team can run the business, integrate acquisitions, replace core systems and redesign pricing simultaneously.

McKinsey’s June 2026 analysis identifies this execution burden directly and describes dedicated transformation leadership inside portfolio companies as one response. That is an option, not a reason every company needs another executive. ([McKinsey & Company][5])

My test for every major initiative would be:

**Who has the time to deliver it, and what will they stop doing?**

A smaller company might need a strong project lead and temporary specialist support. A larger transformation may need a dedicated executive. Either way, someone must own implementation—not just the presentation about it.

## 6. Challenge the investor with evidence, while keeping problems visible

You should not interpret active ownership as a requirement to agree with every suggestion. McKinsey’s CEO guidance explicitly stresses the importance of having a defensible point of view and debating it with logic and numbers. ([McKinsey & Company][1])

My recommended approach is to bring the objective, the trade-off and an alternative.

For example, rather than:

> “We cannot reduce service costs.”

Present a hypothetical decision like this:

> “The proposed staffing reduction would save €500,000 annually, but our current workload analysis indicates a service backlog. I recommend changing scheduling and reducing contractor spend first, then reassessing staffing against agreed service thresholds.”

That is not resistance to improving performance. It is a different route to the objective, with consequences made visible.

Use the same discipline for bad news. State what happened, the estimated impact, what remains uncertain, what you are doing and what decision you need. Do not wait for perfect certainty before flagging a potentially material issue.

Inside the business, avoid making “the PE firm wants it” the main explanation for change. Explain the business rationale honestly, acknowledge the trade-offs and take responsibility for decisions you support. Where you disagree, resolve that through the appropriate governance process rather than leaving employees between competing authorities.

## 7. Build a business that remains strong after this owner exits

An exit-oriented plan does not have to mean managing only for the next quarter. McKinsey’s work on PE boards describes a willingness to consider longer-term value, while its 2026 analysis emphasizes testing whether claims about a company’s future can be supported with evidence. ([McKinsey & Company][3])

For management, I would translate that into a simple standard: **do not achieve the target by creating a problem the next owner will have to repair.**

That means watching customer retention alongside pricing gains, service quality alongside cost reductions, maintenance alongside capital-spending restraint, and leadership depth alongside headcount efficiency.

It also means making the company less dependent on individual heroics. Reliable data, repeatable processes and a capable second layer of management should be part of the improvement agenda.

For your own position, assess the working relationship as carefully as the potential financial upside. Spencer Stuart’s survey of 1,015 senior leaders found that culture and the sponsor’s approach to partnering with management were prominent considerations when evaluating PE opportunities. This is evidence about respondents’ priorities, not proof that any particular sponsor is a good partner. ([Spencer Stuart][6])

## What I would put in place first

Whether you are newly appointed or resetting an existing relationship, I would start with **three agreed documents**: a concise value creation plan, a decision-authority map, and a common performance dashboard.

Together, they should answer:

**What are we trying to achieve? Who decides? What resources are committed? How will we know whether it is working? What happens when the assumptions change?**

My central advice is to aim for a model in which **the investor strengthens your ability to run the company rather than becoming a parallel management team—and you give the investor reliable visibility without turning the business into a reporting operation.**
