**Private equity operating models describe how a PE firm works with the businesses it owns: who makes decisions, who executes improvements, and what expertise the investor supplies.** The biggest distinction is how hands-on the investor is—from steering through the board to deploying dedicated operating teams. There is no single standard model. ([mckinsey.com][1])

I’ll focus mainly on established-company buyouts, rather than venture capital or minority growth investments.

## 1. There are two layers to consider

**The PE firm’s own operating model.** This covers how it sources and evaluates investments, raises capital, manages funds, reports to investors, and handles finance, compliance, technology and data. A major design choice is what to keep in-house versus outsource—for example, using an external fund administrator while retaining investment decisions internally. ([McKinsey & Company][2])

**Its portfolio-company operating model.** This is how the firm helps the companies it owns become more valuable. It includes governance, management support, specialist expertise and responsibility for delivering improvements. Some firms use internal operating teams; others rely on external executives and advisers, or a combination. ([mckinsey.com][1])

These are related but different: running an efficient investment-management business does not automatically make you good at improving the companies you buy.

## 2. The main portfolio operating models

A useful comparison is the following. These are overlapping archetypes, not rigid industry categories; the trade-offs are a practical assessment.

| Model                          | How it works                                                                                        | Main trade-off                                                                        |
| ------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Board-led / management-led** | The investor sets expectations and oversees performance; company management executes.               | Preserves autonomy, but depends heavily on management capability.                     |
| **Operating-partner advisory** | Experienced executives coach management, challenge strategy and support selected initiatives.       | Adds judgment and experience, but may add limited implementation capacity.            |
| **Central specialist team**    | Shared experts support functions such as pricing, procurement, technology and talent.               | Makes expertise available across companies, but can create competing priorities.      |
| **Embedded execution**         | Operators work directly inside a business, sometimes in interim leadership or transformation roles. | Adds delivery capacity, but requires particularly clear authority and accountability. |

The underlying distinction between advisory support, internal specialists and direct implementation is reflected in McKinsey’s research on operating groups. Firms can combine these approaches rather than choose just one. ([mckinsey.com][1])

There is also a separate question: **how standardized should the approach be across companies?** Vista, for example, describes a software-focused model that shares operating methods, specialist resources and learning across its portfolio. Its current approach also includes in-house AI engineers building alongside portfolio-company teams. That illustrates a repeatable, sector-specific model—not proof that standardization is always better. ([Vista Equity Partners][3])

## 3. What these models actually do

The work is broader than cost reduction. It can involve improving sales, developing products, changing pricing, strengthening management, increasing efficiency, or acquiring and integrating other businesses. The objective is to improve the company’s performance and ultimately its value. ([British Business Bank][4])

A practical way to understand the work is across the ownership cycle:

**Before acquisition: test whether the investment plan is achievable.**
The investor should establish not just whether a company is attractive, but what can realistically be improved, what that will cost, and whether the necessary capabilities exist. Bain’s 2026 report emphasizes combining commercial, operational and technology analysis before buying, rather than postponing the improvement plan until after closing. ([Bain][5])

**Immediately after acquisition: turn ambitions into an executable plan.**
My recommended starting point would be a small number of priorities, each with a named owner, budget, baseline, target and deadline. “Improve sales effectiveness” is too vague; “increase conversion in the enterprise segment through revised qualification and sales coaching” is something a team can act on.

**During ownership: deliver changes and build company capability.**
For example, a central pricing specialist might help redesign discounting, while the company’s commercial director owns implementation. The operating-model question is not merely *who has the expertise*, but *who has the time, authority and accountability to use it*.

**Before exit: demonstrate that improvements will last.**
The business needs to be attractive to its next owner, not just produce an attractive presentation. Exit preparation is therefore connected to the strategy from the outset, rather than being a completely separate final-stage exercise. ([British Business Bank][4])

## 4. The most important design issue is decision-making authority

My view is that **a clear division of responsibilities matters more than the number of operating partners on the payroll**.

A sensible starting design would give the board responsibility for strategic oversight and major approvals, management responsibility for running the business, and the operating team a clearly defined support or execution mandate. The details should be explicit: who can approve an initiative, commit resources, change priorities and resolve disagreements?

Consider a pricing project. There are several different responsibilities:

* The operating team can provide analysis and design options.
* The commercial director can own rollout and customer communication.
* The CEO can resolve trade-offs between growth, retention and margin.

Trouble arises when the operating team effectively directs the business but management remains accountable for decisions it does not control. The opposite problem is an advisory team that makes recommendations without anyone having the capacity to implement them.

I would also make **who pays for the support** explicit. Costs may sit with the manager, the fund or portfolio companies, depending on the arrangements. This is not just an administrative detail: the SEC identifies expense allocation and payments for portfolio-company services as potential sources of conflicts of interest. ([Investor][6])

## 5. How operational improvement translates into investment returns

Here is a simplified, hypothetical example using an EBITDA valuation. EBITDA means earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization.

|                    | At acquisition |      At exit |
| ------------------ | -------------: | -----------: |
| Annual EBITDA      |    €20 million |  €30 million |
| Valuation multiple |            10× |          10× |
| Enterprise value   |   €200 million | €300 million |
| Net debt           |   €120 million | €100 million |
| Equity value       |    €80 million | €200 million |

In this example, equity value rises from €80 million to €200 million—a **2.5× gross multiple**, ignoring fees, taxes, interim distributions and any further equity investment.

The arithmetic separates two effects: €100 million of additional enterprise value from higher earnings at an unchanged valuation multiple, and €20 million from lower net debt. It assumes the company generates sufficient cash to achieve that debt reduction; EBITDA itself is not cash available for repayment.

That distinction matters because a good return is not, by itself, evidence of a good operating model. Returns can also benefit from a higher exit valuation multiple. Bain’s analysis explicitly separates the contribution of operating growth from leverage and valuation changes. ([Bain][5])

## 6. How I would judge whether a model is good

I would assess it against three tests.

**Does it fit the investment strategy?**
A business needing stronger management requires different support from one needing factory improvements or repeated acquisition integration. Build the operating capability around the problems the firm expects to solve—not around a fashionable organizational structure.

**Does it add delivery capacity?**
Ask what the team actually changes. Better reporting can improve visibility, but it is not the same as improving customer retention, product quality, productivity or cash generation.

**Does it leave a stronger company behind?**
I would look beyond short-term earnings to customer outcomes, employee capability, necessary investment and whether the company can sustain performance without permanent sponsor intervention. Apparent savings are less valuable when they create larger future problems.

This focus is particularly relevant in the environment described by Bain’s **2026 Global Private Equity Report**, which argues that investors can rely less on cheap financing and easy valuation gains, and need stronger, more repeatable operating improvements. That is an industry assessment—not a guarantee that hands-on ownership produces superior results. ([Bain][7])

**The central takeaway: the best operating model is not necessarily the most hands-on. It is the one that matches the investment plan, gives people clear authority, and supplies capabilities the company genuinely needs.**
