## 2. Audience and purpose

Write primarily for experienced product and engineering leaders who understand technology and organizational leadership but may be unfamiliar with private equity.

The book should help them understand:

- How private equity works, what investors are trying to achieve, and how those objectives affect portfolio companies.
- How to navigate ownership, governance, incentives, and the relationship between investors and company leadership.
- How product and technology capabilities can create measurable, durable business value.
- What a Technology Principal actually does across the investment and ownership lifecycle.
- When private equity can support a mutually beneficial relationship—and when incentives or operating choices undermine that possibility.

The book should neither promote private equity uncritically nor assume it is inherently destructive. Explain mechanisms, trade-offs, conditions for success, and failure modes.

## 3. Develop a coherent book structure

Propose an architecture that works both as a book with a clear narrative progression and as a collection of articles that can be read independently.

Use the following themes as a starting point, not a fixed table of contents. Add important missing topics and reorganize where that produces a stronger book.

### Part I: Understanding private equity

Explain the fundamentals: funds, investors, general and limited partners, fund lifecycles, investment theses, ownership structures, governance, leverage, fees, carried interest, and exits.

Explain how returns are generated and distinguish operational improvement from financial engineering and changes in valuation multiples. Introduce relevant financial concepts through accessible, clearly labeled examples.

Explore company development stages and circumstances in which different forms of investment may fit. Distinguish venture capital, growth equity, buyouts, carve-outs, turnarounds, and other relevant models without implying that every company follows the same path.

### Part II: The investor–company relationship

Explore relationships among investment teams, operating teams, boards, executives, and product and engineering leaders.

Cover decision rights, incentives, management equity, reporting, investment horizons, leadership changes, and the tension between near-term performance and long-term capability.

Identify patterns and anti-patterns: productive challenge versus micromanagement, disciplined execution versus indiscriminate cost-cutting, useful standardization versus forced uniformity, and realistic value-creation plans versus unsupported ambitions.

Explain how leaders can assess investor fit, recognize warning signs, and ask better questions before and during an engagement.

### Part III: Creating value through product and technology

Explain how technology investments and operating changes translate into business outcomes, rather than treating modernization as an end in itself.

Cover relevant areas such as product strategy, engineering effectiveness, technical debt, architecture, infrastructure costs, cybersecurity, data and AI, talent, operating models, acquisitions, integration, and carve-outs.

Connect interventions to explicit value mechanisms: revenue growth, retention, margin improvement, cash generation, risk reduction, resilience, or strategic flexibility.

Address sequencing, dependencies, measurement, and attribution. Distinguish durable improvements from changes that improve reported results while weakening future performance.

### Part IV: The Technology Principal’s work

Use the supplied job description to ground this section. Distinguish responsibilities specific to that role from practices that may apply more broadly across firms.

Explore the role across the investment lifecycle: sourcing support, technical due diligence, investment thesis development, value-creation planning, the first 100 days, ongoing execution, portfolio collaboration, and exit preparation.

Explain how a Technology Principal works with investment professionals, operating partners, CEOs, CFOs, CTOs, and product leaders. Address influence without direct authority, credibility with company teams, and the balance between hands-on support and oversight.

Include practical tools where useful: diligence questions, assessment frameworks, value-creation plans, decision records, and outcome-focused scorecards.

### Part V: Private equity and technology capabilities as products

Develop and critically examine the Productscapes concept.

Explore who the customers and users are, what needs are served, what capabilities constitute the offering, how value is delivered and measured, and how feedback improves the offering over time.

Distinguish the investment firm, its portfolio-support capabilities, and the portfolio companies themselves. Explain where product thinking clarifies the model and where the analogy becomes misleading.

Make this a distinctive contribution of the book, grounded in both my material and external evidence.

### Part VI: Durable value, success, and failure

Investigate the conditions under which private equity can create sustainable benefits for investors and portfolio companies while considering consequences for employees, customers, and other stakeholders.

Do not assume these interests always align. Examine trade-offs, time horizons, debt burdens, reinvestment, organizational health, and outcomes after exit.

Integrate successful engagements, mixed outcomes, and cautionary tales throughout the book. Consider a dedicated comparative case-study section where it adds value.

## 4. Research standards

Conduct thorough, multi-pass research across relevant, high-quality sources. Seek broad coverage, but do not claim to have reviewed every possible source.

Prioritize primary evidence where available: filings, financial statements, transaction documents, regulatory records, original research, and direct accounts. Supplement these with credible journalism, academic work, and well-supported practitioner analysis.

Treat investor presentations, consultancy reports, and company announcements as interested perspectives. Triangulate important claims and actively seek contradictory evidence.

Support substantive factual claims, statistics, and case-study details with traceable citations close to the relevant text. Maintain a bibliography with titles, authors or organizations, publication dates, and links or identifiers. Cite only sources actually consulted.

Clearly distinguish established facts, reported claims, contested interpretations, your analysis, my hypotheses, and illustrative examples. Never invent references, quotations, financial results, or causal explanations.

For time-sensitive claims, state the relevant date. For performance assessments, distinguish realized outcomes from interim results or unrealized valuations. Identify material evidence gaps explicitly.

## 5. Case-study expectations

Select cases for explanatory value and evidence quality, not just name recognition. Include variation in investment strategy, company maturity, industry, and outcome where the evidence supports it.

For each substantial case, investigate the initial situation, investment thesis, ownership and financing context, major interventions, product and technology decisions, outcomes, and stakeholder consequences.

Explain what the evidence supports about causation—and what it does not. Consider alternative explanations such as market conditions, acquisition effects, or valuation changes.

Avoid treating investor returns alone as proof of company success, or a disappointing exit alone as proof that every operational intervention failed.

Extract transferable lessons and explain the conditions under which those lessons may or may not apply. Label any fictional or composite example explicitly; never present it as a real case.

## 6. Writing and editorial approach

Use clear, precise, accessible prose for an intelligent technical audience. Explain financial terminology on first use. Avoid promotional language, generic business advice, unexplained jargon, and simplistic narratives.

Combine conceptual explanation with concrete examples, practical questions, and implications for product and engineering leaders.

Make each article answer a meaningful question and contribute to the book’s broader argument. Use cross-references and a consistent glossary rather than repeatedly explaining the same concepts.

Use tables, diagrams, checklists, and worked examples when they improve understanding—not as substitutes for analysis. Avoid forcing every chapter into an identical template.
