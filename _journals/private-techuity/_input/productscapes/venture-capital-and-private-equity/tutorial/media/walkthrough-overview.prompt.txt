Use case: scientific-educational
Asset type: one illustration in a plain-language product-domain tutorial for adults.
Domain: Venture capital and private equity, from the beginning
Reader: A curious reader coming from a different field. You do not need a finance background or experience running a company.
Scope: An introduction to investing in businesses through venture, growth, and buyout funds, with software companies as everyday examples. It explains common arrangements rather than any particular investment firm's terms. Fund structures, investor access, and ownership rights vary by country and agreement. The people, businesses, and financial examples are fictional.
Subject: Maya's software business: raising money or selling ownership
Setup: Maya and her company, ShiftPlan, are fictional. Follow a young-company investment, then compare it with an alternative buyout situation. The euro amounts teach the mechanics; they are not market prices or return forecasts. The ownership arithmetic assumes all shares have the same rights, and nobody else holds shares or rights to obtain them.
Step 1: Investors choose a fund and its purpose. Before investing in ShiftPlan, a manager raises a fund with an agreed strategy: what kinds of companies it may back and how it will operate. Investors examine the team, terms, and track record before committing money. Why: The manager is investing other people's money within an agreement. A venture strategy and a buyout strategy may belong in different funds. Possible complication: None specified.
Step 2: Maya and the investor examine each other. Maya explains what the extra money would help achieve. The venture investor checks customers, finances, technology, and the team. This investigation is called due diligence. Maya also asks other founders what the investor is like when a company struggles. Why: Both sides are choosing a relationship that may last years. Possible complication: A promising sales forecast may depend heavily on one customer renewing. The parties need to understand that dependency before treating the forecast as dependable.
Step 3: Agree on the price and rights. They agree to value the existing ownership at €8 million before the investment. The fund will put €2 million into newly issued shares. In this simplified case, the ownership value immediately after investment is €10 million: the fund owns 20% and Maya owns 80%. Why: Maya now owns a smaller percentage of a business with more resources. Separately, the agreement determines board seats and approval rights. A board oversees major company decisions. Possible complication: None specified.
Step 4: Complete the investment and follow the cash. Required approvals and documents are completed, and the fund arranges its cash, calling investor commitments as needed. The €2 million goes into ShiftPlan's bank account because this is a new-share investment. Maya receives no personal sale proceeds in this version. Why: Fundraising by a fund, investment into a company, and payment to a founder are separate transactions. Possible complication: Compare an alternative mature-business case: a buyout fund pays Maya €8 million for 80% of her existing shares. That payment goes to Maya, and ShiftPlan receives none of it. Extra company funding would need a separate arrangement.
Step 5: Work on the business and face setbacks. ShiftPlan hires carefully and tests a new sales approach. Maya's team still runs the company; investors may help with recruitment, introductions, and major decisions. Management reports progress and cash needs. Why: The investment agreement starts the ownership period; it does not deliver business improvement by itself. Possible complication: Sales take longer than expected. The team revises spending and milestones. The fund considers whether further investment is justified; another round of funding is not automatic.
Step 6: Sell an investment and return proceeds. Imagine a successful later sale of all ShiftPlan's shares for €15 million. If the fund still owns 20%, rights are equal, and no other claims or costs intervene, its share is €3 million. Against its €2 million investment, that is a €1 million gain before costs. The fund then handles proceeds under its agreement, including any distributions. Why: The company sale price, the fund's proceeds, its gain, and the investors' final receipts are different amounts. Possible complication: The sale could happen later, at a lower price, or never. Later funding could also change the ownership percentages or payment rights. If the business fails with nothing left for shareholders, the equity investment can be lost.
Outcome: The venture case gave ShiftPlan resources to grow; the buyout comparison gave an existing owner a way to sell. The investor's result depends on the price paid, business performance, ownership terms, costs, and eventual proceeds. A signed deal is only the beginning.

Teaching scene:
Create a sparse, spacious story in six panels: two rows of three, read left to right. Each panel has ONE simple scene, at most three people, and at most two relevant objects. Preserve the same Maya throughout: an adult woman with medium-brown skin, shoulder-length dark curly hair, a teal jacket, and dark trousers. The investment manager is a different person, in blue.
Panel 1: investors and a manager sit together with one plain folder, agreeing on their fund’s purpose. Heading: "1 Choose a fund".
Panel 2: Maya and the manager face one another, each holding one plain folder and asking questions. Heading: "2 Check each other".
Panel 3: Maya and the manager sign one agreement at a small desk. Heading: "3 Agree the terms".
Panel 4: a simple container labeled "Fund" sends a single arrow with banknotes into a company office labeled "ShiftPlan". Maya stands outside the arrow. Heading: "4 Invest in the company".
Panel 5: Maya and two colleagues adjust their plan at a desk, with a calendar and a simple blank checklist. Heading: "5 Work and adapt". One short caption: "Sales take longer".
Panel 6: Maya, the manager, and a potential buyer discuss a possible sale at a desk with a clock. Heading: "6 Possible sale". One short caption: "A sale is not guaranteed".
CRITICAL TEXT LIMIT: The headings, Fund, ShiftPlan, Sales take longer, and A sale is not guaranteed are the ONLY text allowed anywhere. Do not write on the folders, documents, calendar, checklist, screens, background, or clothing. Use a few plain strokes on paper without letters. No thought bubbles, speech bubbles, charts, dashboards, tables, logos, industry icons, extra symbols, statistics, prices, percentages, acronyms, explanations, or financial vocabulary. The surrounding prose explains every detail; this image only helps readers remember the six stages. Use large headings, broad empty margins, and simple recognizable actions. Keep the atmosphere professional and suitable for adults, with restrained flat color. Do not blend the alternative buyout case into this venture story.

Style and composition:
- Aspect ratio 16:9, generous margins, bright warm-white background.
- A polished adult educational illustration: flat editorial drawing, crisp navy outlines,
  restrained teal, blue, and warm amber accents, subtle texture, very little shading.
- Make the concept understandable at the width of a reading column and on a phone.
- Use a small number of recognizable objects and people. Keep relationships spatially clear.
- Use an inclusive cast without occupational or cultural stereotypes.
- Short everyday labels are welcome where they teach the distinction. Use only labels
  explicitly requested in the teaching scene; otherwise keep the image free of text.
- Arrows must have a clear meaning and direction; do not connect unrelated comparison
  panels as though they are required stages. Do not imply certainty, causality, proportions,
  cash movements, or guaranteed outcomes that the explanation does not support.
- Keep all important content inside the frame. No headline, watermark, brand logo,
  decorative charts, invented statistics, jargon, tiny writing, photorealism, or 3D rendering.
- Explain the idea visually. The surrounding tutorial provides its full definition and caveats.
