Yes. If you treat **PE Technical Capability as a product rather than a staff function**, it becomes much more powerful.

I would define the product as:

# **Technology Value Creation Platform**

> A repeatable system that helps the fund make better technology investment decisions and helps portfolio companies turn technology into measurable enterprise value.

The key shift is from:

**“We have some smart technical people who help when needed.”**

to:

**“Vortex has a technology operating system that is used from investment thesis through exit.”**

## 1. Start with the customers

Your "product" has several customer groups, with different needs.

| Customer             | Their problem                                           | What your product gives them                     |
| -------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ |
| Investment Partner   | "Should we buy this business?"                          | Technology investment thesis                     |
| Deal Team            | "What are the hidden risks/opportunities?"              | Fast, consistent technical DD                    |
| Investment Committee | "How does technology affect returns?"                   | Decision-ready technology view                   |
| Portfolio CEO        | "Where should technology help the business?"            | Value-creation priorities                        |
| CTO/CPTO             | "How do I execute this?"                                | Coach, benchmarks, expertise, playbooks          |
| Board                | "Are we progressing?"                                   | Outcome-based technology scorecard               |
| Exit team            | "Will buyers like what they see?"                       | Technology exit readiness                        |
| LPs                  | "Does Vortex have differentiated operating capability?" | Evidence of repeatable technology value creation |

That last one becomes increasingly relevant as Vortex grows toward another fund.

Your capability itself becomes part of **the fund's proposition to investors**.

# 2. Build around the investment lifecycle

I'd design one continuous product journey:

### **Discover → Diagnose → Decide → Accelerate → Scale → Exit**

Rather than separate tools for separate teams.

### Discover

Before there is even a deal.

Questions:

**Which markets are being transformed by technology?**

**Where is AI creating or destroying moats?**

**What technology characteristics make a company attractive?**

Output:

**Technology Market Lens**

For example:

> Vertical SaaS category: attractive recurring revenue, but AI may materially reduce the value of workflow-only products without proprietary data.

Now you're contributing to **deal sourcing**, not waiting for the data room.

---

# 3. Diagnose: Technical DD becomes a product

Instead of every DD being an artisanal consulting exercise, build a repeatable **Technology Investment Assessment**.

Not a 100-page document.

I'd structure it around perhaps seven dimensions:

| Dimension             | Core question                                      |
| --------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| Product               | Does the product create defensible customer value? |
| Architecture          | Can it support the investment thesis?              |
| Engineering           | Can the organization deliver fast enough?          |
| Data & AI             | Asset, opportunity, threat or liability?           |
| Security & resilience | What could materially damage value?                |
| Organization          | Do we have the right CTO/team?                     |
| Economics             | What technology investment is actually required?   |

Then score each dimension against the **investment thesis**, not some generic technical standard.

A 15-year-old monolith may be perfectly acceptable.

A beautifully modern microservice architecture might be financially disastrous.

The question is:

> **Does technology enable or constrain the value-creation plan?**

---

# 4. The flagship output: Technology Investment Thesis

This could become one of your most important products.

Every investment gets a one-page **Technology Investment Thesis**.

Something like:

### Company X

**Technology advantage**

Highly configurable platform with strong domain-specific workflows.

**Main constraint**

Customer onboarding requires too much engineering involvement.

**Value-creation opportunity**

Productize configuration and integrations.

**Leadership**

Strong CTO; weak product-management capability.

**AI opportunity**

Automated workflow classification could materially increase product differentiation.

**Required investment**

€1.2m over 24 months.

**Expected business outcome**

Onboarding time ↓60%
implementation margin ↑
international scalability ↑

**Exit story**

Scalable international vertical SaaS platform.

Now the investment team can actually reason about technology economically.

# 5. Convert DD directly into the first 100 days

This is where most PE technical capabilities break.

They do the DD.

Then the PDF goes into SharePoint.

Your product should automatically create:

### **Technology 100-Day Plan**

Not 35 remediation items.

Three to five important moves.

For example:

**1. Strengthen product leadership**

**2. Stabilize enterprise onboarding**

**3. Establish engineering productivity baseline**

**4. Define AI product roadmap**

Everything else goes into the backlog.

This creates continuity:

**DD → ownership → value creation**

rather than:

**DD → transaction complete → start over.**

# 6. Build a Portfolio CPTO product

This is your **coach/co-pilot layer**.

I would make the core offering to each portfolio CTO something like:

### **CTO Partnership**

Not mandatory consulting.

A trusted operating relationship.

It could have a regular monthly cadence around:

**What's changed?**

**What's the hardest decision you're facing?**

**Which value-creation initiative is stuck?**

**What do you need from Vortex?**

The Principal brings:

pattern recognition, external experts, recruiting help, benchmarks, challenge and board context.

That's where the role becomes much more than governance.

# 7. Build reusable "accelerators"

Your product becomes scalable when you stop solving identical problems ten times.

I would gradually create a library like:

| Accelerator                  | Purpose                                     |
| ---------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| AI Opportunity Assessment    | Product + productivity + disruption         |
| Engineering Effectiveness    | Delivery, quality, organization             |
| CTO Assessment               | Leadership capability and gaps              |
| Nearshore Builder            | Location, model, economics, operating model |
| Cloud Economics              | FinOps and infrastructure efficiency        |
| Product/Technology Alignment | Roadmap vs business strategy                |
| M&A Tech Integration         | Platform/team/data integration              |
| Cyber Readiness              | Material risk mitigation                    |
| Exit Readiness               | Prepare for buyer DD                        |
| CTO Hiring                   | Role design, assessment, network            |

But don't create 40-page playbooks.

Each should be a **minimum viable accelerator**:

**diagnostic + benchmark + decision framework + recommended actions.**

# 8. Build a portfolio intelligence layer

This is where it becomes a real product.

You want to accumulate knowledge across companies.

Suppose ten companies use GitHub, AWS and Jira.

Over time, you can understand patterns around:

**cloud cost / revenue**

**engineering headcount / ARR**

**release frequency**

**support burden**

**AI adoption**

**developer productivity**

**customer onboarding effort**

**nearshore effectiveness**

Not to create simplistic league tables.

But to answer questions like:

> "Is 18% of revenue on engineering unusually high?"

or:

> "Are we actually getting productivity benefits from AI coding tools?"

or:

> "Which organizational models work best in €10–30m SaaS businesses?"

Eventually you develop something very powerful:

### **Vortex Portfolio Technology Intelligence**

That creates proprietary pattern recognition.

And *that* can become a competitive advantage.

# 10. Exit readiness should be built in from Day 1

A really mature PE technology product asks at acquisition:

> **What technology story do we want the next buyer to see in four years?**

Then you work backwards.

At exit, you want to demonstrate:

**strong leadership**

**predictable engineering**

**scalable architecture**

**healthy product roadmap**

**controlled technical debt**

**strong cybersecurity**

**AI strategy**

**good unit economics**

**low key-person risk**

**clean IP**

**documented M&A integration**

You aren't just preparing a DD room.

You're creating an **investable technology story**.

# It needs product metrics too

If you're treating the capability as a product, don't measure yourself by:

**number of DD reports**

or:

**number of portfolio meetings.**

Those are activity metrics.

I'd measure three levels.

### Adoption

Do people voluntarily use the capability?

**% of deals where technology is involved before DD**

**% of CTOs actively engaging**

**number of proactive CEO/CTO requests**

### Outcome

Did something actually improve?

**cloud cost reduction**

**onboarding improvement**

**engineering productivity**

**leadership upgrades**

**M&A integration speed**

**AI products launched**

### Enterprise value

Harder, but ultimately what matters.

**revenue enabled**

**EBITDA created**

**risk avoided**

**acquisition integration value**

**multiple/exit-story improvement**

You probably won't be able to calculate perfect causality.

But the mindset matters.

# The most important product principle

I'd use:

> **Standardize the questions, not the answers.**

You don't want:

**"Every company must use this architecture."**

You want:

**"Every company should be able to answer these important questions."**

That avoids central IT bureaucracy.

Vortex should create **better decisions**, not standard technology stacks.
