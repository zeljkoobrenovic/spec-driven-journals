A company can improve and still disappoint its investors; an investor can make money while the company gets weaker. Neither is a paradox once operating performance is seen as only one of four drivers — alongside purchase price, borrowing and the moment of sale.

Two words need pinning down. An **investment return** is what an investor gets back against what they put in, and how long it took — it says nothing about revenue, product quality or customers. And **"improve"** means either that *earnings* rose (what the price is calculated from) or that the *company* got better at serving customers. The two can come apart: deferring maintenance raises earnings and weakens the business. Below, "improves" means earnings unless stated.

**One fictional buyout.** A company earns €10 million a year. A fund buys it at 10× EBITDA — €100 million — paying €40 million of its own money and borrowing €60 million, which the company now owes. Five years on EBITDA reaches €15 million, €20 million of debt is repaid, and the fund sells at the same multiple. The business is worth €150 million; after lenders take €40 million, the fund receives €110 million.

**Earnings grew 50%. The fund's money grew 175%.** Of the €70 million gain, €50 million came from higher earnings and €20 million from shrinking debt. That is leverage, and it works as hard in reverse: had the business been worth €80 million, lenders would still take €60 million, leaving €20 million on €40 million — a fifth off the business, half off the investor.

**MOIC** is what came out over what went in (2.75×). **IRR** is the steady annual rate producing that over five years (22.4%). MOIC says how much, IRR how fast; neither says whether the company is better.

When the multiple moves as well as earnings, part of the gain belongs cleanly to neither, and splitting it is a convention rather than a measurement. **The uncomfortable test:** hold performance fixed and change only the exit multiple. At 7× the fund makes 1.6×; at 10×, 2.8×; at 12×, 3.5×. Same team, same customers, same earnings growth — a return that more than doubles on market conditions. Timing does the same: €40 million into €80 million is 2× whether it takes three years (about 26% a year) or seven (about 10%).

At fund level, three ratios separate cash received from value estimated. An investor who paid in €100, received €60 and holds €90 of unsold value has **DPI** 0.6×, **RVPI** 0.9×, **TVPI** 1.5×. Only DPI is money. Strong TVPI with weak DPI says: we believe we have done well, and have not proved it yet.

"Our platform work created €30 million of enterprise value" is the claim to avoid: it must survive an entry price, a debt structure and a market multiple engineering never touched. A defensible claim starts lower — what changed, for which customers, at what cost, with what effect. Better still: which improvements survive a falling multiple and an eight-year hold? Those improve the company, not just the year's earnings.
