Business improvement and investment return are related but different. Investors' results depend on the purchase price, financing, operating outcomes, sale valuation, and timing; technology controls only part of that chain.

The full article's fictional buyout starts with €100 million of enterprise value and €60 million of net debt, leaving €40 million of equity. Five years later, enterprise value is €150 million and net debt €40 million, leaving €110 million of equity. Under the example's simplifying assumptions, that is 2.75× invested capital and about 22.4% annualized internal rate of return (IRR). It is a gross investment result, not a net LP (limited partner, an investor in a fund) fund return.

**Separate the mechanisms.** Earnings growth changes the operating value at a given multiple. A different multiple changes what the market or buyer pays for earnings. Debt repayment changes the equity bridge but must itself be financed. If new equity funds that repayment, include the additional contribution in the return calculation.

A valuation decomposition also contains an interaction between earnings and multiple changes. Allocating that interaction to operations is a presentation convention, not proof of causality. A higher exit price cannot, by itself, establish the contribution of an engineering program.

At fund level, distinguish cash distributions from residual valuations. DPI concerns distributions relative to paid-in capital. RVPI concerns remaining value. TVPI combines them on a consistent basis. IRR additionally needs cash-flow dates. Always identify the period, currency, gross or net basis, and treatment of borrowing. ILPA's performance work supports this emphasis on explicit methodology. [S05: ILPA performance guidance](https://ilpa.org/industry-guidance/templates-standards-model-documents/ilpa-templates-hub/ilpa-performance-template/)

For technology leaders, the useful operating question is which improvements still make sense if the exit multiple falls or the hold lasts longer. Start contribution claims with observed customer or operational changes. Reconcile costs and alternative explanations with finance before discussing valuation implications.

This approach may produce a less dramatic value-creation claim. It produces a more credible one, and it protects teams from being judged solely through market conditions they did not control.
