**A plan can fit the budget and still be impossible to deliver.** Choose a combination of work that fits both the cash and the team’s time, then revisit the whole combination when the evidence changes.

After an investment, good ideas compete for money and for the same people’s time. At Rotaline, the book’s fictional scheduling-software company, the board allows up to €500,000 of additional cash and 24 engineer-weeks, one person’s work for one week, for new commitments in the first hundred days. Four priced requests are on the table: faster restoration after a failure (€80,000, four weeks), simplifying one customer-setup step (€180,000, twelve), building a full customer portal (€220,000, twelve) and researching whether customers need that portal (€20,000, two).

**The money fits; the time does not.** Together the requests cost exactly €500,000 but need 30 engineer-weeks against 24. A fifth item needs no cash: the one specialist who can release and recover the scheduling engine teaches a second engineer, in about eight weeks set aside before the 24 were counted.

So sort the requests by what they rest on. Restoration and the training meet **agreed obligations**: Rotaline has promised its service back within four hours of a failure, with at most fifteen minutes of updates lost, and it has decided not to depend on one person. The setup change rests on customer evidence and on the condition the investor funded: many more customers set up without the setup team growing as fast. The portal rests on an assumption, which is exactly what the research tests.

Ines, the chief executive, proposes restoration, the setup change and the research, with the training alongside, and the board approves €280,000 and 18 weeks. The full portal waits: spare money doesn’t make its twelve weeks fit into the six left. The remaining €220,000 and six weeks are a reserve only the board may draw on.

Then the plan meets reality. At day 45 the restoration test fails: eleven hours instead of four, and a night of updates lost. The €80,000 and four weeks already spent are a **sunk cost**: they can’t justify more spending, but the obligation still stands. Ines asks the board for €20,000 and two weeks from the reserve for a fix and retest, and it agrees. It rejects pausing the setup change to free people, because that work’s evidence is the condition for the next expansion step.

The day-85 retest passes, but only for one kind of failure. Losing the cloud provider’s whole region was never tested, and it stays on the record as a risk. At day 100 the board funds a customer-data check before setup, taking commitments to €340,000 and all 24 weeks. **Committed is not spent, and allocated is not finished**: some work and payments run past day 100.

Review the whole combination, not each project alone, and update one decision record after every review. Whether the setup change really pays off is the next question: [[test-revenue-assumptions]].
