**An investment announcement is not a budget.** The headline may be a valuation, an estimate of the company’s worth, or the amount that changes hands. Of the money paid for shares (units of ownership), payment for new shares goes to the company; payment for existing shares goes to the seller. Other parts may repay loans or pay deal costs; a lender may separately lend the company cash. To plan hiring, establish how much cash reaches the business, when, and who can authorize its use.

**Compare arrangements.** A fictional minority round, investors buying new shares totalling under half the company, puts €8 million (€8m) into Larkspur at closing, when the deal completes. A fund’s €40m purchase of the founder’s existing shares pays the founder. Under corporate ownership, establish which group company funds the work and who approves Larkspur’s budget.

A fund-paid **buyout**, the purchase of control of a company, can involve **four types of organization**. The **investment firm** manages the **fund**, a pool of investors’ money under agreed rules; a **holding vehicle**, a company formed to own the investment, may borrow; the **portfolio company** is the operating business. A firm’s newer fund is not automatically available to a company an older one holds; any investment needs its own justification and approvals. Borrowing elsewhere may still depend on the operating business’s cash.

A **limited partner (LP)** is a fund investor. Its **commitment** is a promise to pay when asked; a **capital call** is that request. The **general partner (GP)** holds management powers, often exercised by the investment firm as manager. A rise in an investment’s estimated value pays the LP nothing; it receives cash only through a **distribution**, a payment under the fund agreement from a sale or other cash received.

The manager is paid a **management fee** for managing the investments and may receive **carried interest**, also called carry: a share of investment profits: the gain after investment costs and the agreement’s permitted deductions. Carry differs from **management equity**, the company shares its executives own. Both shape the requests a company receives ([[fund-economics]], [[different-bets]], [[three-different-returns]]). The US Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC), the federal investing regulator, describes the conflicts. [S01: SEC private equity guide](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/investment-products/private-investment-funds/private-equity)

The fund’s investment committee approves its investments; the company’s board and executives approve its work. Shares alone let nobody direct staff or sign contracts.

**The completed record.** In the fictional round, Larkspur received €8m at closing on 3 October; €0.4m of deal costs left €7.6m. The board’s year-one plan, approved 10 October, allows four new engineering hires. Spending above €250,000 outside the plan, or more hires than it allows, needs consent from the investor director, the board member the investors appointed. Within the plan, Ines, the chief executive, authorized three customer set-up engineers on 12 October. Five hires, one above the four allowed, and an “innovation budget” no agreement contains were rejected. A revised plan, less first-quarter customer set-up work than assumed, or quarter-end cash over €300,000 below the approved forecast would reopen it.
