**An investment announcement does not tell you the company’s spending budget.** Money may go to selling shareholders, existing lenders or transaction costs. An announcement describes a transaction; to turn it into a hiring plan, establish how much cash reaches the business, when it arrives and who can authorize its use. These questions matter under every ownership arrangement; a transaction changes the conditions, because the deal structure decides how much reaches the business.

Compare the **actual arrangement**. A fictional minority round can put €8m into Larkspur; a fund’s €40m purchase of existing shares pays the seller; a corporate acquisition needs a separate agreement about group funding and local authority. The fund map below applies where that structure exists; not every investor uses a fund or a holding company.

A fund-backed buyout map separates **four types of organization**. The **investment firm** (manager, financial sponsor) employs investment professionals. A **fund** pools investors’ money under agreed terms. A **holding vehicle** holds the investment and possibly borrowing. A **portfolio company** is the operating business. When the company is later sold, the buyer pays for existing shares, either the portfolio company’s shares held by the holding vehicle or the holding vehicle itself; ask which.

A firm raising a new fund does not automatically make that money available to a company held by an older one. Borrowing can sit in several places while its payments depend on cash from the operating business.

A **limited partner (LP)** is an investor in the fund. In a common arrangement it makes a **commitment**, a promise to supply money under agreed conditions; a **capital call** requests a payment against it. The **general partner (GP)** has management responsibilities and powers under the fund arrangements, often with the investment firm acting as manager. An increase in an investment’s estimated value pays the LP nothing until it is realized.

The manager is paid a **management fee** and may receive **carried interest**, a share of investment profits paid in an order set by a **distribution waterfall**. These terms shape which requests a company receives and when; the worked arithmetic is in the optional [[fund-economics]] reference, and [[different-bets]] and [[three-different-returns]] examine the incentives and return measures. [S01: SEC private equity guide](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/investment-products/private-investment-funds/private-equity)

The fund’s investment committee approves investments; the company’s board and executives approve company work. Ownership alone does not give a shareholder authority to direct staff or sign contracts.

**The completed record.** Under the fictional €8m share issue, Larkspur received €7.6m net on 3 October; finance confirmed it from the bank statement. The board approved the year-one plan on 10 October, including four engineering hires; legal confirmed that spending outside the plan above €250,000 needs the investor director’s consent. Ines authorized Alex’s three onboarding hires on 12 October within the plan. Five hires, which needed consent, and an “innovation budget” no agreement contains were rejected. A revised plan or lower first-quarter onboarding volume would reopen the decision.
