**An investment announcement does not tell you the company’s spending budget.** The headline may be a valuation or the amount that changes hands, and only money paid for newly issued shares reaches the company; the rest goes to selling shareholders, lenders or transaction costs. To turn it into a hiring plan, establish how much cash reaches the business, when it arrives and who can authorize its use. These questions matter under every ownership arrangement; the deal structure decides the answers.

Compare the **actual arrangement**. A fictional minority round can put €8m into Larkspur at closing; a fund’s €40m purchase of existing shares pays the seller; a corporate acquisition needs a separate agreement about group funding and local authority. Not every investor uses a fund or a holding company.

A fund-backed buyout separates **four types of organization**. The **investment firm** (manager, financial sponsor) employs investment professionals. A **fund** pools investors’ money under agreed terms. A **holding vehicle** holds the investment and possibly borrowing. A **portfolio company** is the operating business. When the company is later sold, the buyer pays for existing shares, either the portfolio company’s shares held by the holding vehicle or the holding vehicle itself; ask which.

A firm raising a new fund does not make that money available to a company held by an older one. Borrowing can sit in several places while its payments depend on the operating business’s cash.

A **limited partner (LP)** invests in the fund. In a common arrangement it makes a **commitment**, a promise to supply money under agreed conditions; a **capital call** requests payment against it. The **general partner (GP)** has management responsibilities and powers under the fund arrangements, often with the investment firm acting as manager. An increase in an investment’s estimated value pays the LP nothing until it is realized.

The manager is paid a **management fee** and may receive **carried interest**, a share of investment profits paid in an order set by a **distribution waterfall**. These terms shape which requests a company receives and when; see [[fund-economics]], [[different-bets]] and [[three-different-returns]]. The fund’s investment committee approves investments; the company’s board and executives approve company work. Ownership alone does not authorize a shareholder to direct staff or sign contracts.

**The completed record.** Under the fictional €8m share issue, announced 20 September, Larkspur received €7.6m net at closing on 3 October, confirmed from the bank statement. The board approved the year-one plan on 10 October, including four engineering hires; legal confirmed that spending above €250,000 outside the plan, or any increase in approved headcount, needs the investor director’s consent. Ines authorized three onboarding hires on 12 October within the plan; Alex’s request for five, above the approved headcount, and an “innovation budget” no agreement contains were rejected. A revised plan, lower first-quarter onboarding volume or cash falling materially below the approved forecast would reopen the decision.
