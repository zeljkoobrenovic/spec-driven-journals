# Editorial review: An Investment Announcement Is Not a Budget

Reviewed 14 September 2026. Read the complete article, summary and reader-facing comic text.

## Overall assessment

The central argument is that a transaction headline cannot establish a company's spending authority or available cash. Product and engineering leaders must locate the recipient, funding entity, conditions and authorized decision-maker. The €100 million opening is strong, and the four-entity map gives the collection useful technical depth.

The article tries to be both a practical guide to an announcement and a primer on fund economics. Capital calls support the main argument directly; the detailed distribution waterfall, catch-up and clawback discussion creates a second lesson. The opening also introduces an inaccurate contrast with companies that have no outside investor.

## Detailed feedback

- **Introduction and accuracy:** “In a company with no outside investor, the budget is roughly what the business earns and chooses to spend” is a substantive overstatement, not merely a tone preference. The preceding chapter itself explains borrowing, customer prepayments and asset sales. Those companies also face cash timing, obligations and approval limits. A transaction does not uniquely “break” an earnings-to-budget link.
- **Organization and flow:** “Map the Arrangement Before Relying on the Money” clearly serves the reader's immediate task. The four organizations and capital-call example deepen it well. “How Fees and Profit Sharing Work” then changes the task from finding usable funding to understanding distributions to fund investors. The final Mermaid map arrives after most of the concepts it could help organize.
- **Clarity and terminology:** Separating the firm, fund, holding vehicle and portfolio company is especially effective. The text sensibly acknowledges more complex structures. The categorical sentence “The general partner … manages the fund and makes the investment decisions” is less nuanced than the earlier separate-manager model and the summary's “under the fund arrangements.” Confirm or qualify the division between GP and delegated manager for the structure being illustrated.
- **Examples and pacing:** The fictional LP timeline makes “a valuation increase pays nobody” memorable. The €160 waterfall arithmetic is internally correct under its assumptions, but its relevance to Alex's hiring request appears only after the calculation. Definitions of preferred return, catch-up and clawback add disproportionate load here.
- **Tone and conclusion:** The pointed questions about which entity pays and which body wants a change are excellent; retain their practical skepticism. The conclusion summarizes the map clearly but never resolves the opening hiring expectation. Six questions and five reading recommendations further postpone the final takeaway.
- **Other formats:** The summary and comic introduction reproduce the misleading founder-owned-company contrast. This is an issue across all three versions, not an isolated sentence in the article.

## Recommended changes

- **High impact — Correct the opening across formats.** Suggested rewrite: “An announcement describes a transaction. To turn it into a hiring plan, establish how much cash reaches the business, when it arrives and who can authorize its use. These questions matter under every ownership arrangement.”
- **High impact — Make one completed funding map the payoff.** Return to Alex and show the named payer, approver, conditions and date for one proposed hire or migration. Specify which information comes from finance and which from the board or legal team. Readers should leave with an example of the promised one-page record.
- **Medium impact — Move the detailed waterfall to an optional fund-economics note.** Retain a brief explanation that fees and profit sharing can shape incentives, then link to “Different Bets” and “Three Different Returns.” Preserve the calculation for interested readers rather than deleting useful depth.
- **Medium impact — Bring the entity diagram forward and label the sale route.** Place it with the entity table, and state whether the future buyer purchases the operating company from the holding vehicle or the holding vehicle from the fund. The current arrow is a possible route, but beginners should not have to infer which shares are sold.
- **Low impact — Reduce the reflection list.** Combine questions about cash commitments and entity identity; keep the particularly strong “which body, exercising which right” question.

## Proposed structure

1. The announcement and Alex's hiring assumption.
2. Three possible cash destinations.
3. The four-entity map, with the diagram beside it.
4. Promised, called and available capital; why support can fail to arrive.
5. The completed company funding-and-authority record.
6. Optional fund fees and distributions, or a link to the relocated material.

This structure retains the fund mechanics that explain company constraints while giving the chapter a clear practical destination.
