**Positive earnings are not cash, and a technology plan that pays off in years cannot pay for next week's stock.** Toys R Us had a real improvement plan and ran out of room to fund it.

In 2005 a group linked to KKR, Bain Capital and Vornado bought the toy and baby-products retailer. By 2017 it was replacing its US e-commerce platform. The chief executive told the court that the old technology had prevented subscription deliveries of baby products, and described a proposed $90.4 million technology program for 2018–2021. The company knew what it wanted to build; the question was whether it could pay for it.

The fiscal 2016 accounts show why that was hard. **Operating earnings**, the result from running the business before interest and tax, were $460 million. Interest took $457 million, and after tax the group reported a net loss of $29 million. The cash side was starker: running the business, interest included, produced roughly no cash at all, while $252 million went on equipment, warehouses and software. [S35: Toys R Us fiscal 2016 results](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1005414/000100541417000010/truq4-16earningsreleaseexh.htm)

Then the suppliers moved. In September 2017, management told the court, reports of a possible bankruptcy led nearly 40% of suppliers to tighten terms, some demanding payment in advance or on delivery. The same stock now had to be paid for before it was sold, and management estimated the extra cash need could exceed $1 billion, on top of roughly $400 million a year for interest and repayments. These are management's explanations, not court findings, but the mechanism holds: **the product on the shelf was unchanged, yet the cash needed to put it there had grown.**

The company entered Chapter 11, the US court process that lets a business keep trading while it reorganizes its debts. The court authorized up to $3,125 million of new borrowing, a ceiling under lenders' conditions. That bought time, not a solution. In March 2018, with no buyer or agreed restructuring, the company sought to close its US stores, affecting roughly 33,000 jobs. One supplier, Hasbro, recorded $60.4 million of costs associated with the bankruptcy.

The documents show a funding problem; they do not prove the technology plan would have saved the business. That is the lesson to carry elsewhere: **a funded transition is necessary, not sufficient.** Before committing to a transformation timetable, ask whether the company can meet its obligations and fund the transition until the improvement pays off. Start from operating cash flow, check whether interest and supplier timing are already inside it so nothing is counted twice, then subtract capital spending and scheduled repayments. Test the plan against a change in payment terms, and keep the consequences for employees and suppliers visible in the decision record.
