**Choose investor support by what your company is missing, not by what the investor has on offer.** A stuck decision, a judgment nobody on the team has made before, a scarce skill and a lack of time to execute each call for a different kind of help.

The trouble often starts with a generous offer. An investor proposes “the full operating platform”: a technology partner, a pricing specialist, an acquisition team and a reporting package. The chief executive welcomes it; the technology chief sees four new streams of work for the same engineers. Before any of it reaches the roadmap, ask what the help would make possible and who would decide.

Begin with the business priority, whether growth, profitability, buying other businesses or scaling beyond the founder, and find what blocks it. The chapter offers four blueprints, which can be combined. **Board oversight** fits a capable team with a funded plan: the investor works through the board and management delivers. An **operating adviser** brings experience and challenge while the company supplies the people to act on it. **Shared specialists** supply scarce expertise, but only through named people, dates and costs. **Dedicated execution** puts someone inside the company to lead work its own team has no time for, with a reporting line and a planned handover.

How the company organizes its own work is a separate choice: businesses can keep local teams while sharing one service. But sharing an investor gives no business authority over another, and common ownership alone does not allow pooling customer data. Whatever you share needs an owner, funding and agreed access to information.

In a fictional example, an investor backs three software businesses whose customers sign in twice to use two products and want one sign-in and one help desk. Rather than rebuild everything on one platform, the group trials a shared sign-in service. A specialist commits six weeks, a company program leader twelve, and two engineers reserve one day a week each, with some internal improvements deferred to make room.

At six weeks the trial works, but moving every customer would take engineers already promised to a customer release. So the group chief executive approves a smaller step: two volunteer customers until week ten, when they return to their original sign-ins. The board gets a revised forecast that moves the offer’s full launch one quarter later, and at week twelve management decides whether to end, narrow or make the assignment permanent.

That is **what a usable blueprint records**: the purpose, who is accountable, who decides, which people and money each side commits, which work makes room, and when the arrangement is reviewed or ended. Technical progress informs the next commitment; it is not yet revenue.
