**Governance** is the arrangement for making decisions, overseeing them and holding people responsible. In a fictional example Morgan, the investor’s technology adviser, mentions that Larkspur should change cloud provider; Alex, the CTO, hears an instruction and starts planning. Establish the actual authority, funding and deadline first. The remark settles the adviser’s boundary; a separate onboarding proposal shows the approval process.

![A proposal moves through explicit recommendation, decision, funding and delivery roles, with advice and an escalation route.](assets/images/05-decide-who-decides/summary-at-a-glance.jpeg)
**Figure 1:** *Make authority, resources and the route for disagreement explicit.*

**Start by establishing what changed**: shares and the rights that come with them, expected outcomes, approvals needed and from whom, and who is accountable for each result. Morgan holds no approval right. Ines, the CEO, may approve spending up to a fictional €500,000 inside the approved plan; reserve draws, changes to what an approved envelope funds, and hires outside the headcount plan stay with the board. The suggestion is an option to cost, not a plan. The fund’s investment committee approved the investment, not Larkspur’s plans; a public operating-support description is a stated model, not a delegation of authority. [S22: KKR Capstone description](https://www.kkr.com/approach/capstone)

**Then fill in the record with names, thresholds and dates.** For the €1 million onboarding proposal: Priya and Alex recommend, Ines resolves trade-offs, Morgan tests the assumptions, the board approves because the amount exceeds Ines’s delegation, within two weeks. With several investors, do not turn **conflicting requests into parallel company plans**; find the forum that settles the choice. A board should connect a technical proposal to a business outcome, not design it. A request to appoint an executive, change the headcount plan or cut a team arrives as a formal right, a funding condition or influence; tell them apart first.

**Make conditional commitments explicit and plan the missed deadline.** The board declines the €1 million program but confirms the first stage Ines approved within her delegation: €210,000 (the €180,000 build plus first-year maintenance) and twelve engineer-weeks of the current team. Alex can deliver it in October if two hires start in June (about €175,000 this year, €300,000 a year recurring, a board decision because they add headcount), or in January without them. The board decides the hires in May, on first-quarter collections and the first three pilot implementations. If none has arrived, Alex takes the January option back to Ines and the team is told in writing that January is the plan. Customer promises tied to October need an explicit decision.

**Report to ask for a decision.** Three sentences do: what changed (effort per implementation fell from 80 to 62 hours, not the 50 assumed; about 40% of the remainder traces to customer data quality), what management proposes (a €40,000 data-quality step from the reserve), and the decision requested. It goes to the board, not Ines, because a reserve draw that adds a step changes what the envelope funds.

Engineers should hear one set of priorities: route conflicting instructions from adviser, CTO or investment team to the accountable company leader; see [[investors-adviser]] and [[useful-engagement]].
