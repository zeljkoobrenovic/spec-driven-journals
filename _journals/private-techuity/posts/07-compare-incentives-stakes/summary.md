**Owning shares together does not give everyone the same interests.** Executives, the fund, employees and customers can gain and lose different things from one decision, on different clocks, so alignment has to be designed rather than assumed.

A leader’s share percentage is only the beginning: what it pays depends on who is paid first, when the rights are earned, how many new shares are issued later and what happens if the leader leaves. In one fictional plan, 5% of sale money above a €100 million threshold pays €2 million at a €140 million sale, not 5% of the whole. An employee’s job is a stake too, but it pays month by month and can be lost with no sale money in return.

Targets need the same care. A bonus for 25% revenue growth can be bought with discounts and loss-making contracts. So Rotaline, the fictional company used throughout, counts only contracts that earn more in their first year than they cost to serve, and **pairs the target with the measure it could damage**: last year’s customers must still pay at least 90% of what they paid, not counting extra sales to them, and new customers must not be left waiting. If either slips, that quarter’s growth payment is withheld. Measuring the damage is not enough; the payout rule itself has to change.

The four bets become visible in one proposal. Rotaline’s board wants to close a second support location of fourteen people within nine months, saving about €600,000 a year. At ten times earnings, that adds about €6 million of company value. The executive on the 5% plan gains about €300,000 at a sale and the fund, holding 70%, about €4 million. The fund manager’s share of the fund’s profits, called carry, might take about €800,000 of that, but only once the fund has paid its investors everything its agreement puts first. Meanwhile **eleven of the fourteen lose their jobs**, and thirty customers renew their contracts in the next six months, just as the specialists who know their setups might leave and the handover begins.

So Ines, the chief executive, recommends a **staged closure over twelve months**, and the board approves it. Six people leave at month six and five at month twelve; the three specialists are paid to stay. Before each stage she checks renewals and support waiting time, and can pause if either slips. Staging costs €200,000 more and moves the full saving from month ten to month thirteen, and the fund waits longer. It reduces the customers’ risk rather than removing it, and the eleven people’s loss remains. **The decision record says so**, instead of claiming that a higher company value makes everyone better off.
