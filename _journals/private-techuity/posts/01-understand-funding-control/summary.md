**An investment announcement is not a budget.** Before planning hires on a headline figure, establish how much cash actually reaches the business, when it arrives and who can authorize spending it.

The headline number can mean several things. It may be a valuation, an estimate of what the whole company is worth, or the amount that changes hands. Even that amount need not reach the company. Money paid for newly issued shares goes to the company; money paid for existing shares goes to whoever sells them. Part of it may also repay old loans or cover deal costs.

So start by mapping the arrangement. In a fictional minority round, investors buy €8 million of new shares in Rotaline, and the company receives the money at closing, the day the deal completes. If a fund had instead paid €40 million for the founder's shares, the founder would have been paid and Rotaline's cash would not have changed. If a corporate group bought Rotaline, you would need to know which group company funds the work and who approves the local budget.

A fund-backed buyout adds layers. An investment firm manages a fund, a pool of investors' money with its own rules and lifetime. The fund owns a holding company, which may borrow, and that company owns the operating business that serves customers. **Common ownership does not mean one bank account.** A firm raising a new fund does not automatically support a company its older fund owns, and loans sitting higher up may still be repaid from the operating company's cash. The fund's own investors are paid only when cash comes back, typically from a sale: **a higher valuation pays nobody**.

Authority is just as layered. The fund's investment committee approves investments; the company's board and executives approve the company's work. Owning shares alone lets nobody direct staff or sign contracts. When someone says "the investor wants X", ask which body, exercising which right.

Back to the minority round. On the day of the announcement, Alex, the chief technology officer, proposed five onboarding engineers, assuming part of the €8 million was an innovation budget. Alex and Sam, who runs finance, then filled in a one-page funding-and-authority record. It showed €7.6 million, not €8 million, added to Rotaline's cash after €0.4 million of deal costs. The board had approved a year-one plan allowing four new engineering hires; any hire beyond that, or spending above €250,000 outside the plan, needs consent from the investors' board member. **Ines, the chief executive, authorized three hires within the plan.** Five would have exceeded the hiring allowance, and the innovation budget existed in no agreement. The decision reopens if the plan is revised, first-quarter onboarding work falls below plan, or quarter-end cash comes in more than €300,000 below forecast.
