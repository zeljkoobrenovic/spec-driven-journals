**An investment return compares what an investor receives or still holds with what it put in.** The company’s operating performance contributes to that result, alongside purchase price, borrowing, sale price and timing. Better earnings do not by themselves establish better products or a good investor result. Leaders inside the company judge progress by customers and earnings; the investor judges the same company by the return on its holding. Many requests that seem hard to justify from the business follow from the mechanics of the investment.

**EBITDA** means earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization. The last two are accounting charges that spread asset costs over time. EBITDA leaves out several payments a business must make. A **multiple** expresses business value relative to a financial measure: 10× EBITDA means ten times the stated annual earnings figure.

In the fictional buyout, annual EBITDA is €10 million and enterprise value is €100 million. The acquisition uses €60 million of debt and €40 million of fund equity. Using borrowing in this way is **leverage**. Five years later, EBITDA is €15 million, the business is valued at the same 10× multiple, and debt has fallen to €40 million. With no cash to offset debt, the fund receives €150 million less €40 million: €110 million.

The fund’s gain is €70 million on €40 million invested. Of that gain, €50 million reflects higher earnings at the unchanged multiple and €20 million reflects lower debt. The debt repayment needs its own explanation: operating cash, an asset sale and additional investor funding have different consequences. Include any new equity when calculating the return.

**MOIC**, multiple of invested capital, is €110 million divided by €40 million: 2.75×. **IRR**, internal rate of return, accounts for payment timing. With one investment and one receipt five years later, the annual rate is about 22.4%. More complicated payment patterns require every payment and date.

Leverage can **magnify losses too**. In a **separate downside scenario**, the business is worth €80 million and no debt has been repaid. With €60 million still owed, the fund receives €20 million: half its original investment. This differs deliberately from the successful example’s €40 million remaining debt.

For a whole fund, **DPI** compares distributions with money paid in, **RVPI** compares the remaining estimated value with money paid in, and **TVPI** combines them. Keep cash received separate from estimated unsold value, and state which fees the figures include.

A separate fictional minority example starts with €2m buying 200 of 1,000 shares. Another round issues 250 shares; the original investor buys none and falls from 20% to 16%. With identical payment rights and €20m of eventual equity proceeds, it receives €3.2m: 1.6 times its investment, before fees or tax. Corporate owners can also seek benefits in their own operations; those expectations need a separate funded operating case.
