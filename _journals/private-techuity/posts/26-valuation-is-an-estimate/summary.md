**A valuation estimates what a business or an ownership interest is worth at a date and for a purpose.** Different assumptions produce different answers, and each needs evidence. Product and engineering leaders rarely need to produce a valuation, but they do need to read one: an investor’s valuation assumptions can become growth, margin and cost targets, and understanding them lets you test whether the operating plan can support those targets. The chapter works in two stages, following one question throughout: what does “the company is worth €60 million” mean?

**Stage 1 reads the business’s numbers.** **Revenue** records sales during a period. **Earnings**, or profit, deduct the costs included in a specified measure. **Cash flow** follows money actually received and paid; a customer can pay before or after revenue is recorded. **EBITDA** means earnings before interest, income taxes, depreciation and amortization; the last two spread asset costs over time. Leaving them out helps compare operating earnings across differently financed businesses, but the excluded costs still matter. In the fictional income statement, €20 million of revenue less €16 million of operating expenses gives €4 million of EBITDA; after €1 million of asset charges, €1 million of interest and €0.5 million of tax, net profit is €1.5 million. The stage ends with a checkpoint: which of these measures is the €60 million compared with, for which period, and what has been excluded from it?

**Stage 2 interprets the valuation.** **Enterprise value** estimates the operating business’s value; **equity value** is what is attributable to the shares after **net debt**, borrowings less included cash. A €60 million business with €20 million of net debt has €40 million of equity value before other adjustments. Three approaches organize the evidence differently. **Market comparisons** use multiples: €60 million is 3× this company’s revenue or 15× its EBITDA, two expressions of one price, while an assumed 12× EBITDA would give €48 million, so examine the comparisons behind each multiple rather than picking the larger answer. **Discounted cash flow** translates expected future cash into today’s value and makes timing and reinvestment explicit; **asset-based valuation** examines assets and relevant obligations. Growth can influence any of them; it is not a separate method.

A funding round answers a different question. In a separate fictional round, an €8m pre-money equity valuation plus €2m of new cash gives €10m post-money and 20% for the new investor, assuming identical rights and no other adjustments. The company receives €2m, not €10m.

The chapter ends with one assumption to challenge. If the model behind the €60 million assumes onboarding cost per customer falls as sales grow, ask which change produces that reduction, when it becomes usable and who funds the transition. If no change is named, the target derived from the assumption needs revising before the plan does; [[growth-into-design]] chooses the system change.
