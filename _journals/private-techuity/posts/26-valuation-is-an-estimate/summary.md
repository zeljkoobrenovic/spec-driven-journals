**A valuation estimates what a business or an ownership interest is worth at a date and for a purpose.** Product and engineering leaders rarely produce a valuation but must read one: an investor’s valuation assumptions can become growth, margin (profit as a percentage of sales) and cost targets, so you can test whether the operating plan can support them. The chapter follows one question in two stages: what does “the company is worth €60 million” mean?

![Three valuation lenses (market comparisons, future cash and assets) estimate business value; equity value, the value of the shares, is business value minus included borrowing plus included cash, before other adjustments; new cash received by the company stays separate.](assets/images/26-valuation-is-an-estimate/summary-at-a-glance.jpeg)
**Figure 1:** *Business value − included borrowing + included cash = equity value, before other adjustments; the new cash a company receives is a separate quantity.*

**Stage 1 reads the business’s numbers.** **Revenue** records sales during a period. **Earnings**, or profit, deduct the costs included in a specified measure. **Cash flow** follows money received and paid; a customer can pay before or after revenue is recorded. **EBITDA** means earnings before interest, income taxes, depreciation and amortization; the last two spread the cost of assets such as equipment or software over the years they are used. Leaving them out helps compare operating earnings across differently financed businesses; the excluded costs still matter. In the fictional example, €20 million of revenue less €16 million of operating expenses (excluding depreciation and amortization) gives €4 million of EBITDA; after €1 million of depreciation and amortization, €1 million of interest and €0.5 million of tax, net profit is €1.5 million. The checkpoint: if the €60 million is quoted as a multiple, which measure and period does it use, and what has been excluded?

**Stage 2 interprets the valuation.** **Enterprise value**, or business value, is what the operating business is worth; **equity value** is the value of the shares: business value − included borrowing + included cash, that is, minus **net debt**. A €60 million business with €20 million of net debt has €40 million of equity value before other adjustments. Three approaches estimate business value. **Market comparisons** use multiples: €60 million is 3× this company’s revenue or 15× its EBITDA, two expressions of one price; an assumed 12× EBITDA would give €48 million, so examine the comparisons behind each multiple. **Discounted cash flow** converts expected future cash into today’s value; **asset-based valuation** adds up what the business owns (its assets) and subtracts what it owes. Growth influences all of them; it is not a separate method.

A funding round answers a different question. In a separate fictional round, the shares are valued at €8m just before new funding (the **pre-money valuation**). An investor pays €2m for newly issued shares, so the shares are worth €10m immediately after (the **post-money valuation**) and the new investor owns €2m / €10m = 20%, assuming identical rights and no other adjustments. The company receives €2m, not €10m.

One assumption to challenge: if the model behind the €60 million assumes the cost of setting up each new customer falls as sales grow, ask which change produces that reduction, when it becomes usable and who funds the transition. If no change is named, the target needs revising before the plan does; [[growth-into-design]] chooses the system change.
