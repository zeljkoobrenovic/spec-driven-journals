# Editorial review: A Valuation Is an Estimate, Not a Fact

Reassessed 15 September 2026 against the complete current article, summary and comic text, the previous review, revision log and Git changes. See the [collection review](../REVIEW.md).

## Current assessment

The two named stages and the €60m checkpoint substantially improve this finance primer. Beginners can now see why they learn revenue, earnings and cash before interpreting enterprise and equity value. The methods table puts the double-debt-deduction warning in the right place, and the onboarding conclusion translates valuation assumptions into work, timing and funding. Keep the full income statement, the distinction between fair-value reporting and negotiated prices, and the explicitly introductory treatment of DCF. A further chapter split is not justified by the current text alone.

The most consequential remaining problem is a newly introduced percentage error in the unicorn-study annotation. Later opening callouts also restore deterministic language that the revision carefully removed elsewhere. These are substantive fixes; the long but clearly signposted teaching sequence is principally a pacing preference.

## Active recommendations

- **High — Correct the percentage denominator in “To Probe Further.”** The Gornall–Strebulaev annotation says modeled fair values “average about 50% below” reported valuations. The cited NBER working-paper abstract says reported valuations average 50% **above** modeled fair values. Those statements are not equivalent. For a single illustrative pair, fair value 100 and reported value 150 means 50% above in one direction and 33⅓% below in the other; moreover, an average ratio cannot simply be inverted to obtain the average reverse ratio. Use the source's direction: “In 135 US unicorns, reported post-money valuations averaged about 50% above the authors' modeled fair values.” Keep the sample and model qualifications. The revision log itself records the correct source wording while claiming the incorrect manuscript wording is verified. [Primary source: NBER working paper 23895](https://www.nber.org/papers/w23895).
- **Medium — Restore the qualification in both opening callouts.** “Assumptions quietly become your … targets” loses the body's “can become”; “the investor's own forecast turned into a price” also collapses several kinds of valuation into one. Suggested wording: “A valuation may shape the targets proposed for your team. Identify its purpose and test the assumptions before adopting them.” In the Stage 1 checkpoint, replace “A valuation … is a price” with “an estimated value”; a transaction price may be evidence for that estimate, but the next section correctly distinguishes them.
- **Medium — Fix the calendar claim in “One Assumption to Challenge.”** Two quarters to build plus a proving cohort do not establish that a reduction “can't start in year one.” Some benefit could begin later in that year, depending on the start date and cohort duration. Say: “The model cannot assume a full year of savings; phase benefits from the expected validation date.” This preserves the excellent transition-funding lesson without adding an unsupported timing rule.
- **Low — Make the closing competence claim proportionate.** “You can now read a valuation” overstates what an introductory chapter establishes. “You can now ask which value is being quoted and which assumptions need testing” better matches the audience contract. Keep the SEC beginner route and optional deeper readings. Shorten repeated opening promises before cutting calculations.
