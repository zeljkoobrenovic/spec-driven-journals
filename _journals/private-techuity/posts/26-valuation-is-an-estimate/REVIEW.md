# Editorial review: A Valuation Is an Estimate, Not a Fact

Reviewed 14 September 2026. Read the complete article, summary and reader-facing comic text.

## Overall assessment

This is a careful finance primer for leaders who need to test the assumptions behind company targets. It distinguishes revenue, profit and cash; enterprise and equity value; and alternative valuation methods. Its numerical continuity, explanation of EBITDA's purpose and attention to accounting comparability are substantial strengths.

It contains two sizeable learning tasks: understanding financial statements and understanding valuation. The opening €60 million question disappears during a long earnings lesson, while the more familiar funding-round example arrives near the end. The chapter's substance should be preserved, but its teaching sequence needs a clearer route.

## Detailed feedback

- **Organization and introduction:** “Its assumptions decide which … results the company must deliver” makes a valuation sound like a binding operating instruction. The collection elsewhere treats an investment thesis as revisable. Say that valuation assumptions can become targets that leaders must examine. Revisit the opening number after the income-statement example so the reader sees why the preliminary lesson was necessary.
- **Clarity and technical depth:** The €20m revenue → €4m EBITDA → €1.5m net-profit table is clear and arithmetically sound. Explaining income taxes, financing and acquisition amortization before warning about exclusions makes EBITDA understandable rather than merely suspect. Preserve that balance. The simplified EV-to-equity bridge and cash restrictions are appropriately qualified.
- **Terminology and flow:** Defining an asset twice across the opening chapters is tolerable for independent reading, but the cumulative tangibles/intangibles/reconciliation/terminal-value vocabulary is substantial. “Three Ways to Estimate Value” mixes methods that can start from different claims; its later warning against deducting debt twice is useful and should sit nearer the table.
- **Examples and pacing:** The 3× revenue versus 15× EBITDA comparison demonstrates that two multiples can describe one price. The subsequent €48m alternative usefully exposes different assumptions. DCF gets only a one-payment calculation before terminal value appears, so its practical explanation is thinner than the multiples explanation. This asymmetry is acceptable if the chapter labels it an introduction rather than implying equal mastery of all methods.
- **Conclusion and voice:** “What to Carry Forward” is lucid but largely recaps the opening key points. A short example of challenging a growth or margin assumption would better connect the calculations to leadership.
- **Evidence:** The SEC's [EBIT/EBITDA guidance, questions 103.01–103.02](https://www.sec.gov/rules-regulations/staff-guidance/corporation-finance-interpretations/non-gaap-financial-measures) supports distinguishing adjusted measures and reconciling them; the simplified table explicitly excludes other charges. The Gornall–Strebulaev reading annotation loses important sample context: the study concerns 135 US unicorns, rather than private companies generally, and its fair values are model estimates. The [research paper](https://centers.tuck.dartmouth.edu/uploads/cpee/files/SSRN-id2955455.pdf) identifies that population. Correct the scope rather than treating “about 50%” as a universal adjustment to round valuations.
- **Other formats:** Both companions retain the main distinctions. Their introductory “the estimate becomes the target” language needs the same qualification as the article.

## Recommended changes

- **High impact — Create two explicitly named learning stages.** “Read the business's numbers” should end with a short checkpoint before “Interpret a valuation.” If audience testing shows beginners still struggle, split these into separate chapters, keeping this permalink for valuation and adding a financial-statements primer before it.
- **High impact — Correct the research annotation's population and inference.** Suggested rewrite: “A study of 135 US unicorns shows how valuing every share at the latest preferred-share price can overstate the modelled value of the whole company.”
- **Medium impact — Soften deterministic ownership claims.** “An investor's valuation assumptions can become growth and margin targets. Understanding them helps you test whether the operating plan can support those targets.”
- **Medium impact — Add one engineering-facing conclusion.** For example: “The model assumes onboarding costs fall as sales grow. Ask which change produces that reduction, when it becomes usable and who funds the transition.” Link to “Growth Into Design” for the full treatment.
- **Low impact — Prioritize the further reading.** Mark the SEC primer as the beginner route and the valuation texts as optional depth. Avoid suggesting the chapter teaches a comparative ranking of method accuracy unless the cited review supports that claim.

## Proposed structure

Use the €60m question as a continuous thread: revenue/profit/cash → financial-statement checkpoint → EV/equity bridge → valuation methods and assumptions → new-money example → one operating assumption to challenge. Keep the full calculations; reduce repeated reminders that a valuation is not cash.
