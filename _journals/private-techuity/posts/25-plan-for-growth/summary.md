**“Make the software more flexible” is not a design brief.** Recover the business requirement behind the request, then compare options against that requirement and choose one you can fund, date and reverse.

Investors hand over expectations, not designs. Fast growth reaches the team as a vague request with the reasoning left behind: which customers, which changes, by when, at what cost. Without it, a team builds too much or the wrong thing, and pays before any benefit arrives.

Take Larkspur, a fictional software company whose investor expects it to enter a second country. Recovered, “more flexible” means: invoice, contract with, support and set up customers under that country’s tax and pricing rules, with **the first customer payments received within twelve months**, and each further country cheaper to add than the last, because the investor’s plan assumes a third. The obstacle is known: the country rules are written into the same code as the rest of the invoicing module, so only two specialists can safely change them.

With €300,000 approved for the work, Alex, who leads technology, and Priya, who leads product, price three options:

- **A. Buy a supplier’s service:** €60,000 setup plus €40,000 a year per country, first invoice in month four or five. Fast and cheap now, but the tangle stays, and finance must reconcile two billing routes every month.
- **B. Separate the rules and keep them:** €140,000 once, no supplier fee, first invoice in month eight. Later countries become settings, but the work could break invoices that already work.
- **C. Replace the billing system:** €1.3 million, first invoice around month twelve. Too costly and too late; it stays only as a benchmark.

Larkspur chooses B. It costs €80,000 more upfront, A is cheaper over one year, and the two are level at two. What decides it is **how long the design will be used**. The investor expects to keep its share for about four more years, and over four years A’s subscriptions reach €220,000 against B’s €140,000, even if no third country arrives. Ines, the chief executive, approves the choice within the board’s budget.

The team writes down in advance what would change the choice. At month four, last year’s invoices are rerun through the new code. If they show differences nobody can explain within two weeks, or incidents eat the specialists’ time, Larkspur **switches to A**, which still needs two weeks of specialist effort of its own; without it, the launch is deferred rather than the date assumed. If fewer than five customers have signed by month six, the launch waits. If the third country leaves the plan, the decision is reassessed, not reversed.

One caution about savings: they must be funded before they arrive, and counted once. Future savings and a valuation multiplied up from them are two prices for the same benefit.

B leaves Larkspur running the country rules itself; the next cost line an investor will question is the hosting bill, which [[evaluate-cloud-costs]] takes up.
