**Technical debt is not a number to pay off; it is a monthly carrying cost.** Measure what each item costs, risks and slows, and fund large fixes in stages that each must earn the next.

Investors hear “technical debt” as spending customers never see, and “rewrite” as a year of engineering with the benefit at the end. An issue count or a percentage of the code gives them nothing to decide with. A register does: each item in three columns, **what it costs to keep**, in euros a month; what risk it carries, such as incidents or unsupported software; and what it slows, such as delivery or customers the product cannot serve. The last two keep their own units and are never added to the first.

Take Larkspur, the fictional software company, in October 2027. The investor’s adviser asks Alex, who leads technology, how much debt there is, and why not rewrite the old parts. Alex’s register shortlists five items costing about €10,000 a month. Only €5,500 of that is cash paid to hosting providers; the rest is engineers’ time already on the payroll, so freeing it releases no cash. The biggest problem is the scheduling engine, which runs each region on one server. Switching to a replacement server takes 45 minutes, and the design keeps out customers above about 400 technicians: two prospects, about €12,000 a month of revenue not won.

A rewrite would take four engineers nine months and about €340,000, €300,000 of it staff time and €40,000 cash, with the old engine frozen and every benefit arriving at once at the end. Alex proposes **tranches** instead: separately approved stages, each released by a gate, a check agreed in advance. Tranche 1 splits the schedule computation from the part that answers customers, so it can switch servers quickly and run on smaller ones. It costs about €58,000 and pays off in month three. Tranche 2, for the largest customers, costs €80,000 and starts only if the gate passes on 31 March 2028: switchover under five minutes, hosting down €1,500 a month, no incident caused by the split, and fixes no slower than before.

Benefit-at-the-end still fits when a date is close and no useful half-way state exists. The reporting software lost its security fixes in March 2027, and the largest prospect needs supported software by March 2028. Moving it costs six engineer-weeks and €5,000, so it goes first, in November and December. A retiring AI model gets the same treatment: test its successor before signing anything.

Not all debt has to go. The remaining tangles in the invoicing code cost about €3,000 a month, but removing them would take the two specialists Larkspur can least spare. **Carrying debt on purpose is a decision**, not neglect, once the cost is stated, the reason written down and a review date set.
