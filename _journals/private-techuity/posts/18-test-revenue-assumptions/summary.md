**A product change creates business value only through a chain: it changes what customers can do, then what they do, and eventually the company’s income and costs.** Each link can break, so test each one and let the measured result shape the next commitment.

Start with a need worth serving. At Larkspur, the book’s fictional scheduling-software company, customers who have already bought are queuing for setup, and most need the same specialist to enter their settings by hand. The investor’s plan assumes many more new customers without a matching rise in setup staff. So Priya, who leads product, proposes one reusable setup step. Write down what the investor expects from it, but treat that as an expectation to test, not proof of demand.

Then do honest arithmetic. The model assumes 100 setups a year at 80 staff hours each, priced at €75 an hour including employment costs: €600,000 of staff time. If the step brings setups down to 50 hours, it frees 3,000 hours, worth €225,000. **That is capacity, not cash**, because the same people are still paid. Freed time becomes a result only through a plan: serving waiting customers, or avoiding a supplier’s invoice or a hire. And the step has its own costs, €180,000 to build and €30,000 a year to maintain.

The pilot compared its first eight customers with the twelve setups of the previous two quarters. That comparison leaves differences: sales chose the pilot customers, and the same specialist, perhaps faster with practice, served both groups. The honest claim is that **the step plausibly helped**, not that it alone caused the result.

At day 90 the eight averaged 62 hours, not 50. That is 144 hours released; the 1,800 hours and €135,000 a year quoted beside it are a projection that assumes 100 comparable setups. Yet the median wait from contract to first real use stayed at about ten weeks. The team spent less time on each setup, but customers still waited for their own data, and about 40% of the remaining hours traced to poor customer data. No contract had been cancelled, and no cash benefit had appeared.

The board had agreed its rule when it funded the pilot, and 62 hours fell in the middle band. So at day 100 it funded a step that checks and corrects customer data before setup, for €40,000 and four engineer-weeks from its reserve. The money already spent argued neither way; the measured bottleneck did. It rejected both stopping the pilot and hiring two specialists at €300,000 a year. A second group of eight must now average 50 hours or less, counting all data work, bought-in help included, with a median wait of eight weeks or less. **Both tests must pass** before Priya may request another stage.

The measurement did its job: it changed the next commitment instead of decorating the last one. Plans like this increasingly lean on AI, and that word hides three different investment questions, which [[clarify-ai-strategy]] separates.
