**Incentives are rewards and consequences that influence choices.** An investment may add new equity terms, exit expectations and fund-level incentives to the rewards and risks already shaping company decisions. Shared ownership creates some common interests, but executives, the fund, employees and customers still face different exposure, rights and time horizons, and that layer shapes which proposals get approved.

**Management equity** gives company leaders ownership or economic rights under an agreed plan. A founder may reinvest part of a sale, called a **rollover**; a manager may buy shares; an executive may receive **options**, rights to buy shares on stated terms. Each pays differently. **Vesting** describes how rights are earned. **Preferences** determine which shares receive money first. **Dilution** describes how issuing more equity changes existing ownership. Departure terms decide what a leaver keeps. In the fictional example, a manager has 5% of proceeds above a €100 million threshold; at €140 million the payment is €2 million, not 5% of the whole. In a separate example, vested options on 10,000 shares cost €2 each to exercise; a sale at €3 per share leaves €10,000 before tax or fees. The company’s payout waterfall governs these; the fund has its own waterfall for **carried interest**, the manager’s share of fund profits, described in [[fund-economics]]. The fund’s investors also have interests that do not cover every employee or customer concern. [S02: ILPA principles](https://ilpa.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/06/ILPA-Principles-3.0_2019.pdf)

**A target needs the measure it could damage beside it.** In a worked fictional choice, a bonus on 25% revenue growth counts only contracts with positive first-year contribution, is paired with net revenue retention of at least 95% and the onboarding waiting-time target, is reviewed quarterly by Ines and the board’s pay committee from Sam’s numbers, and is withheld when the guardrail fails. Measurement alone cannot fix a payout rule that rewards the damaging choice; the rule has to change.

**One consolidation shows the four bets.** Closing an acquired product’s second support location would save about €600,000 a year. At an assumed ten-times multiple that is about €6 million more equity value at a sale: about €300,000 for a manager already above the threshold, and a share for the fund under its waterfall, both on the exit clock. Eleven of fourteen employees lose their jobs now. Thirty add-on customers risk losing the people who know their configurations during the quarters in which they renew. Larkspur decides a staged twelve-month consolidation with three specialists retained, funds about €550,000 through a board approval, and pauses the next stage if renewals fall or waiting time is breached for two months. The burden on the eleven remains unresolved, and the record says so instead of claiming enterprise value makes everyone better off.

Review a decision by separating its quality, its execution and its result, and reward early reports of a failed assumption.
