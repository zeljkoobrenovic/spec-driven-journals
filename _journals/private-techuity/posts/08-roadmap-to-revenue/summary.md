Product work creates business value through **changes in customer behavior and operating economics**. A **roadmap** item describes proposed work; its benefit still needs an explanation. An investor may expect a change to support a particular growth, margin or retention target. Make that expectation explicit, then test whether the benefit and its timing are credible; that chain is usually asserted, not shown.

**Start with a need worth serving.** At fictional Larkspur, customers already sold are waiting for setup, most depending on one implementation specialist’s manual configuration, and the growth plan assumes their number will rise without more implementation staff. Apply the same customer and economic test to every request; an investor introduction is a lead to test, not proof of demand. [S15: DORA 2024 report](https://dora.dev/research/2024/dora-report/)

**Make the chain explicit**: intervention, operating change, customer outcome and economic effect. **Onboarding** is the setup a customer needs before using the product. Reusable settings may reduce that effort, bring forward billing, or help customers stay. Every connection is a hypothesis; weak demand, poor adoption or new maintenance costs can break it.

**Distinguish capacity from cash.** At €75 an hour fully loaded, €150,000 per specialist a year, 100 implementations at 80 hours is €600,000 of capacity; at the assumed 50 hours, €375,000. The €225,000 difference is 3,000 hours of released time, not cash, while the same people are paid. A conversion plan says how it becomes a result: serve waiting customers, avoid an invoice or a hire, or find that another bottleneck binds. Count the €180,000 build commitment and €30,000 a year of maintenance before claiming a net benefit.

**Check what explains the result.** Fix a baseline (customers, dates, costs); compare like groups; name the differences that remain. The pilot compared eight customers with the previous twelve implementations, but sales chose the pilot customers, one specialist served both groups and the earlier period included a quarter-end rush. The honest claim: the setup step plausibly helped, with those limits.

**Keep observed apart from projected, and let the result change the commitment.** Priya’s day-90 cohort of eight averaged 62 hours, not 50; about 40% of remaining hours traced to poor customer data; waiting time was unchanged. That is 144 hours observed; 1,800 hours and €135,000 a year are a projection over 100 comparable implementations. As cash: €180,000 committed, about €90,000 incurred by day 100; maintenance starts next financial year; the contract specialist’s twelve months had not run, so nothing was cancelled. At day 100 the board applied its rule for 60–70 hours: fund a data-quality step (€40,000, four engineer-weeks from the reserve), justified by a named bottleneck, not by money already spent, keep hiring deferred and move the expansion decision to the next quarterly review. The gate at month six is 50 hours or less with waiting time down; otherwise hiring, a narrower target or the financing conversation reopen.

Watch indicators that could reveal harm: abandonment, error rates, support demand and time to first useful outcome. The chain broke between released effort and customers served; the measurement changed the next commitment.
