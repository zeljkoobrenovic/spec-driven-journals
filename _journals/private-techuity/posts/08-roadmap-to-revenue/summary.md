Product work creates business value through **changes in customer behavior and operating economics**. A **roadmap** sets out intended product changes. An item describes proposed work; its benefit still needs an explanation. An investor may expect a change to support a particular growth, margin or retention target. Make that expectation explicit, then test whether the benefit and its timing are credible. That chain is most often asserted rather than shown.

**Start with a need worth serving.** In the fictional Larkspur example, customers already sold are waiting for setup, most of them depending on one implementation specialist’s manual configuration, and the growth plan assumes their number will rise without more implementation staff. Apply the same customer and economic test to requests from sales, executives, investors and acquisitions; an investor introduction is a lead to test, not proof of demand. [S15: DORA 2024 report](https://dora.dev/research/2024/dora-report/)

**Make the chain explicit**: intervention, operating change, customer outcome and economic effect. **Onboarding** is the setup needed before a customer can use the product. Reusable settings may reduce that effort, bring forward customer use and billing, or help customers stay. Every connection is a hypothesis. Weak demand, poor adoption or new maintenance costs can break it.

**Distinguish capacity from cash.** In the pilot model, 100 implementations a year at 80 hours and €75 an hour is €600,000 of capacity; at the assumed 50 hours it is €375,000. The €225,000 difference is 3,000 hours of released time, not a cash saving, while the same people are paid. The company needs a conversion plan: serve waiting customers, avoid an invoice or a hire, or find that another bottleneck binds. Count the €180,000 build and €30,000 a year of maintenance before claiming a net benefit. Distinguish **financial contribution**, revenue less specified costs, from the causal claim that a change helped produce a result.

**Check what explains the result.** Establish a baseline with clear customers, dates and costs; compare like groups; name the differences that remain. The pilot compared eight customers with the previous twelve implementations, but sales chose the pilot customers, the same specialist served both groups and the earlier period included a quarter-end rush. The honest claim is that the setup step plausibly helped, with those limits.

**Let the result change the commitment.** Effort fell from 80 to 62 hours, not 50. About 40% of the remaining hours traced to poor customer data. Customer waiting time was unchanged, because customers still waited for their own data: the released 18 hours per implementation, worth about €135,000 a year in modelled capacity, were not converted into customers served. The board funded a data-quality step from the reserve (€40,000, four engineer-weeks), kept the second-country expansion deferred and left hiring deferred. The next cohort’s waiting time decides whether the structural explanation, that one specialist cannot scale, returns.

Protect the future product with indicators that could reveal harm: abandonment, error rates, support demand and time to first useful outcome. The chain broke at the link from released effort to customers served; the measurement changed the next commitment.
