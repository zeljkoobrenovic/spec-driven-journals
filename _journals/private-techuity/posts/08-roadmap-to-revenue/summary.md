Product work creates business value only when it **changes what customers do, or what the business earns and spends**. A **roadmap** item describes proposed work, not its benefit. An investor, who has put money into the company for a share of it, may expect a change to support a target: more sales, a larger share of sales kept as profit, or more customers staying. Write that expectation down; the proposal must demonstrate the chain from work to result, not assume it.

**Start with a need worth serving.** At fictional Larkspur, paying customers are waiting for **onboarding**, the setup needed before they can use the product. Most depend on one specialist entering settings by hand, yet the growth plan assumes more customers with the same setup staff. A potential customer introduced by an investor is someone to test, not proof of demand. [S15: DORA 2024 report](https://dora.dev/research/2024/dora-report/)

**Make the chain explicit**, and test every link: the product change, the change in daily work, the result for the customer and the effect on money.

**Distinguish freed time from cash.** The model assumes 100 setups a year at 80 staff hours each, priced at €75 an hour (pay plus employment costs): €600,000 of staff time. At the hoped-for 50 hours, 3,000 hours are freed, worth €225,000. That is time, not cash, because the same people are still paid. A plan must say how the time becomes a result: serving waiting customers, or avoiding a supplier’s invoice or a hire. Count the build (€180,000 agreed) and upkeep (€30,000 a year) before claiming a benefit.

**Check what explains the result.** The pilot, a limited trial, compared its first eight customers with the previous twelve setups. But sales chose the pilot customers, and one specialist served both groups. So the setup step plausibly helped; it was not proved the cause.

**The measured result.** At day 90 Priya, the product leader, found the eight averaged 62 hours, not 50. About 40% of remaining hours traced to poor customer data. Customers still waited about ten weeks from contract to first real use. That is 144 measured hours. The yearly 1,800 hours and €135,000 are an estimate assuming 100 comparable setups.

**The spending.** By day 100 about €90,000 of the agreed €180,000 of work had been received, which does not mean paid. No contract had been cancelled, and no cash benefit had appeared.

**The next decision.** At day 100 the board, the directors who oversee the company, applied its pre-agreed rule for a result of 60 to 70 hours. It funded one step to check and correct customer data before setup: €40,000 and four engineer-weeks (each one engineer’s work for a week), from money held back for such decisions. The reason is the obstacle the measurement found, not money already spent. At month six a second group must average 50 hours or less, data work included, and wait eight weeks or less. Otherwise the board approves no further stage.

The chain broke between freed effort and customers served. The measurement changed the next commitment.
