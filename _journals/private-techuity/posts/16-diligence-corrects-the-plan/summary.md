**Technical due diligence** investigates a company’s product, systems, people and risks before an investment. In this transaction, it tests the product, technology and team assumptions behind the proposed investment: whether fictional Larkspur can double onboarding volume without proportional growth in implementation staff. Its findings feed the price, funding, accepted risk, conditions and the plan the company will be held to. Whether new cash reaches the company depends on the transaction form; the findings shape the plan either way. Diligence is useful when it tests an important assumption, even when evidence confirms it.

**What you can do depends on when you have access.** Before signing, take part in the information process and attach conditions to every commitment. Between signing and closing, where that gap exists, confirm findings with those who were absent and prepare the acceptance record. After closing, when earlier participation was impossible, request the material findings, record the access limit and ask for an early review; your levers are then the plan and its funding, and any price adjustment depends on the signed terms. These are opportunities the process may give you, not rights.

Begin with the **investment thesis**, why the investment should succeed, matched to the transaction: a minority round tests the use of new money, a buyout the plan under its financing, a strategic investment rights and interfaces. Help the reviewers test those assumptions against actual work; explain how the samples were chosen and what they cannot establish.

The chapter completes one **material finding**, D-3, with fictional figures. Observed: five of the last twelve implementations sampled (one country, two quarters); three needed one specialist’s manual work; about 80 hours on average across all five, every recorded hour from contract to go-live. Reported by Alex: roughly 40% of the effort is poor customer data. Inferred by Morgan: the dependency is structural. Not established: representativeness, and how much each explanation accounts for; the two are compatible. Options: hire two specialists at about €300,000 a year; fund a reusable setup step as a pilot for €180,000, €30,000 a year and 12 engineer-weeks; or narrow the target. Agreed response: the investment proceeds, the first-year volume assumption falls from 2× to 1.5× as a forecast conditional on pilot evidence, second-country expansion waits for that evidence, and the pilot becomes a funded condition of the early plan. The fund’s investment committee approves the terms; the Larkspur board adopts the plan and funding; Priya is accountable; Alex’s disagreement is recorded, to be sized by Priya’s day-90 cohort measurement; the next money follows the largest actionable share of remaining effort with a costed step, and the board decides at day 100.

The seven assessment dimensions are a coverage check, not a score; missing evidence stays missing. The one-page **Technology Investment Thesis** summarizes and links the findings rather than repeating them ([[toolkit]]).

Receiving a report is not acceptance. Each accountable leader records agreement, disagreement or the evidence still needed, and the finding’s identifier travels into the early operating plan: D-3 becomes a funded priority in [[first-hundred-days]].
