**Technical due diligence** investigates a company’s product, systems, people and risks before an investment. In this transaction, technical diligence tests the product, technology and team assumptions behind the proposed investment: whether fictional Larkspur can double onboarding volume without proportional growth in implementation staff. Its findings feed the price, the funding, the accepted risk, the transaction conditions and the plan the company will later be held to. Whether new cash reaches the company depends on the transaction form; the findings shape the plan either way. Diligence is useful when it tests an important assumption, including when the evidence supports keeping the existing plan.

**What you can do depends on when you have access.** Before signing, take part in the information process and attach conditions to every commitment. Between signing and closing, where that gap exists, confirm findings with the people who were absent and prepare the acceptance record. After closing, when earlier participation was impossible, request the material findings, record the access limit and ask for an early review. These are operating opportunities the agreed process may give you, not universal rights.

Begin with the **investment thesis**, the explanation of why the investment should succeed, matched to the transaction: a minority round tests the proposed use of new money, a buyout tests the plan under the proposed financing, a strategic investment tests rights, interfaces and commercial benefit. Help the reviewers examine those assumptions using evidence from actual work. Explain how the samples were chosen and what they cannot establish. “High confidence” should describe evidence within a stated scope, not the assessor’s seniority.

The chapter completes one **material finding**, D-3, with fictional figures. Observed: in three of five sampled implementations, configuration needed one specialist’s manual work, about 80 hours each. Reported by Alex: roughly 40% of the effort comes from poor customer data. Inferred by Morgan: the dependency is structural. Not established: representativeness, and the true split between data quality and product limitation. Options: hire two specialists at about €300,000 a year; fund a reusable setup step as a pilot for €180,000, €30,000 a year and 12 engineer-weeks; or narrow the first-year segment. Agreed response: the investment proceeds, the first-year volume assumption falls from 2× to 1.5×, second-country expansion becomes conditional on pilot evidence, and the pilot becomes a funded condition of the early plan. The fund’s investment committee approves the terms; the Larkspur board approves the plan and funding; Priya is accountable; Alex’s disagreement is recorded, to be resolved by the pilot cohort’s measured effort split.

The seven proposed assessment dimensions, from product to economics, are a coverage check, not a score; missing evidence stays missing. The one-page **Technology Investment Thesis** does not repeat the findings; it summarizes and links them, as the [[toolkit]] shows.

Receiving a report is not acceptance. Each accountable leader records agreement, disagreement or the evidence still needed, and the finding’s identifier travels into the early operating plan. D-3 becomes a funded priority in [[first-hundred-days]].
