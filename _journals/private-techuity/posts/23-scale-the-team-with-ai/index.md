> **IN THIS SECTION, YOU WILL:** Learn to answer the claim that artificial intelligence (AI) changes what your existing team can complete, whether it arrives as a reason to buy tools, to hire fewer people or to cut. The method is the one the hiring chapter used for a headcount claim: **trace** the work, recording each stage it passes through and each wait between stages, measure the work that completes, count the cost, and decide at a dated **gate**, a fixed day on which the result is judged by rules written in advance.

> **WHY INVESTORS CARE:** An investor can fund people or tools, and hears from every direction that tools now replace people. It wants to know whether money spent on AI buys work that completes, or buys **seats**, licensed user accounts, that wait behind the same queue as the last hire.

> **WHY YOU SHOULD CARE:** “AI means we need fewer engineers” is a capacity claim, and it is tested the same way whether it argues for a hire or against one. If you cannot say which stage of the work a tool touches and what completed work changed, someone else will decide your headcount from a vendor’s slide.


> **KEY POINTS:**
>
> * **A tool can only relieve a constraint it touches.** Waits for a decision are a question of authority, and no tool removes them. Building a change (writing and testing its code) and releasing it (putting the finished change into the live system) can change, and can be measured. Whether a queue that exists because only two people can do the work safely shortens is the question the pilot has to answer.
> * **Capacity is work that completes with the old quality checks.** Lines written, seats active and tokens used are activity. Measure changes and customer setups delivered per month, with the error rate beside them, checked the same way as before the tool, and count the time spent checking generated work as a cost.
> * **“Fewer people” is a claim to test, not a plan to execute.** Separate a condition attached to money, a vote by a director (a member of the board that oversees the company) and an adviser’s comparison with other companies, answer each with the response it requires, and leave the deferred hire’s conditions unchanged until the gate is passed.

<br>
At Larkspur, the fictional scheduling-software company this book follows, the claim arrives in a note. Ninety days after the hiring chapter’s decision, Morgan, the technology adviser to Larkspur’s investor, writes to Ines, the chief executive who leads the company. **Portfolio companies**, the other companies the investor has money in, are reporting large gains from AI coding tools: **artificial intelligence (AI)** is software that predicts or generates content from data, and a coding tool generates code. Larkspur should adopt such tools across engineering, and the second team deferred in that chapter, Morgan suggests, may no longer be needed at all.

The note is well meant and partly right. It makes the promise that outside investment made in the chapter [[fix-decisions-before-hiring]]: more capacity, faster than the company could grow it alone. That chapter showed that money for people buys capacity only when the queue is made of missing people rather than of unclear authority and concentrated knowledge. Money for AI tools faces the same test, with one difference: the AI claim runs in both directions at once, a reason to buy tools and a reason to hire fewer people or to let some go.

The note lands on a plan the chapter [[fix-decisions-before-hiring]] recorded, with the same people and the same figures, all fictional. A **pricing rule** is an instruction that decides what a customer is charged, for example “customers in one country pay a surcharge on bookings made by phone”. A traced change to one such rule took sixteen working days, thirteen of them waiting. Two approval waits were removed by **delegation**, giving named people the authority to decide within stated limits so that nobody waits for a signature, and the forecast **lead time**, the time from a customer’s request to the change running in **production** (the live system customers use), came down to about ten working days. Seven of the ten still wait for the two specialists who alone can safely change the **invoicing module**, the part of the software that prepares customer invoices, the bills that state what a customer owes.

One billing engineer was funded to become the third such person and moved into the billing area on the day of that decision, which is the day the month counts in this chapter start from. A five-person second team was deferred until three conditions are met: customers in the second country show they want the product, the next **funding round**, the company’s next attempt to raise a further amount of investment, is **committed**, meaning the investors have signed the documents that oblige them to pay (the cash itself arrives later), and the **board**, the directors who oversee the company on behalf of its owners, has approved the roles. Alex, who leads technology as chief technology officer (CTO), Priya, who leads product, and Sam, who leads finance, appear below as they act.

## Capacity Is Work That Completes

The hiring chapter defined **capacity** as the work a team can complete, as distinct from **headcount**, the number of people it employs. The definition does not change because the proposed addition is software rather than a person.

AI tools produce activity that is easy to count and pleasant to report: **lines accepted** (lines of code the tool suggested and a developer kept), draft answers generated, seats active and **tokens** consumed. Tokens are the small pieces of text or code an AI service reads and writes, and the service bills by them. None of these is capacity. They show how busy the tool was, not what customers received.

A change is delivered when it has been **released**, put into production. Whether it was sound is a separate question. Each month finance **reconciles** every invoice: it checks the invoice against the orders, prices and billing records behind it. An invoice that does not match is an error, traced to the change that caused it, and a change is judged by the invoices it affects in the thirty days after its release.

A customer setup is delivered when the customer is using the software. Whether it was sound depends on whether anyone had to correct its **configuration** in the thirty days afterwards: the settings entered for that customer from its order, such as its currency, the day its invoice is issued and the discount agreed. Delivery and soundness are both counted, and they are counted apart.

Four terms carry that definition into numbers.

- A **delivered** item is a change that has reached production or a setup that has gone live.
- An **error** is an invoice that fails reconciliation, traced to the change that caused it, or a correction a customer needs after go-live. Every item is followed for the same thirty days after delivery, and an item with at least one error in that time is **faulty**.
- The **error rate** is faulty items per hundred delivered items. The faulty items stay in that hundred, so they cannot disappear from the denominator, the count the rate is divided by.
- **Throughput** is delivered items per month, every delivered item included, faulty or not.

For example, ten setups of which one is faulty, twenty with two faulty and twenty with three are counted as ten, twenty and twenty delivered, with error rates of ten, ten and fifteen per hundred.

The qualification is what makes the count honest: a rise in throughput counts as capacity only while the error rate holds at or below its level before the tool. If ten per hundred were that level, the second month would be a real rise in capacity and the third would not, although it delivered as many setups.

The rate counts faulty items, not wrong invoices, because one change can touch many invoices. Suppose ten changes produce ten wrong invoices out of 1,000 checked, and ten comparable changes in a busier month produce twenty wrong out of 2,000. The share of wrong invoices is 1% both times, but wrong invoices per hundred changes would be 100 and then 200. The second month would look twice as bad only because it had twice the invoices. The rate therefore asks each change one question over the same thirty days: did it cause an error? A change that breaks one invoice and a change that breaks fifty both count once.

Two figures sit beside the rate for engineering: the number of wrong invoices, the **correction burden** finance has to clear, and wrong invoices per hundred checked. They show how much damage a faulty change did, and a change that breaks many invoices is investigated whatever the rate says. Neither is the gate’s quality test, because both depend on invoice volume and on how many invoices a change happens to touch.

The table separates that evidence from activity.

| Counts as evidence of capacity | Does not count |
| --- | --- |
| Pricing-rule changes delivered per month, request to production, read together with the error rate the monthly invoice reconciliation gives them | Lines of code written or accepted from suggestions |
| Customer setups delivered per month, read together with the share that needed a correction in the following thirty days | Draft configurations produced |
| Lead time per change, in elapsed working days, measured on the same trace as before | Time a developer reports feeling faster |
| Reviewer hours per change, compared with the hours the same review took before the tool | Seats licensed, seats active, tokens consumed |
| The same quality checks as before the tool, applied to every change and every setup each month, reported as faulty items per hundred delivered items | A quality check redesigned after the tool arrived |

The **quality checks are the old ones**, because a tool that raises the number of changes while lowering the share that reconcile has not added capacity; it has moved the work to whoever finds the errors. And **review time is a cost**, because generated work has to be read by someone who understands it, and at Larkspur the people who understand the invoicing module are the two the plan is trying to protect. Only the *additional* review counts, the hours above what the same review took before the tool; review that happened anyway is not charged to the pilot.

## Which Constraint Can a Tool Touch?

The hiring chapter’s trace sorted the sixteen days into three constraints: a **decision constraint** (waits that exist because authority sits with one person), a **knowledge constraint** (a queue that exists because only two people can safely change the module) and a **staffing constraint** (how much capacity is actually missing once the first two are addressed). Morgan’s note, like most AI proposals, does not say which of the three the tool would relieve. Run it against the trace and the answer is different for each stage.

| Stage, after the delegation | Working days | Can an AI tool change it? |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Request waits for a commercial rule decision | 0 for a rule type already in Priya’s **catalogue**, the list of rule types product has already approved; up to 3 for a new rule type | No. A wait for a decision is a question of who is authorized; a tool that drafts the decision faster does not change who must make it |
| Change waits for one of the two specialists to be free to take it up | 7 | Not by faster coding alone. The queue exists because only two people can change the module safely, and it is filled by their incidents and other work. A tool relieves it only if someone else can now make the change safely, which is the same knowledge-transfer test the billing engineer faces, or if it cuts the specialists’ own effort per change enough that they clear the queue faster, which this calculation does not assume and the pilot’s lead-time measure would show |
| Change built and tested by the specialist | 2 | Yes, and measurably. This is the stage the coding studies measure, and it is two of ten days |
| Invoice sign-off in finance | 0 | Delegated; a tool changes nothing here |
| Release to production | 1 | Marginally; release is already under two days |
| **Request to production** | **10 for a catalogue change; up to 13 with a decision wait** | **A tool that halved building would save one day of ten** |

The ten-day case is the standard one: a change of a rule type already in Priya’s catalogue, with no decision to wait for. If an AI tool halved Larkspur’s building time, that change would arrive one working day sooner out of ten. The seven-day queue is held fixed in that calculation as an assumption, not as a finding about the tool. The seven days are the wait for one of the two specialists to be free, and they could shorten in two ways: if the tool cuts the specialists’ own effort per change enough that they clear the queue faster, or if a third person can change the module safely, which is the transfer test. The pilot measures lead time on the same trace precisely so that either movement shows up. What the calculation does rule out is the easy version of the claim: if the other twelve engineers use a tool to draft changes to the module, the drafts still join the queue, because a draft needs a specialist to check and finish it. That is not an argument against the tool. It is the reason the tool’s claim has to be attached to a stage of the trace before anyone attaches it to a headcount plan.

There is one way a tool touches the knowledge constraint, and it deserves its own line. If the billing engineer, using a tool that explains the module’s code and drafts changes, can release a country-rule change without either specialist reviewing it, and the reconciliation shows no errors, then the transfer has happened. That is a real gain, and it is the gain that would change Larkspur’s staffing arithmetic. Whether the tool made it happen sooner cannot be told apart from the engineer’s own learning and the specialists’ protected transfer day, so the pilot records such a release as evidence about the transfer, not as the tool’s gain. It is tested the way the hiring chapter tests any transfer: by the receiving person doing the work under real conditions, not by a document, a demonstration or a seat.

## Two Pilots, One Method

A **pilot** is a limited trial run to learn whether something works before committing to it. Alex and Priya propose two, because the team the plan is about is not only engineers. Larkspur’s customer setup work, called **onboarding**, has run through this book since Part III, and it is done by implementation specialists who configure each new customer by hand. Both pilots use the same method and differ in what can go wrong.

**Engineering.** Fourteen engineers get the coding tool for ninety days, the billing engineer among them. The measure is pricing-rule changes delivered per month, request to production, against the three months before; lead time per change on the same trace, in elapsed working days; reviewer hours per change, written down by the reviewer; and the monthly reconciliation, unchanged, as the quality check, with each wrong invoice traced to the change that caused it. Quality is reported as faulty changes per hundred delivered, each change followed for thirty days after release, with the count of wrong invoices beside it. The two specialists use the tool for their own changes, but their protected day for knowledge transfer, one day a week each, is not touched by the pilot and is not counted in it.

**Customer setup.** Three implementation specialists get a tool that drafts a customer’s configuration from the signed order and the customer’s answers to a standard questionnaire, for the same ninety days. The measure is setups delivered (live for the customer) per month against the three months before, with a fixed quality check: any correction the customer needs in the thirty days after go-live counts against the setup, as it did before. Here the risk is not a code defect found by a test but a wrong configuration found by a customer, which is why the check is a customer-facing one and why every setup is followed, not a selection, and the result is reported as setups needing a correction per hundred delivered. This example carries its own fictional numbers and is separate from the onboarding figures used elsewhere in the book.

Four rules apply to both pilots, and they are what distinguish a measurement from an impression.

- **The baseline comes from the trace, not from memory.** The three months before the pilot are measured on the same definitions. A team that starts counting only when the tool arrives has no comparison. Reviewer hours were not written down before, so recording starts on the day of the decision; the weeks before day one supply the baseline for review time, and the reviewers’ estimate for the three months before is labelled as an estimate.
- **Every item, the same checks.** Every change goes through the monthly reconciliation and every setup is followed for thirty days, exactly as before; nobody redesigns the check to suit the tool during the pilot. Quality is reported as a rate, faulty items per hundred delivered items, each item followed for the same thirty days, because a pilot that doubles delivered setups can double the faulty ones while the rate is unchanged: ten setups with one needing a correction and twenty with two are the same quality, and twenty with three is worse even though more sound setups went live. Quality holds when the pilot’s rate is no higher than the baseline’s.
- **Review time is recorded by the reviewer.** The person who reads generated work writes down the hours it took. What enters the cost comparison is the difference: reviewer hours per change during the pilot minus reviewer hours per change before it. If building a change falls from sixteen labour-hours to eight and review stays at two, the saving is eight hours, not six; if review rises to four, it is six. Elapsed lead time is reported separately, in working days, and already contains the review and every wait, so nothing is subtracted from it. If review hours rise by as much as building hours fall, review has absorbed the saving, and the reviewers’ records are what show it. At Larkspur the reviewers are the two specialists, so the added review loads the constraint that already exists. It does not show that the constraint has moved; only a stage-by-stage trace of the pilot’s items could show that.
- **What else changed is written down.** Alongside the before and after figures, Alex and Priya record the mix of rule types and customer sizes, the number of requests that came in, who was on the team and any other change to the way the work is done. Where practical the comparison is between matched tasks, catalogue rule types that occur in both periods. Where it is not, the result is labelled as uncertain in what caused it, because easier work, more requests or the billing engineer’s own learning would raise completed work with no help from the tool.

The pilot is also a **data and security decision**, not only a productivity one. Both tools are run by outside vendors, so code, customer orders and configuration answers leave the company when a tool processes them. Before the first day, Alex and Sam record what data the tools receive, where it is processed, whether it is retained or used to train the vendor’s models, and what the contract says; the generative AI profile published by NIST (the National Institute of Standards and Technology, a United States government standards agency) supplies the questions to ask, as a structure rather than a certificate. [S18: NIST generative AI profile](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ai/NIST.AI.600-1.pdf) The review takes time and, for the customer-data tool, outside advice, and both belong in the cost.

## What the Studies Do and Do Not Show

Morgan’s note quotes a number. The published evidence is worth reading before the number is believed, and the chapter [[ai-strategy-three-questions]] carries the full evidence box; the short version follows, with dates and limits, because the dates and limits are the point.

A **controlled experiment** compares outcomes under different conditions. One, run in 2022 and published in 2023, gave developers a bounded task, a small program that answers requests over the web, and found that those using an AI code assistant took 55.8% less time on average among those who finished. That is a claim about that task. [S19: Peng et al., Copilot experiment](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.06590) Another, a **randomized study** run in early 2025 by METR (Model Evaluation & Threat Research) with experienced developers on their own open-source projects (software whose code anyone may inspect, modify and share under its licence), assigned each task at random to allow or forbid AI and found tasks took 19% longer when AI was allowed: a different population, setting and tool period. [S20: METR early-2025 experiment](https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/) In February 2026 METR reported that its follow-up had selection and measurement problems serious enough that it called the newer data a weak guide to the size of current effects. [S21: METR 2026 update](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/)

None of these studies measures Larkspur’s work, its tools or its people, so none of them can stand in for the pilot. The studies that found gains measured building, which at Larkspur is two days in ten. And the studies disagree with each other by more than any of them differs from zero, which is what a leader should expect from a technology whose effect depends on the task, the person, the tool version and the definition of done. A pilot is not a formality that confirms what the literature already knows. It is the only evidence about this company that will exist.

## Count the Cost Before Payback

**Payback** is the point at which a spend has been repaid by what it saved or earned, and it cannot be dated until the full cost is known. The hiring chapter insisted that “no new hire” is not the same as “no cost”. A tool pilot is where that distinction is most often lost, because the visible cost is a licence and the real cost is people’s time. Sam and Alex cost the two pilots on one basis, separating **cash**, money that actually leaves the company, from **employees’ time**, which is already paid for but is taken from other work, and one-off costs (paid once) from recurring ones (paid for as long as the activity continues). The measured period is ninety days, but the tools stay on and the decision falls on day 120, for reasons the decision record below explains, so the licences run for four months. Every figure is fictional, and the time estimates use Larkspur’s own assumed staff costs.

| Item | Cash or time | One-off or recurring | Amount |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Coding tool, 14 seats | Cash | Recurring | About €700 a month; €2,800 for four months |
| Setup-drafting tool, 3 seats plus usage charges | Cash | Recurring | About €900 a month at expected volume, seats included; usage charges rise with volume, and the contract caps the tool’s whole monthly bill at €1,500; €3,600 expected for four months, €6,000 at most |
| Legal review of both contracts and the data terms | Cash | One-off | About €4,000 |
| Data and security review | Time | One-off | Five days of Alex and two of Sam; a cost-based estimate of about €4,500, not cash |
| Training and setup | Time | One-off | Two days for each of 17 people; a cost-based estimate of about €13,000, not cash |
| Review of generated work | Time | Recurring | Unknown until measured; recorded by reviewers during the pilot and reported as the change in hours per item against the baseline |
| Priya’s and Alex’s pilot management | Time | Recurring for the pilot | About half a day a week each during the ninety measured days, lighter during the thirty days of observation, plus the gate itself; a cost-based estimate of about €6,000 |
| **Pilot, cash: expected** | | | **About €10,400 over four months (€2,800 + €3,600 + €4,000)** |
| **Pilot, cash: authorized ceiling** | | | **€12,800 (€2,800 + €6,000 + €4,000), reached only if the setup tool’s bill hits its cap in every month; it cannot be exceeded without a new authorization** |
| **Pilot, employees’ time** | | | **About €23,500 as a cost-based estimate (€4,500 + €13,000 + €6,000), before review time** |

The cost-based estimates are labelled as such because they are not money that leaves the company; they are the price of the work that does not get done instead, and that work has to be named. At Larkspur the training days come out of the two weeks of lower-priority maintenance work already scheduled for the quarter, not out of the specialists’ protected transfer day and not out of the committed customer dates.

The recurring line that matters most is the one without a number: review. If the pilot shows that generated changes take the reviewers as long to read as the changes took to build, the tool has a recurring cost that the licence never showed. The pilot therefore reports three results and keeps each in its own unit: the throughput gain as a percentage, the elapsed lead time in working days, and the cost as euros of tool charges beside the change in reviewer hours. None is subtracted from another, because a percentage minus euros means nothing, and because the throughput count already contains whatever review the work needed. Suppose the engineering pilot delivers 20% more changes a month with reviewer hours unchanged and the coding tool costing about €700 a month. The result is reported as it is: 20% more delivered changes, €700 a month, no extra review hours. To put a euro value on the gain, Sam would have to state a period and an assumed cost per staff-hour, value the building hours freed over that period, and label the result an estimate; only then can it stand beside the €700 in the same unit. The chapter [[ai-strategy-three-questions]] made the same point for internal work in general: saved time is capacity, not cash, and it becomes a saving only through a further operating change, such as work that would otherwise have been bought in or hired for being done by the existing team.

## “AI Means Fewer People”: Test the Claim

Morgan’s second suggestion is the one with consequences: that the deferred second team may no longer be needed. The chapter [[anatomy-of-a-layoff]] separates three pressures that sound alike when someone says “cut”, and the same three have to be told apart when someone says “you will not need to hire”.

**A funding condition.** A **term sheet** is the short document in which an investor sets out the main terms it proposes for an investment. It is usually not binding on the investment itself: the money is committed only when the funding documents are signed, and it arrives later still. If an investor proposed to attach the next round to a headcount cap or a productivity target, that would be a proposal to negotiate, and Larkspur could accept it, offer different terms or decline the money. If the signed documents contained it, it would be a condition with a date, and Larkspur would meet it or do without that money. Neither is the case. Larkspur’s term sheet contains no such proposal, and nothing in the signed agreements says anything about AI or about the size of the engineering team.

**A director’s vote.** The second team was deferred until the board approves the roles, so the board’s view of whether the team is needed carries authority when that approval is sought. The investor-appointed director, the board member the investor has the right to appoint, can vote against the roles, and can say why. That is a decision the company has agreed the board will make. It is not a decision the board has made yet, and it is not made by an adviser’s note.

**A benchmark.** Morgan’s note is influence: real, worth hearing, and not authority. Its number comes from other companies whose work, tools and definitions of done Larkspur does not know. The chapter [[investors-adviser]] sets out what an adviser is asked to do; an adviser’s comparison is answered with the company’s own evidence, and the pilot is where that evidence comes from.

The second team was not deferred on capacity alone. It was deferred on three conditions, demand, committed money and board approval, and a tool changes none of them. What a tool could change, if the pilot shows a gain, is the **size and shape of the team when the conditions are met**: fewer than five roles, or different ones, or the same five doing more. What the arithmetic above does not credit to a tool is the seven-day queue, which is held fixed in the calculation. If the pilot’s lead-time measure shows it shortening, the cause has to be named: the specialists clearing it faster, which is a measured gain for the tool, or the transfer test passing, in which case the gain is the billing engineer’s independence and not the tool’s throughput.

There is a further reason not to let the claim run ahead of the pilot, and the layoff chapter states it: announcing a staffing consequence before the mechanism is demonstrated removes the people needed to evaluate the output. If Larkspur told its engineers that AI would shrink the team, the people best placed to report honestly that review time had doubled would be the people with the strongest reason not to.

Ines writes back to Morgan the same week. The tools will be piloted, with a date, measures and a gate. The second team’s conditions stand. If the pilot shows a gain in completed work, the roles will be resized when the conditions are met, and the board will see the pilot’s evidence when it decides. Morgan, who has been asked for a benchmark from a portfolio company that measured completed work rather than seats, sends one, and it turns out to be smaller and more useful than the number in the first note. The pilots start about four weeks later, once the data review is done. Counted from the day the billing engineer moved into the module, the timeline runs:

- day 0: the hiring decision, and the engineer moves in;
- day 90: Morgan’s note;
- about day 118: the pilots’ first day, about four months in;
- about day 177: the pilots’ day-60 reconciliation, which completes the thirty-day invoice follow-up of anything released in the pilots’ first month;
- about day 182: the hiring chapter’s six-month deadline. If the engineer cannot yet release a rule change independently, the transfer has failed and the role is reassessed.

The six-month deadline therefore falls inside the pilot period, about pilot day 65, early in its third month and just after the day-60 reconciliation.

She alone authorizes the one sixty-day extension, any month added after a quality breach, any restart and the release of a held gate result, from the operating budget, each recorded as an amendment to this decision. Seats beyond the pilot’s seventeen are a new recurring commitment, decided only after the gate: by Ines within the operating budget, by the board above it (see the chapter [[decide-who-decides]]). Alex is accountable for the engineering pilot and the data review, Priya for the customer-setup pilot. The decision about the second team is unchanged and remains the board’s, on its three conditions |
| Gate | Day 120, by the numbered rules below. The sample is every change that reaches production, and every setup that goes live, on or before day 90 |
| Evidence that would reopen the decision | A data or contract finding in the review that cannot be resolved before day one stops that pilot before it starts. At any month end while a tool is in use, an error rate above the baseline in a month of tool use not yet investigated switches the tool off for that work for the following month (the safety stop below). Nothing restarts by default |

**The schedule.** The measured period is days 1 to 90. Items count by completion date, the rule the baseline uses too, so both periods carry some work begun before them. Work still open on day 90 is listed as carried over, with its start date, and is neither counted nor dropped.

The decision waits until day 120 so that every item in the sample has had thirty days of live evidence. For a setup, that is the customer’s thirty-day correction window. For a change, it is the reconciliation of every invoice issued in the thirty days after its release, which finance runs at each month end on the pilot’s clock (days 30, 60, 90 and 120). The tools stay on for the same seats and the same work through day 120, with no new seats, and the same checks continue. Items delivered in days 91 to 120 are reported beside the gate as interim evidence, outside the sample.

| Phase | Days | Cash for the phase |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Measured months, tools on | 1 to 90 | In the €10,400 expected, €12,800 ceiling |
| Observation, tools on, no new seats | 91 to 120 | In the €10,400 |
| Decision | 120 | The gate itself, in the management estimate |

| Extension decision | 210 | About the same management time as the first pilot, through this day |

A month in which a tool is switched off after a quality breach moves every later date back thirty days. The supporting note at the end of this section gives the shifted dates and their cost.

**The gate rules.** The rules are complete on purpose, so that the result cannot be argued into whatever the room wants on the day. Each pilot is judged separately, on its three measured months of tool use against the three months before, using four numbers:

- delivered items per month;
- the error rate, in faulty items per hundred delivered items, now complete for every item in the sample;
- the **median** elapsed lead time per item, in working days (the middle value when every item’s lead time is sorted, so that one very slow item does not set the result);
- reviewer hours per item.

The **gain** is the change in delivered items per month as a percentage of the baseline: the pilot’s monthly average minus the baseline’s, divided by the baseline’s, times one hundred. From a baseline of ten changes a month, twelve is a gain of 20%, eleven is 10%, ten is 0% and nine is −10%. A baseline of zero, which neither Larkspur pilot has, would make that division meaningless; a pilot on such work would need a comparison agreed separately before day one, such as matched tasks or an absolute count. The rules are read in order, and the first that applies decides.

1. **Evidence missing.** If a month’s reconciliation, a setup’s thirty-day follow-up or the reviewers’ hours are missing, the pilot is not judged. The tool is withdrawn from that work, no gain is reported, and the pilot may be proposed again only with the record complete.
2. **Quality worse.** A completed error rate for the sample above the baseline rate withdraws the tool from that work regardless of throughput, and no throughput gain is reported as capacity.
3. **Lead time worse.** Quality holding but the median lead time longer than the baseline withdraws the tool from that work, whatever the gain. The record states what was measured: delivery became slower. It names a moved constraint, and review as its place, only if a stage-by-stage trace of the pilot’s items and the reviewers’ recorded hours show where the time went, because a specialist’s absence, a change in demand or an unrelated queue can slow delivery too.
4. **Gain of 20% or more, quality and lead time holding.** The tool is eligible to continue for that work, subject to the affordability condition below. If it continues, seats may be extended to the same kind of work, and three figures go into the staffing arithmetic for the board’s decision on the second team, each in its own unit: the gain as a percentage, the tool charges in euros, and the change in reviewer hours. Nothing is subtracted from the percentage.
5. **Gain above 0% and below 20%, quality and lead time holding.** One sixty-day extension for that work (days 121 to 180, then thirty days of observation, decided on day 210), with no additional seats and with the measure narrowed to the task types where the gain appeared. The extension is judged on its own two measured months against the same task types in the three months before the pilot; the supporting note gives the calculation. On day 210, a gain of 20% or more on the narrowed measure continues as in rule 4, subject to the same affordability condition; anything less, or any failure of rules 1 to 3, withdraws the tool. There is no second extension.
6. **Gain of 0% or less.** The tool is withdrawn from that work, and what was learned about review time and the transfer is recorded for the next proposal.

Read against a gain of −5%, 0%, 10%, 20% or 25%, with quality passing or failing and lead time better or worse, every combination lands on exactly one rule: −5% and 0% on rule 6, 10% on rule 5, 20% and 25% on rule 4, and any failure of quality or lead time on rule 2 or 3 before the gain is read.

**The affordability condition.** A gain that meets rule 4 is technical eligibility, not automatic continuation, because the gate’s fourth number can rise without failing any rule. On the gate day Ines takes the change in reviewer hours per month (pilot minus baseline) and decides whether the company can fund it: she names the work those hours displace, which may not be the specialists’ transfer day or committed customer dates, and the recurring tool charges come from the operating budget. If she cannot name that work, the result is recorded as a gain the company cannot afford to review, and the tool is withdrawn from that work. Worked through: ten changes a month become twelve, quality and lead time hold, and review rises from two hours an item to ten. The gain is 20% and rule 4 applies, but review has gone from twenty to 120 reviewer-hours a month, an extra hundred hours, more than half of one specialist’s working month. If those hours are the two specialists’, they displace the transfer day and the queue, which Ines cannot authorize, and the tool is withdrawn; if other engineers can review those changes to the same standard, she names the work they leave undone and continues.

**The safety stop.** Quality evidence arrives late, so it is checked while the tools are in use, not only at the gate. At every month end while a tool is in use, including the observation month and the extension, Alex for engineering and Priya for customer setup read the error rate so far of each month of tool use not yet investigated. If one is above the baseline, the tool is switched off for that work for the following month. The rules make that pause automatic: Alex or Priya apply it and tell Ines the same day. The work continues without the tool and is checked the same way, and at the end of that month Ines decides in writing whether the tool returns to that work or is withdrawn.

A breach in an observation month, the pilot’s or the extension’s, is outside the sample, so it never changes the gain; it holds the gate result instead. The gate is still read and recorded, but nothing it grants takes effect until Ines has read a tool-free month (days 121 to 150 after the pilot’s gate) and decided in writing at its end. The same check continues for as long as a tool stays in use after the gate.

**The transfer test.** If the billing engineer releases a country-rule change without either specialist reviewing it and the monthly reconciliation shows no errors, that is recorded as evidence about the transfer, whichever rule decides the throughput. It shows that the engineer can do the work; it does not by itself show that the tool made that happen sooner.

### Supporting Note: Safety Stops, Held Results and the Extension

*This note is for whoever runs the pilots. The decision above stands without it; the note sets out exactly how late evidence, pauses and the extension are counted.*

**Provisional and completed rates.** A setup delivered on day 29 can need a correction on day 55, so the month-end check groups items by the month in which they were delivered and keeps two readings apart.

- A delivery month’s **completed** rate exists once every item in it has had its thirty days, at the end of the following month: deliveries in days 1 to 30 are complete on day 60, days 31 to 60 on day 90, days 61 to 90 on day 120.
- Until then its rate is **provisional**: faulty items found so far, divided by every item delivered that month. It can only rise as the follow-up finishes, never fall, so a provisional rate already above the baseline is a breach, not a forecast.

A breach is a provisional or completed rate above the baseline in a month of tool use not yet investigated. An error found in the middle of a month is read at that month end, and nothing is switched off retrospectively. On a gate day the sample’s own months are judged as a whole by rule 2, and the check reads only the months outside the sample.

**The steps after a breach.**

1. The tool is switched off for that work for the following month. The work continues without it and is checked the same way.
2. Every error is attributed to the change or setup that caused it and to whether the tool was used on that item, because an error found in the tool-free month can come from earlier tool-assisted work. Alex or Priya compare the task mix and any other change with the months before. A difference of one or two errors in a small month may reflect chance and is investigated, not read as a verdict.
3. Deliveries in the tool-free month go into a diagnostic record, not into the sample, marked by whether the tool was used on them. Work finished after a restart counts in the month of completion, whether it was begun before the pause or during it.
4. At the end of the tool-free month Ines decides in writing, on that record, whether the pilot for that work ends or restarts.
5. If it restarts, the sample keeps its number of measured months of tool use, three for a pilot and two for an extension, and the monthly average divides by that number. Every later date moves back thirty days, and the added month’s licences and management time are funded as an amendment.

**A restart closes the breach it answers.** Ines’s written restart decision names the delivery months she investigated and their completed rates. Every month of tool use before the pause is complete by the day she decides, so those rates can no longer change. They stay in the final sample, faulty items included, but they do not trigger the steps again. After a restart, only a month of tool use delivered after it can start another pause. A further pause follows the same steps and moves every later date back another thirty days.

**Shifted dates.** One tool-free month moves the schedule as follows.

| Phase | Normal schedule | After one tool-free month in the pilot |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Measured months, tool on | Days 1 to 90 | Three tool months, for example days 1 to 30 and 61 to 120 |
| Tool-free diagnostic month | None | For example days 31 to 60; deliveries recorded outside the sample |
| Observation, tools on | Days 91 to 120 | Days 121 to 150 |
| Decision | Day 120 | Day 150 |
| Extension measured months (rule 5 only) | Days 121 to 180 | Days 151 to 210 |
| Extension observation | Days 181 to 210 | Days 211 to 240 |
| Extension decision | Day 210 | Day 240 |

The added month costs another month of licences, about €700 for the coding tool and about €900 for the setup tool (€1,500 at its cap), plus management time. A held gate result shifts the extension the same way: its tool-free month is days 121 to 150, and an extension it releases runs on the right-hand dates.

**Worked case: a breach in the first month.** The baseline is 10%, and the first month delivers ten setups, two of them faulty, 20%. On day 30 that provisional rate is already above the baseline, so the setup tool is off for days 31 to 60. A setup delivered on day 45 goes into the diagnostic record, not the sample. On day 60 the first month is complete, still at 20%, and Ines authorizes a restart, recording that month as investigated. A setup begun with the tool before the pause and delivered on day 70 counts in the second tool month (days 61 to 90); one delivered on day 120 counts in the third (days 91 to 120). On days 90 and 120 the checks read only the months delivered from day 61, so the first month’s unchanged 20% does not pause the pilot again. The sample is every setup delivered in days 1 to 30, 61 to 90 and 91 to 120, the monthly average divides by three, the observation month is days 121 to 150 and the decision falls on day 150. The two faulty setups still count at the gate: if the other two months deliver ten setups each without an error, the sample’s rate is 2 in 30, about 6.7%, within the baseline.

**Worked case: late errors.** The setup delivered on day 29 and corrected on day 55 belongs to the first delivery month. It adds one to that month’s faulty setups; the denominator stays every setup delivered in days 1 to 30; and that month’s rate is complete on day 60. If the correction takes the month above the baseline, the tool is off for days 61 to 90. The tool months become days 1 to 30, 31 to 60 and 91 to 120, and the decision falls on day 150: the pause reaches forward, and days 31 to 60 stay in the sample. A correction found during a pause changes only the timing. If the tool is already off for days 31 to 60 and on day 45 a customer needs a correction to a setup delivered on day 20, the correction joins the first delivery month’s count, not the diagnostic record. Ines reads it on day 60 as part of her restart decision; it does not start a second pause or lengthen the current one.

**Worked case: a breach in the observation month.** The setup pilot’s sample passes every rule with a gain of 20%, so the gate lands on rule 4. The observation month, days 91 to 120, delivers ten setups, and by day 120 two of them already need a correction: a provisional rate of 20% against a baseline of 10%. The check on day 120 comes before the gate, and it is a breach. The gate result is recorded as rule 4 but does not take effect. The ten setups stay out of the gain; the tool is off for setups for days 121 to 150, and the two errors are traced as in step 2. On day 150 Ines decides in writing, on that record, and records the observation month as investigated. Either the tool is withdrawn, or the rule-4 result takes effect, and she applies the affordability condition that day. Had the gate landed on rule 5, a released result would start the extension on day 151, on the shifted dates above.

**The extension’s calculation.** Rule 5’s extension is a smaller pilot with its own count, not a continuation of the first.

- **Measure.** The task types where the gain appeared, named in Ines’s written extension amendment on the gate day.
- **Sample.** Every item of those task types delivered in the extension’s two measured months, days 121 to 180 in the normal schedule. Deliveries from the first pilot, from its observation month and of other task types are excluded.
- **Divisor.** The monthly average divides by two, the number of measured months.
- **Baseline.** The same three months before the pilot, counting only deliveries of the same task types, divided by three. Quality, lead time and reviewer hours are compared with the same narrowed baseline.
- **Example.** The narrowed task types had thirty deliveries in the baseline months, ten a month. The extension delivers 24 of them in its two measured months, twelve a month. The gain is (12 − 10) ÷ 10 × 100 = 20%.
- **Pauses.** A breach during the extension follows the same steps. The sample stays two measured months of tool use, the average still divides by two, and every later date moves back thirty days.
- **A thin baseline.** A task type with no deliveries in the baseline months cannot show a gain against them and is not part of the narrowed measure. If no task type remains, the extension is not granted and the tool is withdrawn from that work. If the narrowed baseline is small, a few deliveries in three months, the amendment says so, and the result carries that label beside its percentage, as the fourth pilot rule labels uncertain causes.

## When the Pilot Shifts the Constraint

Three outcomes are plausible at Larkspur, and each is a result rather than a failure of the method.

**Output rises and the error rate rises with it.** More changes reach production, and a larger share of them cause wrong invoices within their thirty days, not merely more errors because there are more changes: three faulty changes in twenty is worse than one in ten, even though more sound changes went live as well. That fails the quality gate, rule 2, and the tool is withdrawn whatever the throughput, because a rate above the baseline means the work has moved to whoever finds the errors. Whether the count of sound changes rose is not the question the gate asks. What is recorded is the finding, not a diagnosis: each error is traced to the change that caused it, and whether the errors came from review that could not keep up, from requirements that were wrong or from tests that did not catch them is investigated before anyone names review as the constraint. Only if the trace shows sound requirements and tests, and errors concentrated in changes the reviewers had less time for, does the record say that for this module the review is the constraint.

**Building falls and review rises.** Changes are built in a day instead of two, and the reviewers’ recorded hours show that reading generated changes takes longer than reading written ones, because the generated change is plausible in places where it is wrong. Lead time is unchanged. Review has absorbed the day that building saved. The extra hours fall on the two specialists, whose queue was already the constraint, so the tool has added pressure to the existing constraint rather than created a new one. Suppose the seven-day queue stays at seven while one saved building day becomes one more review day: that is the same bottleneck, working harder. Only a stage-by-stage trace showing, for example, the queue shortening while finished changes wait longer for review would show that the constraint had moved. At the gate the numbered rules come first. A gain of 0% or less withdraws the tool under rule 6, and a gain above 0% but below 20% earns only rule 5’s single extension, however easily the review could be funded. Only a gain of 20% or more, with quality and lead time holding, reaches the affordability condition: the tool continues only if Ines can fund the added review hours from work she names, and the two specialists’ hours are the ones the plan cannot give. That is the outcome Morgan’s number could never have shown, and it is the reason review time is measured by the reviewer.

**The transfer shows.** The billing engineer, with a tool that explains the module’s history and drafts changes, releases a country-rule change alone in the pilot’s first month, about four months after moving into the module. The pilot’s day-60 reconciliation completes that change’s thirty days of invoice checks, so the evidence is in hand by the six-month deadline at which the hiring chapter would otherwise reassess the role. Throughput barely moves, because the queue was never about typing, but the knowledge constraint has loosened. Whether the tool shortened the transfer cannot be told apart from the engineer’s own learning and the specialists’ protected day, so it is recorded as transfer evidence and not as the tool’s gain. It is still the result that changes Larkspur’s staffing arithmetic, and it changes it by making the third specialist real before that deadline, not by making engineers unnecessary.

The evidence that would change the chapter’s judgment is the same in each case: completed work with the old checks, over a period long enough to see the review cost, on this company’s work. A vendor’s study, a portfolio benchmark and a leader’s impression of feeling faster are all reasons to run the pilot. None of them is its result.

With the pilots under way and the second team’s conditions unchanged, Larkspur still has the system constraint the hiring chapter left open: country rules coupled into the invoicing module, so that serving a new country means changing the billing code. A tool that drafts changes to that code does not decide whether the code should change at all. The chapter [[growth-into-design]] compares three ways to meet the second country’s need, configuring a supplier’s product, building and keeping the country rules yourself, or replacing the system underneath, against the same customer need, date and cash limit, and chooses one. When a pilot result becomes an argument for cutting rather than for hiring differently, the chapter [[anatomy-of-a-layoff]] is where that argument is costed by date.

## Questions to Consider

1. *Take the trace you made for the hiring chapter. For each waiting stage, can a tool touch it, and if so, what would the completed-work measure be?*
2. *If a tool pilot were proposed in your company tomorrow, what baseline would you compare it against, and does that baseline exist today?*
3. *Who reads generated work in your team, is their time recorded, and are they the people the plan can least afford to load?*
4. *When someone says AI means fewer people, which of the three is it: a condition attached to money, a vote someone holds, or a benchmark someone quoted? What response does each one require, and what would your gate rule say on the day the pilot ends?*
