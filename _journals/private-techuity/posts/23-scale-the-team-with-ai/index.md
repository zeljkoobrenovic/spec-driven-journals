> **IN THIS SECTION, YOU WILL:** Learn to answer a claim that artificial intelligence changes what your existing team can complete, whether it arrives as a reason to buy tools, to hire fewer people or to cut: trace the work, find which constraint a tool can touch, measure completed work rather than activity, count the cost before payback, and record a decision with a dated gate.

> **WHY INVESTORS CARE:** An investor sees two levers it can fund, people and tools, and hears from every direction that the second now replaces the first. It wants to know whether money spent on AI buys work that completes, or buys seats that sit behind the same queue as the last hire.

> **WHY YOU SHOULD CARE:** “AI means we need fewer engineers” is a capacity claim, and a capacity claim is tested the same way whether it argues for a hire or against one. If you cannot say which constraint a tool touches and what completed work changed, someone else will decide your headcount from a vendor’s slide.


> **KEY POINTS:**
>
> * **A tool can only relieve a constraint it touches.** Decision waits are authority and no tool removes them; a knowledge queue moves only if the tool lets someone else do the work safely, which is a knowledge-transfer test; building and release can change, and can be measured.
> * **Capacity is work that completes with the old quality checks.** Lines written, requests answered, seats active and tokens used are activity. Measure completed changes and completed customer setups per period, checked the same way as before the tool, and count review time as cost.
> * **“Fewer people” is a claim to test, not a plan to execute.** Separate a funding condition, a director’s vote and an adviser’s benchmark, answer each with the response it requires, and leave the deferred hire’s conditions unchanged until a dated gate is passed.

<br>
Outside investment and artificial intelligence make the same promise to a team: more capacity, faster than the company could grow it alone. The chapter [[fix-decisions-before-hiring]] showed that money for people buys capacity only when the queue is made of missing people rather than of unclear authority and concentrated knowledge. **Artificial intelligence (AI)** is software that predicts or generates content from data, and money for AI tools is subject to the same test. The difference is that the AI claim now runs in both directions at once: a reason to buy tools, and a reason to hire fewer people or to let some go.

This chapter continues the scenario of the hiring chapter, with the same people and the same figures, all fictional. Larkspur sells scheduling software. Alex leads technology as chief technology officer (CTO), Priya leads product, Ines is the chief executive who leads the company, Sam leads finance, and Morgan is the technology adviser to Larkspur’s investor. A traced pricing change took sixteen working days, thirteen of them waiting. Two approval waits were removed by delegation, forecast to bring the **lead time**, the time from a customer’s request to the change running in **production** (the live system customers use), down to about ten working days, seven of which still wait for the two specialists who alone can safely change the **invoicing module**, the part of the software that prepares customer invoices. One billing engineer was funded to become the third such person, and a five-person second team was deferred until three conditions are met: second-country demand is evidenced, the next funding round is committed, and the board has approved the roles. The shared Larkspur ledger that runs through the chapters [[first-hundred-days]] and [[handover-of-obligations]] is not touched here.

Ninety days later, Morgan sends Ines a note. Portfolio companies, the other companies the investor has money in, are reporting large gains from AI coding tools. Larkspur should adopt them across engineering, and the deferred second team, Morgan suggests, may no longer be needed at all. The note is well meant and partly right. Whether it is right about Larkspur is what the rest of this chapter finds out.

## Capacity Is Work That Completes

The hiring chapter defined **capacity** as the work a team can complete, as distinct from **headcount**, the number of people it employs. The definition does not change because the proposed addition is software rather than a person. What changes is the temptation to measure something easier.

AI tools produce activity that is easy to count and pleasant to report: lines of code suggested and accepted, draft answers generated, seats active, **tokens** (the units in which AI services meter what they read and write) consumed. None of these is capacity. A change is capacity when it runs in production and the invoice it affects reconciles; a customer setup is capacity when the customer is using the software and no one has had to correct the configuration afterwards. The table separates the two.

| Counts as evidence of capacity | Does not count |
| --- | --- |
| Pricing-rule changes completed per month, request to production, with the monthly invoice reconciliation showing no new errors | Lines of code written or accepted from suggestions |
| Customer setups completed per month with no correction needed in the following thirty days | Draft configurations produced |
| Lead time per change, measured on the same trace as before | Time a developer reports feeling faster |
| Review time per change, counted as cost against the gain | Seats licensed, seats active, tokens consumed |
| The same quality checks as before the tool, drawn on fresh samples each month | A quality check redesigned after the tool arrived |

Two points in that table carry the weight. The **quality checks are the old ones**, because a tool that raises the number of changes while lowering the share that reconcile has not added capacity; it has moved the work to whoever finds the errors. And **review time is a cost**, because generated work has to be read by someone who understands it, and at Larkspur the people who understand the invoicing module are the two the plan is trying to protect.

## Which Constraint Can a Tool Touch?

The hiring chapter’s trace sorted the sixteen days into three constraints: a **decision constraint** (waits that exist because authority sits with one person), a **knowledge constraint** (a queue that exists because only two people can safely change the module) and a **staffing constraint** (how much capacity is actually missing once the first two are addressed). Morgan’s note, like most AI proposals, does not say which of the three the tool would relieve. Run it against the trace and the answer is different for each stage.

| Stage, after the delegation | Working days | Can an AI tool change it? |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Request waits for a commercial rule outside Priya’s catalogue | 0 to 3 | No. A wait for a decision is a question of who is authorized; a tool that drafts the decision faster does not change who must make it |
| Change waits in the two specialists’ review queue | 7 | Not directly. The queue exists because only two people can change the module safely. A tool relieves it only if someone else can now make the change safely, which is the same knowledge-transfer test the billing engineer faces, not a licence |
| Change built and tested | 2 | Yes, and measurably. This is the stage the coding studies measure, and it is two of ten days |
| Invoice sign-off in finance | 0 | Delegated; a tool changes nothing here |
| Commit to production | 1 | Marginally; release is already under two days |
| **Request to production** | **about 10** | **A tool that halved building would save one day of ten** |

The arithmetic is the point. If an AI tool halved Larkspur’s building time, the change would arrive one working day sooner out of ten, and the seven-day queue would be untouched. Worse, faster building feeds the queue: more finished changes waiting for the same two reviewers. That is not an argument against the tool. It is the reason the tool’s claim has to be attached to a stage of the trace before anyone attaches it to a headcount plan.

Two teal tiles are bracketed as build and test, 2 days; one of them carries a chip and the note that a tool can move this day. One lighter tile is release, 1 day. Beneath: halving the build saves one day of ten.](assets/images/23-scale-the-team-with-ai/one-day-of-ten.jpeg)
**Figure 1:** *The forecast trace after the delegation, laid out day by day. Morgan’s tools reach the two building days; the seven-day queue exists because only two people can change the invoicing module safely, and a tool relieves it only if it passes the same transfer test as the billing engineer. Halving the building stage would bring a change forward by one day in ten, and would feed the queue faster.*

There is one way a tool touches the knowledge constraint, and it deserves its own line. If the billing engineer, using a tool that explains the module’s code and drafts changes, can release a country-rule change without either specialist reviewing it, and the reconciliation shows no errors, then the tool has shortened the transfer. That is a real gain, and it is the gain that would change Larkspur’s staffing arithmetic. It is tested the way the hiring chapter tests any transfer: by the receiving person doing the work under real conditions, not by a document, a demonstration or a seat.

## Two Pilots, One Method

A **pilot** is a limited trial run to learn whether something works before committing to it. Alex and Priya propose two, because the team the plan is about is not only engineers. Larkspur’s customer setup work, called **onboarding**, has run through this book since Part III, and it is done by implementation specialists who configure each new customer by hand. Both pilots use the same method and differ in what can go wrong.

**Engineering.** Fourteen engineers get the coding tool for ninety days, the billing engineer among them. The measure is pricing-rule changes completed per month, request to production, against the three months before; lead time per change on the same trace; review time per change recorded by the reviewer; and the monthly reconciliation, unchanged, as the quality check. The two specialists use the tool for their own changes but their protected day for knowledge transfer is not touched by the pilot and is not counted in it.

**Customer setup.** Three implementation specialists get a tool that drafts a customer’s configuration from the signed order and the customer’s answers to a standard questionnaire, for the same ninety days. The measure is setups completed per month against the three months before, with a fixed quality check: any correction the customer needs in the thirty days after go-live counts against the setup, as it did before. Here the risk is not a code defect found by a test but a wrong configuration found by a customer, which is why the check is a customer-facing one and why the sample is every setup, not a selection. This example is separate from the onboarding figures in the shared ledger and carries its own fictional numbers.

Three rules apply to both pilots, and they are what distinguish a measurement from an impression.

- **The baseline comes from the trace, not from memory.** The three months before the pilot are measured on the same definitions. A team that starts counting only when the tool arrives has no comparison.
- **Fresh samples, same checks.** Each month’s quality check draws a fresh sample of changes or setups and applies the check that applied before. Nobody redesigns the check to suit the tool during the pilot.
- **Review time is recorded by the reviewer.** The person who reads generated work writes down the time it took. If review time rises faster than building time falls, the tool has moved the constraint rather than relieved it, and the pilot has to show that.

Customer setup pilot: three specialists at a desk with an order form and an AI chip, 3 specialists, 90 days. Both rest on one base of three bars: baseline, the 3 months before; fresh samples, same checks; reviewer records review time, with a stopwatch.](assets/images/23-scale-the-team-with-ai/two-pilots-one-method.jpeg)
**Figure 2:** *Two pilots, one method. The engineering pilot and the customer-setup pilot differ in what can go wrong, a code defect found by a test or a wrong configuration found by a customer, and share the three rules that make the result a measurement: a baseline from the same trace, fresh samples checked the old way each month, and review time written down by the person who reads the generated work.*

The pilot is also a **data and security decision**, not only a productivity one. Code, customer orders and configuration answers leave the company when a tool processes them. Before the first day, Alex and Sam record what data the tools receive, where it is processed, whether it is retained or used to train the vendor’s models, and what the contract says; the NIST profile for generative AI supplies the questions to ask, as a structure rather than a certificate. [S18: NIST generative AI profile](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ai/NIST.AI.600-1.pdf) The review takes time and, for the customer-data tool, outside advice, and both belong in the cost.

## What the Studies Do and Do Not Show

Morgan’s note quotes a number. The published evidence is worth reading before the number is believed, and the chapter [[ai-strategy-three-questions]] carries the full evidence box; the short version follows, with dates and limits, because the dates and limits are the point.

A **controlled experiment** compares outcomes under different conditions. One, run in 2022 and published in 2023, gave developers a bounded task, a small program that answers requests over the web, and found that those using an AI code assistant took 55.8% less time on average among those who finished. That is a claim about that task. [S19: Peng et al., Copilot experiment](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.06590) Another, a **randomized study** run in early 2025 by METR (Model Evaluation & Threat Research) with experienced developers on their own open-source projects, assigned each task at random to allow or forbid AI and found tasks took 19% longer when AI was allowed: a different population, setting and tool period. [S20: METR early-2025 experiment](https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/) In February 2026 METR reported that its follow-up had selection and measurement problems serious enough that it called the newer data a weak guide to the size of current effects. [S21: METR 2026 update](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/)

Three things follow for Larkspur. None of these studies measures Larkspur’s work, its tools or its people, so none of them can stand in for the pilot. The studies that found gains measured building, which at Larkspur is two days in ten. And the studies disagree with each other by more than any of them differs from zero, which is what a leader should expect from a technology whose effect depends on the task, the person, the tool version and the definition of done. A pilot is not a formality that confirms what the literature already knows. It is the only evidence about this company that will exist.

## Count the Cost Before Payback

The hiring chapter insisted that “no new hire” is not the same as “no cost”. A tool pilot is where that distinction is most often lost, because the visible cost is a licence and the real cost is people’s time. Sam and Alex cost the two pilots on one basis, separating **cash**, money that actually leaves the company, from **employees’ time**, which is already paid for but is taken from other work, and one-off costs from recurring ones. Every figure is fictional.

| Item | Cash or time | One-off or recurring | Amount |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Coding tool, 14 seats | Cash | Recurring | About €700 a month; €2,100 for the pilot |
| Setup-drafting tool, 3 seats plus usage charges | Cash | Recurring | About €900 a month at expected volume; usage charges rise with volume and are capped in the contract at €1,500 a month |
| Legal review of both contracts and the data terms | Cash | One-off | About €4,000 |
| Data and security review | Time | One-off | Five days of Alex and two of Sam; a cost-based estimate of about €4,500, not cash |
| Training and setup | Time | One-off | Two days for each of 17 people; a cost-based estimate of about €13,000, not cash |
| Review of generated work | Time | Recurring | Unknown until measured; recorded by reviewers during the pilot and reported as days per month |
| Priya’s and Alex’s pilot management | Time | Recurring for the pilot | About half a day a week each; a cost-based estimate of about €6,000 over ninety days |
| **Pilot, cash** | | | **About €10,000 over ninety days** |
| **Pilot, employees’ time** | | | **About €23,500 as a cost-based estimate, before review time** |

The cost-based estimates are labelled as such because they are not money that leaves the company; they are the price of the work that does not get done instead, and that work has to be named. At Larkspur the training days come out of the two weeks of lower-priority maintenance work already scheduled for the quarter, not out of the specialists’ protected transfer day and not out of the committed customer dates.

The recurring line that matters most is the one without a number: review. If the pilot shows that generated changes take the reviewers as long to read as the changes took to build, the tool has a recurring cost that the licence never showed, and a saving claimed net of licences but gross of review is not a saving. The chapter [[ai-strategy-three-questions]] made the same point for internal work in general: saved time is capacity, not cash, and it becomes a saving only through a further operating change.

## “AI Means Fewer People”: Test the Claim

Morgan’s second suggestion is the one with consequences: that the deferred second team may no longer be needed. The chapter [[anatomy-of-a-layoff]] separates three pressures that sound alike when someone says “cut”, and the same three have to be told apart when someone says “you will not need to hire”. Each requires a different response.

**A funding condition.** If the next funding round’s **term sheet**, the short document that sets out the main terms of a proposed investment, attached the money to a headcount cap or to a productivity target, that would be a condition with a date, and Larkspur would meet it or do without the money. Larkspur’s term sheet contains no such condition. Nothing in the agreements says anything about AI or about the size of the engineering team.

**A director’s vote.** The second team was deferred until the board approves the roles, so the board’s view of whether the team is needed carries authority when that approval is sought. The investor-appointed director can vote against the roles, and can say why. That is a decision the company has agreed the board will make. It is not a decision the board has made yet, and it is not made by an adviser’s note.

**A benchmark.** Morgan’s note is influence: real, worth hearing, and not authority. Its number comes from other companies whose work, tools and definitions of done Larkspur does not know. The chapter [[investors-adviser]] sets out what an adviser is asked to do; an adviser’s comparison is answered with the company’s own evidence, and the pilot is where that evidence comes from.

Answered that way, the claim shrinks to something testable. The second team was not deferred on capacity alone. It was deferred on three conditions, demand, committed money and board approval, and a tool changes none of them. What a tool could change, if the pilot shows a gain, is the **size and shape of the team when the conditions are met**: fewer than five roles, or different ones, or the same five doing more. What a tool cannot change, on the arithmetic above, is the seven-day queue, unless the transfer test passes, in which case the gain is the billing engineer’s independence and not the tool’s throughput.

There is a further reason not to let the claim run ahead of the pilot, and the layoff chapter states it: announcing a staffing consequence before the mechanism is demonstrated removes the people needed to evaluate the output. If Larkspur told its engineers that AI would shrink the team, the people best placed to report honestly that review time had doubled would be the people with the strongest reason not to.

Ines writes back to Morgan the same week. The tools will be piloted, with a date, measures and a gate. The second team’s conditions stand. If the pilot shows a gain in completed work, the roles will be resized when the conditions are met, and the board will see the pilot’s evidence when it decides. Morgan, who has been asked for a benchmark from a portfolio company that measured completed work rather than seats, sends one, and it turns out to be smaller and more useful than the number in the first note.

## The Decision, Recorded

| Element | What was decided |
| --- | --- |
| Chosen | Two ninety-day pilots, engineering and customer setup, with the measures above, a baseline from the three months before, fresh monthly quality samples and reviewer-recorded review time |
| Rejected | Buying seats for everyone now without a baseline, because there would be nothing to compare and the review cost would stay invisible. Refusing the tools, because the building stage is real and measurable and the transfer effect is worth testing. Treating the tools as a reason to cancel the second team, because the team was deferred on demand, money and approval, and a tool changes none of the three |
| Funded now | About €10,000 of cash from the operating budget, the board-approved plan for spending on running the business, within Ines’s delegation; the employees’ time named above, taken from scheduled maintenance work, not from the transfer day or committed customer dates |
| Scarce capacity | The two specialists’ protected day is outside the pilot. The reviewers’ time is inside it and is the measurement that decides the gate |
| Authority | Ines authorizes the pilots and the spending under her delegation; a new recurring commitment above the operating budget would be the board’s (see the chapter [[decide-who-decides]]). Alex is accountable for the engineering pilot and the data review, Priya for the customer-setup pilot. The decision about the second team is unchanged and remains the board’s, on its three conditions |
| Gate | Day ninety, on the measures agreed now, with the rules below |
| Evidence that would reopen the decision | A data or contract finding in the review that cannot be resolved before day one stops that pilot before it starts. A quality check failing in any month stops the tool for that work that month, and the pilot continues only if the following month passes on a fresh sample |

The gate rules are complete on purpose, so that the result cannot be argued into whatever the room wants on day ninety. Each pilot is judged on completed work per month in the pilot period against the baseline, with the quality check as a prerequisite for every branch.

- **Quality first.** If reconciliation errors or thirty-day setup corrections in the pilot period exceed the baseline, the tool is withdrawn from that work regardless of throughput, and any throughput gain is not reported as capacity.
- **Gain of at least a fifth, quality holding.** Completed changes or setups per month at least 20% above the baseline, with review time per item recorded and net of it the lead time not worse: the tool continues for that work, seats may be extended to the same kind of work, and the gain, net of recurring licence and review cost, goes into the staffing arithmetic for the board’s decision on the second team.
- **Between the baseline and a fifth, quality holding.** The pilot is extended by sixty days for that work with no additional seats and with the measure narrowed to the tasks where the gain appeared; the extension ends with the same rule and no further extension.
- **At or below the baseline.** The tool is withdrawn from that work, and what was learned about review time and the transfer is recorded for the next proposal.
- **The transfer test.** Separately from the throughput branches: if the billing engineer releases a country-rule change without either specialist reviewing it and the monthly reconciliation shows no errors, that is recorded as evidence about the transfer, whichever branch the throughput lands in.

## When the Pilot Shifts the Constraint

The honest result of a pilot is often not the one the proposal wanted, and the chapter would be incomplete without saying what that looks like. Three outcomes are plausible at Larkspur, and each is a result rather than a failure of the method.

**Output rises and quality falls.** More changes are built and more of them fail reconciliation. The tool has increased activity and decreased capacity, and the gate withdraws it. The lesson is recorded: for this module, generated changes need the same review as any other, and the review is the constraint.

**Building falls and review rises.** Changes are built in a day instead of two, and the reviewers report that reading generated changes takes longer than reading written ones, because the generated change is plausible in places where it is wrong. Lead time is unchanged; the constraint has moved from building to review, and it has moved onto the two people the plan is protecting. That is the outcome Morgan’s number could never have shown, and it is the reason review time is measured by the reviewer.

**The transfer shortens.** The billing engineer, with a tool that explains the module’s history and drafts changes, releases a country-rule change alone in month two instead of month six. Throughput barely moves, because the queue was never about typing, but the knowledge constraint has loosened. That is the result that changes Larkspur’s staffing arithmetic, and it changes it by making the third specialist real sooner, not by making engineers unnecessary.

The evidence that would change the chapter’s judgment is the same in each case: completed work with the old checks, over a period long enough to see the review cost, on this company’s work. A vendor’s study, a portfolio benchmark and a leader’s impression of feeling faster are all reasons to run the pilot. None of them is its result.

With the pilots under way and the second team’s conditions unchanged, Larkspur still has the system constraint the hiring chapter left open: country rules coupled into the invoicing module, so that serving a new country means changing the billing code. A tool that drafts changes to that code does not decide whether the code should change at all. The chapter [[growth-into-design]] compares three ways to meet the second country’s need, configuring a supplier’s product, building and keeping the country rules yourself, or replacing the system underneath, against the same customer need, date and cash limit, and chooses one. When a pilot result becomes an argument for cutting rather than for hiring differently, the chapter [[anatomy-of-a-layoff]] is where that argument is costed by date.

## Questions to Consider

1. *Take the trace you made for the hiring chapter. For each waiting stage, can a tool touch it, and if so, what would the completed-work measure be?*
2. *If a tool pilot were proposed in your company tomorrow, what baseline would you compare it against, and does that baseline exist today?*
3. *Who reads generated work in your team, is their time recorded, and are they the people the plan can least afford to load?*
4. *When someone says AI means fewer people, which of the three it is: a condition attached to money, a vote someone holds, or a benchmark someone quoted? What response does each one require, and what would your gate rule say on the day the pilot ends?*
