# Editorial review: Raise the Money You Need, Not the Most You Can

Reviewed 14 September 2026. Read the complete article, summary and reader-facing comic text.

## Overall assessment

The chapter argues for matching financing and ownership terms to the company's actual work and uncertainty. It is most useful to leaders advising on a financing decision or trying to renegotiate an inherited plan. Separating owner type, control, financing and transaction context is sound, and treating continued founder ownership as a legitimate choice preserves the book's independent voice.

The major problem is the title's promise. The article compares kinds of arrangements; it does not show how to determine the amount to raise or weigh the benefit of extra funding against dilution and other costs. Much of the comparison also repeats the reading guide and opening chapter, while authority and investor fit are developed more fully in Part II.

## Detailed feedback

- **Introduction and audience:** The retiring-founder versus company-growth distinction is clear. The third paragraph appropriately recognizes that product leaders rarely choose investors, but “the type of capital sets the pace” conflicts with the article's subsequent insistence that actual terms, not categories, determine consequences. The text also moves between a leader who inherits terms and one able to choose a transaction without always signalling the change in agency.
- **Organization and flow:** “Separate the Owner, the Financing and the Situation” is a good framework. “Compare Alternative Larkspur Decisions” mostly repeats its table in prose rather than using the framework to reject one plausible option. The later control and investor-constraints sections preview two other chapters, leaving less space for this one's own decision.
- **Clarity and terminology:** Carve-out, turnaround and acquisition are distinguished well. The financing comparison is carefully conditional. Keep those distinctions, but make them a compact recap with links; a sequential reader has already encountered the categories several times.
- **Examples and pacing:** The four Larkspur sketches give breadth but little development. None includes a proposed funding amount, confidence range, reserve, additional-round assumption or decision outcome. The €4m-versus-€1m interest comparison makes a useful cash consequence explicit, though it is not integrated with a chosen case.
- **Tone and ending:** “A business that decides against outside ownership hasn't failed a test” is a strong statement of authorial judgment. The final work-to-funding question is good; the readiness checklist immediately before it repeats the chapter's opening framework.
- **Evidence:** The practitioner-survey paragraph properly distinguishes reported intentions from delivered results. No numerical error is apparent in the interest comparison. The universal-sounding title is an unsupported prescription as written; uncertainty reserves and the risk of an unavailable future round are counterarguments the article should consider before advocating a smaller raise.
- **Other formats:** The summary and comic accurately reproduce an investor-fit chapter. Neither resolves the title's funding-amount promise, and both repeat the deterministic “type of capital sets” framing.

## Recommended changes

- **High impact — Decide which question this chapter owns.** The economical fix is a title such as “Match the Funding to the Work,” retaining the permalink. If the existing title is important, add an actual sizing decision: base work, transition costs, uncertainty allowance, decision runway and the consequences of raising more or less. Present it as a fictional comparison, not a funding formula.
- **High impact — Give the inherited-arrangement reader an action.** Show Alex and Priya taking a revised scope, cash requirement and alternative to the CEO/CFO. Clarify what they can recommend and who can renegotiate financing. “Choose or renegotiate the arrangement” otherwise overstates their practical authority.
- **Medium impact — Develop one Larkspur case.** Compare two feasible arrangements for the same onboarding problem and explain why one is chosen under the stated assumptions. A rejected plausible alternative teaches more than four category illustrations.
- **Medium impact — Move detailed control and partnership questions to Part II.** Retain one paragraph and links to “Decide Who Decides” and “Your Investor Under Pressure.” Use the freed space for the funding comparison.
- **Low impact — Replace category determinism.** “The terms of the funding and the investor's expectations affect the pace, tolerated losses and time available for the plan.” Apply this wording consistently across formats.

## Proposed structure

Work and uncertainty → funding required under two scenarios → compare actual money, rights and time → make a recommendation within the leader's authority → revisit when the assumptions change. Keep the principle that declining a transaction can be a successful decision.
