**Match the funding to the work.** Personal cash for a selling founder, money for customer learning, funding for expansion and a change of control are different needs. Product and engineering leaders may not choose the investor but they inherit the terms, and it is the terms and the investor’s expectations, not the category, that set the pace, the tolerated losses and the time available for the plan.

**Figure 1:** *Size the work first, then choose the arrangement that can fund it.*

Separate **four dimensions**: who the investor is, what rights it holds, how the company is financed and what event it faces. A carve-out is a separation and a turnaround is a problem to solve; neither identifies the owner. [[customers-lenders-investors]] introduces the arrangements.

**Size the need before naming the investor.** In the fictional Larkspur case, onboarding depends on manual configuration and the queue is growing. Alex and Priya size the fix at about **€610,000**: €180,000 to build a reusable setup step, €30,000 of first-year maintenance, €90,000 of transition, €150,000 for a contract implementation specialist during the build, a €70,000 uncertainty allowance and €90,000 of decision runway if the evidence arrives late. Asking for the €180,000 build alone would fund the software and nothing that makes it work.

Two arrangements could fund it. **€2.5m of growth equity** brings a reserve and makes a later round less likely, but carries approval rights and an expectation of a second country within eighteen months, a pace the pilot has not earned. **A €750,000 four-year term loan** at 8%, principal repaid at €250,000 a year from year two, keeps control; the fixed year-end payments are €60,000, €310,000, €290,000 and €270,000. With about €350,000 of annual operating cash, Ines and Sam recommend the loan: every payment is tested against the €350,000 alone, with €40,000 to spare in the tightest year. Released staff time is a capacity benefit and stays out of repayment cash until a contract is cancelled or additional contribution is collected; the €140,000 of headroom is a reserve for the work, not for interest. The comparison is this chapter’s own scenario; the pilot figures come from [[roadmap-to-revenue]]. The day-90 cohort, decided at day 100, reviews the choice: below 60 hours, request the expansion; 60 to 70, fund the data-quality step and keep hiring deferred; above 70, reopen hiring, the target or the equity conversation while the loan still has headroom. Signed second-country customers would favour the equity.

Alex and Priya do not sign the financing. They bring the revised scope, the cash requirement with its components, the alternative of two hires at about €300,000 a year and the dated review. The CEO, the CFO and the board renegotiate terms.

**Control and partnership are separate questions.** A minority stake can carry material approval rights; a founder can keep shares and lose a role. [[decide-who-decides]] and [[investor-under-pressure]] develop them. Revisit the fit when demand, funding or time changes. Changing the transaction, changing the plan or changing nothing can each be right: a business that decides against outside ownership has answered the question, not failed a test.
