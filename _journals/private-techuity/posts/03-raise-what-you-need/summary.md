**Match the funding to the work.** A selling founder’s personal cash, money for customer learning, funding for expansion and a change of control (a new owner taking the deciding say) are different needs. Leaders who inherit the investor inherit its terms, the conditions attached to the money, and those terms and expectations, not the category, set the pace, the tolerated losses and the time the plan has.

Separate **four dimensions**: who the investor is, what rights it holds, whether the money is borrowed or exchanged for ownership, and what event the company faces. A carve-out separates a business from its parent; a turnaround repairs a business in serious trouble. Neither identifies the owner ([[customers-lenders-investors]]).

**Size the need before naming the investor.** In the fictional Larkspur case each new customer is set up by hand, taking 80 staff hours, and the queue is growing. Alex, who leads engineering, and Priya, the product leader, size the fix at about **€610,000**: €180,000 to build a reusable setup step, €30,000 of first-year maintenance, €90,000 of transition, €150,000 for a contract specialist during the build, a €70,000 allowance for estimates being wrong and €90,000 of runway, six months bought in case the evidence arrives late.

**€2.5 million of growth equity**, money exchanged for a share of ownership, brings a large reserve, but the investor would approve the annual budget and senior hires, and the money is raised on a plan to enter a second country within eighteen months, a pace the pilot has not earned. **A €750,000 four-year term loan** at 8%, the borrowed amount repaid at €250,000 a year from year two, keeps control with the current owners; year-end payments are fixed at €60,000, €310,000, €290,000 and €270,000.

Larkspur generates about €350,000 of operating cash a year after tax and existing obligations, before interest on new borrowing. Ines, the chief executive, and Sam, who runs finance, recommend the loan: every payment is tested against that €350,000 alone, with €40,000 to spare in the tightest year, year two. If operating cash fell to €300,000 that year, the payment would still be made from cash retained in year one; the downside consumes the cushion, not the work. Staff time the step frees is capacity, not repayment cash, until a contract is cancelled or extra customer money is collected. Equity raised later, after a disappointing pilot, might cost more of the company or be unavailable. Pilot figures come from [[roadmap-to-revenue]]; the financing is this chapter’s own scenario.

**The decision is dated.** At day 90 Priya measures the first eight customers set up with the new step, in staff hours per customer, from 80 today with 50 the target; the board decides at day 100. Below 60 hours: request the expansion. 60 to 70: fund a data-quality step, correcting customer data before setup, and keep hiring deferred. Above 70: reopen hiring, the target or the equity conversation while the loan still has headroom.

Alex and Priya do not sign the financing; they bring the scope, the cash requirement, the alternative of two hires at about €300,000 a year and the dated review. Ines and Sam renegotiate terms; the board approves.

**Control and partnership are separate questions.** A minority stake, less than half the shares, can carry material approval rights; a founder can keep shares and lose a role ([[decide-who-decides]], [[investor-under-pressure]]). Changing the transaction, changing the plan or changing nothing can each be right when demand, funding or time changes: a business that decides against outside ownership has answered the question, not failed a test.
