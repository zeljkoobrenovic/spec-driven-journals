**The first hundred days should produce a funded plan, not a finished transformation.** Each priority needs a named leader, approved money, protected engineering time and a decision date, and the day-100 review must be free to change the plan.

**Figure 1:** *Larkspur’s funded plan, the evidence the first hundred days produced, and the day-100 decision it changed.*

Take Larkspur, a fictional scheduling-software company, just after a growth investment. The investor now expects 50% more customers in the first year with the same onboarding team. Diligence left three findings: setting up new customers depends on one specialist entering settings by hand, only one engineer can release and recover the scheduling engine, and nobody has shown the service can be restored from its backups.

At day 0, the board meeting just after closing, the board sets an **envelope** of up to €500,000 of additional cash and 24 engineer-weeks. Its plan commits €280,000 and 18 of those weeks. Priya, who leads product, runs a €180,000 pilot of one reusable setup step. Alex, the chief technology officer, runs an €80,000 restore test and has a second engineer learn the engine, using existing staff time. A further €20,000 pays for research into a customer portal. The rest is a reserve that only the board can release. Building the portal, a second country and two permanent hires are postponed, each with a recorded decision.

A baseline from all twelve recent setups showed about 80 staff hours each, and the rule was agreed in advance: at day 90, under 60 hours per customer may justify the next stage, 60 to 70 funds another learning step, and over 70 reopens hiring.

The restore test failed at day 45, so the board released €20,000 and two engineer-weeks from the reserve, and the day-85 retest brought dispatch back within four hours. That proves recovery from one kind of failure, not from the loss of a whole cloud region. The second engineer passed at day 60.

The pilot cut effort from 80 to 62 hours per customer, short of the 50 an earlier planning model assumed, and customers waited just as long. At 62 hours the same staff handle about 1.3 times as many customers, not the 1.5 the thesis needs. About 40% of the remaining hours traced to poor customer data, so at day 100 the board funded a €40,000 data-quality step rather than more product work, and **kept expansion and hiring postponed** until the next review, around day 190.

That is what the review is for. **Judge the plan by what it learned, not by how many tasks it completed.** A lower forecast backed by evidence beats a deal story kept intact.

Keep the money honest too. Of the €340,000 committed by day 100, only about €210,000 had been incurred, meaning the work was received, not necessarily paid. That distinction matters when the plan changes hands, which is where [[manage-handover]] picks it up.
