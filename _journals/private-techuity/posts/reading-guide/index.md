> **KEY POINTS:**
>
> * **The company leader’s work organizes the book.** Learn how to understand investment expectations, influence decisions, choose work and obtain useful support.
> * **Private equity is the primary setting, with wider applications made explicit.** Check the actual funding, ownership and decision arrangements before transferring a lesson.
> * **Start from the basics.** Terms and calculations are introduced step by step; the cases and fictional examples have different roles in learning.

<br>
Why might a profitable company struggle to fund a needed software change? What changes when an investor enters? How can a product or technology leader use the relationship to build capabilities the company could not easily develop alone?

**Private Techuity: Product and Engineering Leadership in Private Equity** is a practical guide to those questions. Its primary reader leads product or technology inside a company, including someone joining or inheriting an ownership arrangement they did not choose.

**Private equity** means investment in company ownership outside public stock markets. The book concentrates on **buyouts**, purchases that give an investor control of a business, often involving borrowing and an intended later sale. That setting gives the explanations and historical cases a concrete context.

## How the Wider Lessons Apply

The book also explains when the same questions help under other arrangements. A company may obtain investment for uncertain early growth, raise money to expand an established product, sell shares to another operating business or keep funding work from its own customer payments.

Compare arrangements using five questions: where does the money go, who has authority, what outcome does the investor expect, how much time is available, and what support can actually be provided? The answers depend on the terms and circumstances, rather than the investor’s label alone.

The historical cases principally examine private equity. They do not establish the same results for every ownership model. Wider applications are explanations to examine against your situation, not a claim that every company follows the same path.

## Follow the Learning Sequence

Part I introduces money, ownership and financial results. Part II explains how investment changes authority and the decisions you make or influence. Part III helps you choose and evaluate product and technology investments. Part IV shows how to use investor support beyond money. Part V follows your responsibilities through the ownership cycle. Part VI uses historical cases and research to assess what lasts.

You can begin without an accounting, investment or software-architecture background. Read in order on a first pass. The two valuation chapters serve different purposes: [[pt-valuation-and-architecture]] teaches the financial concepts; [[pt-valuation-and-design]] applies them to technical choices after the product and engineering foundations.

The **ownership cycle** is a way to organize the period before, during and after an investment. This preview gives you the map; Part V develops the practical work:

| Stage | The company leader’s question |
| --- | --- |
| Investigation before investment | What can we demonstrate, and which assumptions need to change? |
| Early ownership | Which priorities have funding, team capacity and an accountable owner? |
| Ongoing ownership | What are we learning, and when must the plan or support change? |
| Sale or another ownership transition | Can the next owner understand the business, its capabilities and its unfinished work? |

A new investment need not replace the owner, and a planned sale does not provide a guaranteed timetable. The financial and decision arrangements determine which stages matter in your situation.

## Meet the Fictional Company

**Larkspur** sells scheduling software to maintenance businesses. It helps organize appointments and assign people to work. A recurring challenge is **customer onboarding**: the setup and help needed before a new customer can use the product successfully.

Ines is the **chief executive officer (CEO)**, responsible for leading the company. Alex is the **chief technology officer (CTO)**, leading technology. Sam is the **chief financial officer (CFO)**, leading finance. Priya leads product decisions. Morgan is a **Technology Principal**, the investment firm’s technology adviser in these examples.

Larkspur is a teaching example, not a documented company. The chapters explore alternative situations and proposals. Their figures do not form one continuous set of accounts. Assess each example within its stated assumptions; do not add different planning exercises, pilots and programs together. “€m” means millions of euros.

## Choose a Reading Format or Route

The 29 main chapters and two appendix essays each provide three formats:

- **Article:** the full explanation, worked examples, evidence and limits.
- **TL;DR:** a 300–500-word summary for orientation and recall. The label means “too long; didn’t read.”
- **Comic:** six fictional scenes showing the idea in conversation. These are readable storyboards; artwork is pending.

The six part introductions, appendix introduction and four reference or guide pages use the Article format. The [[pt-appendix]] is optional: it develops **Productscapes**, the author’s proposed approach to designing investor support from the provider’s side.

For a shorter first pass, read the part introductions and summaries. If an immediate situation brought you here, the following routes provide a starting point; use Part I when an unfamiliar financial concept appears.

## Read the Evidence With Its Limits

This is a living manuscript, first drafted in September 2026. The cases cover Hilton, Skype, Visma, Toys R Us and TeamSystem in specified historical periods. Company filings, investor accounts and research answer different questions. Missing customer or employee evidence remains explicit.

The technology-adviser chapter draws on a supplied role brief; confidential organizational details remain outside the published text, and the role is not universal. Proposed working methods and fictional results do not establish proven effectiveness. The [[pt-bibliography]] explains the sources and consultation scope.

Begin with [[pt-part-1]] and [[pt-how-companies-get-money]]. The [[pt-toolkit]] provides practical records, and the [[pt-glossary]] helps you recall terms as needed.
