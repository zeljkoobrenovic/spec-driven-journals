The morning after an ownership event, the customer contracts, the recovery requirement, the half-finished migration and the deferred hire are still there. A funding round adds an investor, a secondary sale gives one shareholder liquidity, a continuation transaction moves the holding to a new vehicle under the same manager, a sale of control changes who holds authority over the plan, and a listing may or may not. **Hand over those obligations, not only the shares.**

The evidence for a handover is kept during the work, not assembled for the exit. At each review, record the baseline, the cost committed, incurred and still payable, the continuing cost, the observed result with its uncertainty, and the open work with its funding status. A retrospective list of projects shows none of the limits of what is known, and a careful counterparty discounts it accordingly.

In the fictional Larkspur chain (the delayed-financing chapter is a separate scenario), the day-100 record for finding D-3 and its pilot ONB-1 reads: baseline about 80 hours per customer; €180,000 committed, about €90,000 incurred by day 100, €90,000 payable over the next two quarters, €30,000 a year of maintenance from the operating budget; about 10 of 12 engineer-weeks used; 62 hours observed in an eight-customer cohort rather than the 50 assumed, with about 40% of remaining hours traced to customer data quality; a funded data-quality step (€40,000, 4 weeks) that leaves the reserve at €160,000 and no engineer-weeks; the expansion decision at the next quarterly review, about day 190, and two hires deferred; Priya accountable.

Obligations are not all of one kind. Keep contractual obligations (customer contracts, the €90,000 still payable), funded work (the data-quality step), accepted risks (the recovery test’s residual, accepted with a quarterly retest) and options awaiting evidence (expansion, hiring) as separate entries: only the first is a debt; the rest are decisions with reasons.

Resist exit-period distortions. Delaying investment, classifying recurring work as exceptional or leaving difficult customer commitments off the roadmap increases what the next owner inherits without knowing it. Buyers can price unfinished transitions; what they cannot price is what was not disclosed.

**Follow who receives cash and who keeps an interest.** A round brings cash to the company; a secondary sale of company shares pays a shareholder and brings a new one; a sale of a fund interest leaves the company’s direct shareholder unchanged; a continuation transaction pays out the fund investors who leave and needs attention to price and conflicts, which ILPA treats as a governance issue. [S02: ILPA principles](https://ilpa.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/06/ILPA-Principles-3.0_2019.pdf) Forms combine, sellers may keep a minority interest, and an IPO can leave control with the founders. Hilton’s final stake sale in 2018 and Visma’s 2023 transaction are different realization contexts; neither is a completed fund outcome until proceeds, retained interests and dates are known. [S24: Blackstone 2018 investor call](https://ir.blackstone.com/files/doc_events/BLACKSTONE-Second-Quarter-2018-Earnings-Investor-Call.pdf) [S30: Visma 2023 transaction](https://www.visma.com/newsroom/visma-attracts-new-investors-for-further-international-expansion-in-a-transaction-valuing-the-company-at-eur-19-billion-59703d9f)

A handover is accepted, not merely sent. In the illustration, Ines, the chief executive with authority to spend inside the approved plan, signs at day 130 with Priya, who stays accountable for onboarding; the new investor’s director receives the record but does not run the work; the unbudgeted €30,000 maintenance is the recorded exception, reviewed at day 190. Later deterioration does not prove the prior owners failed, and later success does not prove every intervention was necessary; a consistent record lets both sides learn.
