A company changes hands more often than it changes work. A funding round adds an investor, a secondary sale gives one shareholder liquidity, a continuation transaction moves the holding to a new vehicle under the same manager, and a sale or listing changes who controls the company. The morning after each of these, the customer contracts, the recovery requirement, the half-finished migration and the deferred hire are still there. **Hand over those obligations, not only the shares.**

The evidence for a handover is kept during the work, not assembled for the exit. At each review, record the baseline, the actual cost to date and the continuing cost, the observed result with its uncertainty, and the open work with its funding status. A retrospective list of projects shows none of the limits of what is known, and a careful counterparty discounts it accordingly.

In the fictional Larkspur chain, the record for diligence finding D-3 and its funded pilot ONB-1 reads: baseline about 80 hours of implementation effort per customer; actual cost €180,000 to build and €30,000 a year to maintain; observed result 62 hours in an eight-customer cohort rather than the 50 assumed, with about 40% of the remaining hours traced to customer data quality; open work a funded data-quality step, a deferred second-country expansion and two deferred hires; accountable leader Priya. That is what she hands to whoever decides next, whether a new investor’s board member, a buyer’s integration lead or the same board after a continuation transaction.

Resist exit-period distortions. Delaying investment, classifying recurring work as exceptional or leaving difficult customer commitments off the roadmap increases what the next owner inherits without knowing it. Buyers can price unfinished transitions; what they cannot price is what was not disclosed.

**Follow who receives cash and who keeps an interest.** A round brings cash to the company; a secondary sale of company shares pays a shareholder and brings a new one; a sale of a fund interest changes nothing at the company because its shareholder, the fund, is unchanged; a continuation transaction pays out the fund investors who leave and needs attention to price and conflicts, which ILPA treats as a governance issue. Authority can change even when the name does not. Redo the approval map for the new arrangement, carry forward the original baselines and results, and do not reset the history for a new reporting format. A handover is accepted, not merely sent: each open obligation has an accountable leader on the receiving side, and the receiving decision-maker signs for the record. Any follow-up role needs an agreed scope and access. Later deterioration does not prove the prior owners failed, and later success does not prove every intervention was necessary; a consistent record is what lets both sides learn from the whole ownership period.
