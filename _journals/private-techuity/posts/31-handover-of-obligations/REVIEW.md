# Editorial review: Hand Over the Obligations, Not Just the Company

Reassessed 15 September 2026 from the complete current article, summary and comic text where present, the previous review where present, revision log and Git changes. See the [collection review](../REVIEW.md).

## Current assessment

The new chapter gives the collection a useful destination for evidence and unfinished work. It is a better home for transaction forms and handover than the delayed-financing chapter. The distinction between company-share and fund-interest secondaries, the persistence of operating history, and the caution about attributing later success or failure are strengths worth preserving.

The chapter is not yet a reliable demonstration of its own method. The day-100 record mislabels a commitment as actual cost, several transaction descriptions lose important qualifications, and acceptance remains a proposed process rather than a completed example. Its new format coverage is also incomplete.

## Active recommendations

1. **High impact — correct the day-100 actual-cost record.** The table and summary label €180,000 as actual build cost. [The first-hundred-days chapter](../17-first-hundred-days/index.md) explicitly says €180,000 committed and about €90,000 spent during the period. A handover chapter must preserve that distinction. Suggested entry: “About €90,000 spent by day 100 against a €180,000 build commitment; reconcile the unpaid balance and incurred costs; €30,000 annual maintenance separately funded.” Record 12 engineer-weeks as actual effort only if that was measured, rather than copying an allocation into an actual-cost field. Carry the reconciled recovery reserve and expansion dates into the open-work row too.

2. **Medium impact — qualify control and retained interests by transaction form.** The opening and summary say a listing changes who controls the company. An IPO need not do so: [Facebook's registration filing](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1326801/000119312512034517/d287954ds1.htm) explicitly describes continuing founder voting control and controlled-company status. Likewise, a sale of control can leave sellers with a minority interest, and a transaction can combine secondary purchases with new capital. Label the table's simplified examples and add the relevant “may retain” or “unless combined with new issuance” qualifications. A sale of a fund interest leaves the direct shareholder unchanged; “changes nothing at the company” is a stronger claim than that fact establishes.

3. **Medium impact — distinguish obligations from deferred choices.** An existing customer contract, an authorized but unbuilt data-quality step and an unapproved future hire are not the same kind of commitment. “Each is a commitment with a cost and a date … owed to a customer, a regulator or the company's own operations” overstates the status of deferred hires and opportunities. Use separate fields for contractual obligations, funded work, accepted risks and options awaiting evidence. This carries the earlier chapters' commitment vocabulary into the place it matters most.

4. **Medium impact — complete one accepted handover.** The article says a receiving decision-maker signs, but names no receiver, acceptance date or unresolved exception in the worked record. Add a brief fictional acknowledgment: who has authority, what they accept, which funding remains open and when it is reviewed. A new investor's board member is not automatically the operating leader accountable for onboarding. Keep Priya's continuing responsibility distinct from oversight or receipt of information.

5. **Medium impact — complete the promised reading formats.** There is no current comic text for this new main chapter, and its logo/icon are also absent. The missing comic is a coverage gap, not a finding that existing panels are wrong. Define the six-panel handover story before artwork work; it should preserve actual versus committed cost and the receiving leader's acceptance. No article or asset changes are made by this review.

6. **Low impact — trim universal opening claims.** “Investors realize value through the next transaction” excludes distributions during continued ownership. “A company changes hands more often than it changes work” is a rhetorical claim, not needed evidence. Open directly with the customer obligation that remains after the transaction. The current section order is otherwise effective and does not need another outline.

## Verification and limits

The day-100 spending conflict is confirmed from the two current articles. The IPO qualification is confirmed against a primary filing; the table is not a statement of universal transaction law. Historical Hilton and Visma references are useful comparisons of realization contexts, not complete vehicle cash-flow reconstructions. The annual maintenance amount is a continuing cost assumption, not evidence that a full year's maintenance has already been paid.
