**Incentives are rewards and consequences that influence choices.** Shared ownership creates some common interests, but investors, executives, employees and customers can still face different risks and time horizons.

**Management equity** gives company leaders ownership or economic rights under an agreed plan. A founder may retain or reinvest an interest after a sale, called a **rollover**. A manager might buy shares, receive them subject to conditions, or receive **options**, rights to buy shares on stated terms.

The possible payment depends on more than a percentage. **Vesting** describes how rights are earned over time or through conditions. **Preferences** determine which shares receive money first. **Dilution** describes how issuing more equity changes existing ownership or payouts. Departure terms determine what happens when someone leaves. Ask for worked scenarios using the actual documents.

In the fictional example, a manager has 5% of proceeds above a €100 million threshold. If qualifying proceeds are €140 million, the relevant pool is €40 million and the payment is €2 million. It is not 5% of the entire sale amount. Other plans can work differently.

**Carried interest**, the investment manager’s contractual share of fund profits, is a separate arrangement. It need not track the payment to executives in one company. The fund’s investors also have interests that do not cover every employee or customer concern. [S02: ILPA principles](https://ilpa.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/06/ILPA-Principles-3.0_2019.pdf)

Examine what a target encourages people to postpone or conceal. A reward based only on this year’s earnings may discourage investment whose benefits arrive later. Growth can be achieved through discounts that leave little profit. Pair targets with measures of customer outcomes, service quality and the ability to keep investing. These supporting measures help reveal harmful side effects.

Leadership assessments need the same care. Establish what a new leader should change, and consider the authority, resources and priorities around the role. Review decision quality, execution and results separately: a sound decision can have a poor outcome, and a weak decision can get lucky.

Finally, record who gains and who bears the costs. Employees without shares and customers facing difficult product changes need their own assessment. With authority and incentives understood, the next chapter asks how to judge a particular investor: [[pt-investor-fit]].
