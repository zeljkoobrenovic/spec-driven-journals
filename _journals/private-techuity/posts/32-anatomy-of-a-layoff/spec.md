# Spec: Anatomy of a Layoff

## Intent

Follow a product and engineering reduction from the investor’s request to an authorized, funded operating plan and its consequences for people and delivery. Continue the separate delayed-financing scenario from [[the-financing-slipped]] into the deeper reduction it left open: establish the staffing and payroll baseline that chapter never stated, name where the reduction pressure actually comes from (a condition on the bridge, a director’s formal vote, an adviser’s benchmark), compare the alternatives (hiring freeze, contractor reduction, redeployment, pay measures, a reduction), and reconcile notice periods, severance, transition spending, recurring savings and cash by date. Show the resulting team, the work stopped, the customer commitments revised, the knowledge transferred and the responsibilities retained, and address both the people who leave and the people who remain.

## Audience

Product and engineering leaders inside companies working under investors, including leaders who inherit an ownership arrangement. Assume no specialist finance background; explain necessary terms before use. Investor-side readers are secondary.

## Success criteria

- Frame the chapter, at the author’s request, with the two-sided effect of outside investment on a team: it can expand headcount significantly and quickly, and it adds obligations and financial pressures that can require cost reduction just as quickly, which is how unpopular and damaging layoffs come about; state this as the leader’s condition, not as a prevalence claim.
- Open on 1 October in the delayed-financing scenario with the board’s costing request and the bridge commitment’s condition; state once that this scenario is deliberately separate from the shared Larkspur ledger and every figure is fictional.
- State the missing baseline (40 employees, €324,000 monthly payroll by function, €50,000 contractors, €76,000 non-payroll, €250,000 collected revenue, €200,000 net burn) so that it reproduces the earlier chapter’s €200,000 burn, €150,000 after R1 and €500,000 at 31 December without the bridge.
- Distinguish three pressures with different responses: the investors’ funding condition on the bridge (a cash date, not a headcount number), the investor director’s formal vote and the fictional reserved matter over the operating plan, and the “20% of engineering” benchmark as influence.
- Compare the alternatives in one table (freeze already in force, R1, redeployment, an executive pay deferral, reduced hours, the chosen reduction R2, the revenue-only floor R3) on recurring saving, one-off cost, first full month of saving and what each removes.
- Reconcile R2 by date: eight roles, notices on 31 October; three leave payroll on 30 November, three on 31 December, one second-country engineer on 31 January and the other, retained on an agreed fixed term at about €8,500 a month (€17,000), on 31 March; savings of €20,000 in December, €45,000 in January, €63,500 in February and March and €72,000 a month from April; one-off costs of €120,000 paid between November and March (€30,000, €45,000, €32,000, €13,000); a cash table showing €488,000 at 30 June with the bridge (condition met, reserve reached in the first week of August), €200,000 at 30 June without R2 (condition failed) and the reserve breached in January without the bridge whatever R2 does; one contingency of one further month for the retained specialist (€8,500, to 30 April) leaving about €480,000 at 30 June if used.
- State the cash-timing conclusion conditionally: at Larkspur’s notice periods, severance terms and dates the reduction cannot stand in for the late bridge; elsewhere the answer depends on the costs, savings and funding dates, so the general lesson is to compare them by date. Key points, TL;DR, comic captions and illustration prompts carry the same qualification.
- Record the decision with chosen option, alternatives rejected, funding, scarce capacity, who is authorized (the board on 15 October with the investor director’s consent; Ines for individual notices within the plan and the law), review dates and the evidence that would change it, including that a later round does not reverse the reduction. Reviews read only evidence that exists by their date: 1 December (notices, bridge, the first customer’s amendment, second-customer progress), 1 February (readiness: departures executed, retention in force, December and January savings against the ramp), 1 March (February’s actual savings), 15 March (transfer test), 1 April (second customer live, March savings, the round’s stage), 1 May (April’s actual full-month savings; contingency outcome).
- Show the team after R2 (12 engineers, Priya, one product manager, one designer; onboarding, core with the second-country rules, the recovery pair redeployed), the work stopped (the field-worker mobile app, further countries), the customer commitments (both contracts promise first-quarter go-live, with a separate right to terminate after 30 June; the first customer signs an amendment to 30 September before the notices; the second declines any change, so its 31 March promise is kept by retaining one second-country specialist to 31 March, with the engineer-month arithmetic stated and the termination right recorded as the remaining risk; three mobile beta customers), the knowledge-transfer test run on the real second-country work before the specialist leaves (first week of March, read on 15 March; one funded further month to 30 April as the only fallback, retest read on 15 April; board re-planning on 1 May with three recorded options and no contractor assumed), and the on-call and workload consequences for the people who remain.
- Treat the people who leave concretely (notice, severance formula, accrued leave, options under the leaver terms in [[different-bets]], references and outplacement) and the people who remain (a published stopped list, the on-call change, what they are not expected to absorb, what would restore the plan), with one communication sequence derived from the same dated cash table.
- Tie employment-process details to jurisdiction: cite [S77] for the directive’s definition, its establishment-size bands (more than 20 and fewer than 100; at least 100 and fewer than 300; 300 or more, over 30 days) and 90-day alternative, the assimilation of other employer-initiated terminations once there are at least five redundancies, consultation of representatives as a duty distinct from written notification of the authority and the 30-day period, and member states’ freedom to be more favourable; cite [S78] for the US statute’s employer definition (100 or more employees excluding part-time employees, or 100 or more who in aggregate work at least 4,000 hours a week exclusive of overtime), the single-site 30-day plant-closing and mass-layoff tests (33 percent and 50, or 500), the 90-day aggregation rule, 60 days’ notice and its recipients, and the reduced-notice exceptions; present the passage in several short paragraphs; keep Larkspur’s fictional notice, severance and retention terms distinct from any statutory minimum; make no claim about which law governs Larkspur.
- Add two short variations without a second full case: a profitable company pursuing a margin target (no cash date; the [[different-bets]] consolidation is the worked example) and an acquisition-related consolidation (the synergy date follows the knowledge-transfer test in [[fix-decisions-before-hiring]] and the completion tests in [[acquisition-adds-work-first]]).
- Keep the company leader’s decisions central; distinguish investor influence, formal approval rights and funding conditions throughout; avoid duplicating the delegation, incentive and location material owned by other chapters.
- Provide a TL;DR of 300–500 words and a six-panel comic consistent with the article, two article figures with numbered captions, the TL;DR overview visual, a header logo and a navigation icon, all generated with the journal’s workflows and read back against their prompts.
- Close with a handoff to [[handover-of-obligations]].
