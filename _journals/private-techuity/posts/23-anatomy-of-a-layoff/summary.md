Outside investment can let a company hire faster than revenue alone would allow; its obligations can force cuts as quickly. Fictional Larkspur, a scheduling-software company, awaits a **round**, €4 million of new investment not yet signed. Its existing investors offer a **bridge**, a €600,000 loan to carry it until then. The board’s **reserve** is a €400,000 minimum cash balance. Figures are fictional, separate from other Larkspur examples.

The bridge comes with a condition: by 31 October the board must adopt a plan that keeps cash above the reserve until 30 June without the round. **Trace a request to cut staff to its source first.** The condition is a cash date, not a headcount; Sam, the finance chief, turns it into a saving of at least €40,000 a month from February. The investor’s board member holds a **formal vote**. A fifth of engineering, other companies’ cut, is only **influence**, and saves too little: €28,000 a month.

Larkspur has forty employees and spends €200,000 a month beyond customer payments, €150,000 once contractors leave in October. The chosen reduction stops a mobile app and narrows a second-country expansion: eight roles, saving €72,000 a month, for €120,000 of one-off costs. Cutting about sixteen roles, to what customer payments cover, was rejected: it removed staff revenue depends on.

**Savings arrive after costs.** Leavers are paid through one to three months’ notice and receive severance, a leaving payment. Leaving costs fall due November to March; the full €72,000 saving comes only in April. With the bridge, cash is €488,000 on 30 June, meeting the condition; without the reduction, €200,000. Without the bridge, cash is below the reserve by January either way. **At these terms the reduction makes the bridge last longer but cannot replace it**; elsewhere, compare costs, savings and funding by date.

The eight are told individually, first, on 31 October. One second-country customer agrees to start in September; the other keeps 31 March. One specialist stays until a colleague proves, on real work, they can support both alone. If that test fails, pre-agreed options keep the specialist to 30 June at most; if a final test fails, Larkspur seeks agreed early ends with both customers, recording any refusal as unresolved.

Leavers receive paid notice, severance on a written formula, job-search help, a reference, and a year to use share options already earned: rights to buy shares at a fixed price, by paying it. The thirty-two who remain get a published list of work no longer expected and an out-of-hours duty rota shared by eight, not ten. The reduction only buys time: Larkspur still spends €78,000 a month beyond customer payments, so financing must be settled before August.

Local law decides whether a group layoff needs a special procedure. European Union rules can require consulting employee representatives and notifying a public authority before dismissals take effect. The United States Worker Adjustment and Retraining Notification (WARN) Act generally requires sixty days’ notice of large closings and layoffs, with exceptions.
