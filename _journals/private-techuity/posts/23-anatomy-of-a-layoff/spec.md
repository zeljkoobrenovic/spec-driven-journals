# Spec: Anatomy of a Layoff

## Intent

Follow a product and engineering reduction from the investor’s request to an authorized, funded operating plan and its consequences for people and delivery. Continue the separate delayed-financing scenario from [[the-financing-slipped]] into the deeper reduction it left open: establish the staffing and payroll baseline that chapter never stated, name where the reduction pressure actually comes from (a condition on the bridge, a director’s formal vote, an adviser’s benchmark), compare the alternatives (hiring freeze, contractor reduction, redeployment, pay measures, a reduction), and reconcile notice periods, severance, transition spending, recurring savings and cash by date. Show the resulting team, the work stopped, the customer commitments revised, the knowledge transferred and the responsibilities retained, and address both the people who leave and the people who remain.

## Audience

Product and engineering leaders inside companies working under investors, including leaders who inherit an ownership arrangement. Assume no specialist finance background; explain necessary terms before use, including in the opening callouts. The TL;DR and the comic are read on their own, so each explains bridge, round and reserve itself and uses descriptive plan names rather than bare R-labels. Investor-side readers are secondary.

## Success criteria

- Frame the chapter, at the author’s request, with the two-sided effect of outside investment on a team: it can expand headcount significantly and quickly, and it adds obligations and financial pressures that can require cost reduction just as quickly, which is how unpopular and damaging layoffs come about; state this as the leader’s condition, not as a prevalence claim, and in conditional wording (investment *can* let a company hire faster than revenue would allow).
- Open on 1 October in the delayed-financing scenario with the board’s costing request and the bridge commitment’s condition; state once that this scenario is deliberately separate from the shared Larkspur ledger and every figure is fictional. Introduce each person by role (Alex, chief technology officer; Priya, product leader; Sam, chief financial officer; Ines, chief executive; Morgan, the investor’s adviser) and say briefly what the onboarding pilot and the recovery work are.
- State the missing baseline (40 employees, €324,000 monthly payroll by function, €50,000 contractors, €76,000 non-payroll, €250,000 collected revenue, €200,000 net burn) so that it reproduces the earlier chapter’s €200,000 burn, €150,000 after R1 and €500,000 at 31 December without the bridge. The payroll row is pay, employer charges and benefits, the cost that stops when employment ends; equipment and software sit in the non-payroll row and do not simply stop when a person leaves. State the cash model’s assumptions next to the cash table: €1.0m on 1 October, customer collections constant at €250,000 a month, the bridge’s terms from [[the-financing-slipped]] (convertible, no fee, interest added to the debt, nothing payable in cash for twelve months after the money arrives), the executive pay deferral and any round money excluded, receipts from the two second-country customers excluded throughout the table (even after go-live, so a go-live or a termination changes the sales forecast, not the cash table, apart from any settlement), the month-end formula, and reserve-crossing days approximate.
- Distinguish three pressures with different responses: the investors’ funding condition on the bridge (a cash date, not a headcount number), the investor director’s formal vote and the fictional reserved matter over the operating plan, and the “20% of engineering” benchmark as influence.
- Compare the alternatives in one table (freeze already in force, R1, redeployment, an executive pay deferral, reduced hours, the chosen reduction R2, the revenue-only floor R3) on recurring saving, one-off cost, first full month of saving and what each removes.
- Reconcile R2 by date: eight roles, notices on 31 October; three leave payroll on 30 November, three on 31 December, one second-country engineer on 31 January and the other, retained on an agreed fixed term at about €8,500 a month (€17,000), on 31 March; savings of €20,000 in December, €45,000 in January, €63,500 in February and March and €72,000 a month from April (€63,000 of the eight people’s pay, plus €9,000 of the mobile app’s non-payroll spending on contracts that end on 31 January, so saved from February); one-off costs of €120,000 paid between November and March (€30,000, €45,000, €32,000, €13,000); a cash table showing €488,000 at 30 June with the bridge (condition met, reserve reached in the first week of August), €200,000 at 30 June without R2 (condition failed) and the reserve breached in January without the bridge whatever R2 does; one contingency of one further month for the retained specialist (€8,500, to 30 April) leaving €479,500 at 30 June if used. The three plans are compared in a short table, not one long paragraph. Figure 1 is a chart plotted from these month-end balances (starting from €1.0m on 1 October, showing November’s bridge receipt, the €400,000 reserve labelled, month abbreviations and a euro scale) with the bridge-without-R2 series marked as a comparison that fails the condition.
- State the cash-timing conclusion conditionally: at Larkspur’s notice periods, severance terms and dates the reduction cannot stand in for the late bridge; elsewhere the answer depends on the costs, savings and funding dates, so the general lesson is to compare them by date. Key points, TL;DR, comic captions and illustration prompts carry the same qualification.
- Record the decision with chosen option, alternatives rejected, funding, scarce capacity, who is authorized (the board on 15 October with the investor director’s consent; Ines for individual notices within the plan and the law), review dates and the evidence that would change it, including that a later round does not reverse the reduction. Reviews read only evidence that exists by their date, shown as a compact table: 1 December (notices, bridge, the first customer’s amendment, second-customer progress), 1 February (readiness: the seven departures executed, retention in force, December and January savings against the ramp), 1 March (February’s actual savings), 15 March (transfer test), 1 April (second customer live, March savings, the round’s stage), 1 May (April’s actual savings; contingency outcome). The 1 April and 1 May rows are conditional on the transfer test: they state the expected departure date, recurring saving and month of the specialist’s final leaving payment for the base case (31 March; €72,000 from April; payment in March), the April contingency (30 April; €63,500 in April; payment in April; €479,500 on 30 June) and extensions to 30 June (payment in June; €462,500), and compare actuals with the branch that applies. The branch figures sit in a separate three-row comparison table (specialist leaves 31 March, 30 April or 30 June: final-payment month, cash at 31 March and 30 April, April saving, first full-saving month, cash on 30 June, reserve date), so the review calendar only says what each review checks. The evidence that would change the decision is a trigger/response table, not one long bullet; the incident trigger distinguishes work nobody has contracted for, which stops at once, from contractual commitments, which move only by the customer’s agreement, negotiated by Priya, with the board deciding between costed extra capacity and the contract’s remedy, on legal advice, if a customer refuses and the team cannot deliver safely.
- Show the team after R2 (12 engineers, Priya, one product manager, one designer; onboarding, core with the second-country rules, the recovery pair redeployed), the work stopped (the field-worker mobile app, further countries), the customer commitments (both contracts promise first-quarter go-live, with a separate right to terminate after 30 June; the first customer signs an amendment to 30 September before the notices; the second declines any change, so its 31 March promise is kept by retaining one second-country specialist to 31 March, with the engineer-month arithmetic stated from 1 November (an engineer-month defined; 13 engineer-months of capacity to 31 March, 1 reserved for writing down and reviewing the tax-rule mapping, 12 against the 11 needed, so about two weeks of calendar margin; the first customer’s 5 engineer-months in April–September leave about one engineer-month for supporting the live second customer) and the termination right recorded as the remaining risk; three mobile beta customers), the knowledge-transfer test run on the real second-country work before the specialist leaves, in two parts read separately (routine support: configuration changes and the customer’s support requests; a legally required tax-rule change), first week of March, read on 15 March; the retention agreement, signed before any notice is served and a precondition of serving them, gives Larkspur month-by-month options to keep the specialist to 30 June at €8,500 a month; the first further month to 30 April is the plan’s only contingency, retest read on 15 April; if it fails, the board decides by 22 April whether to use the May and June options (€462,500 on 30 June, reserve about 25 July; board approval with the investor director’s consent) with a final test read on 15 June, and Priya opens talks with both second-country customers on an agreed early end as the fallback, because passing both parts of the test is also the evidence that the remaining engineer can build and support the first customer’s configuration alone (the six engineer-months from April establish time, not capability); settlements for both customers together come from the €62,500 headroom (about four days earlier reserve per €10,000; neither customer’s fees were ever in the cash forecast); if the final test fails and either customer refuses an early end, the record states that customer’s commitment as unresolved: the specialist’s departure is not part of an approved plan until the board agrees new terms with the specialist long enough to cover it (to the first customer’s 30 September go-live and its later rule changes, if that customer stays), costed into the forecast, or takes legal advice on the contract; no contractor assumed, and the on-call and workload consequences for the people who remain.
- Treat the people who leave concretely (notice, severance formula, accrued leave, options under the leaver terms in [[different-bets]], references and outplacement) and the people who remain (a published stopped list, the on-call change, what they are not expected to absorb, what would restore the plan), with one communication sequence derived from the same dated cash table.
- Tie employment-process details to jurisdiction: cite [S77] for the directive’s definition, its establishment-size bands (more than 20 and fewer than 100; at least 100 and fewer than 300; 300 or more, over 30 days) and 90-day alternative, the assimilation of other employer-initiated terminations once there are at least five redundancies, consultation of representatives as a duty distinct from written notification of the authority and the 30-day period, and member states’ freedom to be more favourable; cite [S78] for the US statute’s employer definition (100 or more employees excluding part-time employees, or 100 or more who in aggregate work at least 4,000 hours a week exclusive of overtime), the single-site 30-day plant-closing and mass-layoff tests (33 percent and 50, or 500), the 90-day aggregation rule, 60 days’ notice and its recipients, and the reduced-notice exceptions (the capital-seeking exception covers only a shutdown of a single site, and only where the employer reasonably and in good faith believed notice would have prevented it obtaining capital or business that would have avoided or postponed the shutdown; linked to § 2102); the part-time exclusion applies to the 100-employee test and the plant-closing and mass-layoff counts, not to the alternative 4,000-hours aggregate test; present the passage in several short paragraphs; keep Larkspur’s fictional notice, severance and retention terms distinct from any statutory minimum; make no claim about which law governs Larkspur. Name the Worker Adjustment and Retraining Notification (WARN) Act in full; a plant closing needs a shutdown of a site or of facilities or operating units within it as well as fifty losses; WARN’s part-time category includes a tenure test; the directive’s thirty-day period can be shortened or extended by the authority where national law allows; say that a collective procedure means rebuilding the timetable under the applicable rules (which periods can overlap), not adding periods mechanically; works-council rules apply where they exist. Put the thresholds in a labelled reference table. In the TL;DR, keep the EU and US examples in separate sentences.
- Add two short variations without a second full case: a profitable company pursuing a margin target (no cash date; the [[different-bets]] consolidation is the worked example) and an acquisition-related consolidation (the synergy date follows the knowledge-transfer test in [[fix-decisions-before-hiring]] and the completion tests in [[acquisition-adds-work-first]]).
- Keep the company leader’s decisions central; distinguish investor influence, formal approval rights and funding conditions throughout; avoid duplicating the delegation, incentive and location material owned by other chapters.
- Explain severance, burn, pay deferral, job-search support (outplacement) and works councils where they first matter, and in the TL;DR say that share options are rights to buy shares at a fixed price, which costs money to use. Provide a TL;DR of 300–500 words and a comic of nine pages of three strips each consistent with the article, two article figures with numbered captions, the TL;DR overview visual, a header logo and a navigation icon, all generated with the journal’s workflows and read back against their prompts.
- Close with a handoff to [[handover-of-obligations]] that also states what R2 does not solve: it still spends €78,000 a month more than customers pay, reaches the reserve in the first week of August, and the bridge remains owed under its terms, so the board must decide financing or another outcome before that date.

## Non-goals

Legal or tax advice on dismissals in any jurisdiction; universal severance or notice formulas; the shared Larkspur ledger (this scenario stays separate); the incentive comparison already worked in [[different-bets]]; the location comparison and knowledge-transfer test already in [[fix-decisions-before-hiring]]; promises of investment performance or disclosure of confidential inputs.

- Full Article: index.md, the substantive argument.
- TL;DR: summary.md, the practical implications in concise prose (300–500 words) with its overview visual; readable without the article.
- Comic: comics.md, nine illustrated pages of three strips each, generated on 2026-09-23 with the journal’s cast reference from the `comic-page` blocks that are also the artwork scripts, with the dialogue, labels, amounts and dates lettered in the artwork; alt text, visible captions and per-strip transcripts are rendered from the blocks, and every page is inspected against its script. The introduction defines bridge, round, reserve and the R1/R2/R3 labels; captions describe each plan in words and explain options and the on-call rota.
