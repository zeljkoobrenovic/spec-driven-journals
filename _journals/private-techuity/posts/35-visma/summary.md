**A familiar investment firm does not mean the same owners, and the same year's profit can carry two numbers.** For a leader who runs shared systems, both change who pays for the next piece of work.

In December 2023, the business-software group Visma announced a share sale that valued it at €19 billion. About 20 new investors would put in over €1 billion and existing investors around €3 billion. Hg, involved since 2006, would keep more than half the shares. [S30: Visma 2023 transaction](https://www.visma.com/newsroom/visma-attracts-new-investors-for-further-international-expansion-in-a-transaction-valuing-the-company-at-eur-19-billion-59703d9f)

It sounds like continuity. But this was a **secondary sale**: existing shareholders sold, so the money went to them rather than to Visma and the announcement states no new money for the company. Hg, too, is a manager, not a single pot of money. It invests through funds, each with its own investors and its own view of when to sell. Its 2017 announcement names different funds for 2006 and 2014, and describes KKR selling all its shares and Cinven selling part of its holding. Nothing says Hg's 2023 majority sits in one fund. **The manager stayed; the money behind it changed.**

Meanwhile Visma kept building and buying. In 2024 it reported 33 acquisitions, nearly three a month, and described local decision-making backed by shared technical foundations such as hosting and security. That model is a question the case raises, not proof.

The buying pace matters for the numbers. Visma's 2024 annual report gives EBITDA, a profit measure before interest, tax and the charges that spread asset costs over time, of about €893 million. Its 2025 report adds back €11.665 million of costs of carrying out acquisitions, reaching adjusted EBITDA of about €904 million for the same year. [S44: Visma 2025 annual report, p. 96](https://cdn.prod.website-files.com/69787181d8720ea08c1f22fe/69bb9fc407e856cb8d6f7726_Visma%20Annual%20Report%202025.pdf) No cash comes back. The higher figure simply answers a different question: what the businesses earned before deal costs.

For a group that buys every year, that leaves a planning gap. **Costs can be exceptional for one transaction while recurring across a continuing program.** The adjusted figure cannot say whether next year's deal costs will be similar, whether integrating last year's purchases is funded and staffed, or which earnings definition the lenders use. That takes a cash plan: the expected deals and their cost, integration capacity in engineer-weeks, and a named owner for the budget.

So after a transaction like Visma's, recheck three things: which funds now hold the shares and what they expect; whether shared services and integration work are still funded, and by whom; and which earnings definition the board, lenders and plan each use. Independent evidence from customers, employees and acquired companies is missing, and **a successful total can hide unsuccessful parts**.

Visma reported positive earnings under either definition. The next case asks what happens when operating earnings are positive but the cash left after interest and investment cannot fund the technology plan: [[toys-r-us]].
