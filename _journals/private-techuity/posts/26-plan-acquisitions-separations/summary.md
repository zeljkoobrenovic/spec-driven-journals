**Buying a business, or separating one from its owner, looks like a single signature, but for the teams running the software it starts a long piece of work.** The plan behind the deal can count savings or independence before anyone has checked that the work fits the time, so raise those dependencies while scope, price and timing can still change.

Combining and separating are different jobs under one principle: the deal draws a new boundary, and customers on both sides still expect the product to work. Ownership changes on the signing date; the transition is complete only when the evidence says so.

When combining, **choose the depth of integration for a named benefit**, not as a stage of maturity. Two businesses can stay separate under a sales agreement, share services beneath separate products, connect their workflows, or merge into one product. Selling the acquired product to existing customers needs none of the deeper options; the promise of one sign-in, one bill and one screen does.

Either way, **the work competes with the product plan**. Take Larkspur, a fictional company owned by a buyout fund, which buys a smaller company on 1 April 2026 to connect its dispatch product with Larkspur’s scheduling software. From April to September, after leave and keeping the service running, the only two engineers who understand that software have twenty-six weeks of project time between them. Three jobs need twenty-nine: the integration sixteen, a customer booking portal promised to named customers ten, and investigating the next purchase three. Something must wait. The integration goes first, April to June, so the combined offering still goes on sale on 1 January 2027, as the price assumed. The portal moves one quarter, to 31 December, and the investigation starts in July instead of May. The board approves the change.

Separating asks a different question: what does independence cost? In a second, separate fictional example, Larkspur is a division leaving its parent. The parent charged it €400,000 a year for IT services, but that was an accounting split, not a price. Buying the same services independently costs €700,000 a year, plus €300,000 once to set them up. The deal’s forecast must carry both, or the division’s past profit margin overstates what the separated company will keep.

The parent’s temporary services come with a three-month extension that must be **reserved in writing by 31 March 2027**, while the test that shows whether it is needed runs in May. So Larkspur reserves it whatever March shows. Cancelling is free until 31 May; after that, all €157,500 is owed. Waiting for evidence before reserving leaves no fallback when the evidence arrives.

Before any of this, confirm who holds the rights to code, data, licences and contracts; afterwards, measure the benefit the deal named, counting a shared saving once.
