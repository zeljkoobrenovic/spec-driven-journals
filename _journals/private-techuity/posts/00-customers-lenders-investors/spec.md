# Spec: Customers, Lenders and Investors: What Each Expects in Return

## Intent

Explain the funding behind a company leader's commitments by following one fictional need, Larkspur's €100,000, through a customer prepayment, a loan and a new share issue, and completing the choice. Introduce investor ownership across private funding arrangements, with public markets and founder funding as brief comparisons; distinguish new company cash from payments to existing owners.

## Audience

Product and engineering leaders inside companies working under investors, including leaders who inherit an ownership arrangement. Assume no specialist finance background; explain necessary terms before use. Investor-side readers are secondary.

## Success criteria

- Add one whole-post overview visual to the TL;DR after its opening paragraph, generated with the Nano Banana article illustrator. Keep the 300–500-word summary prose, bold emphasis and citations; provide alt text and a concise numbered caption.
- Provide a distinctive article header logo and a simple navigation icon with unique asset paths. Keep both consistent with the journal's visual style.
- Include two explanatory article figures with accurate short labels, alt text and numbered captions. Use restrained bold emphasis for key claims; preserve the words, citations and historical data. The comic modality is a sequence of comic pages: each page is one image of three stacked strips that carries its dialogue, labels and amounts in the artwork, with consistent fictional characters and a short caption under each page. Every string drawn in a page comes from the page script, which traces to the article, and generated artwork is inspected against the script (spelling, cast identity, which speaker each bubble points to, counts and sizes), not assumed.
- Open with Larkspur's €100,000 need and close it: show what the same amount permits under a customer prepayment, a loan and a new share issue, with one obligation per route that changes the delivery promise; state the chosen option, the alternatives rejected, the funding, the scarce capacity, who is authorized and the evidence that would reopen the decision.
- State that a budget gives the amount and the funding terms give timing, approvals and obligations; do not rank the amount below the terms.
- Explain four common sources of business money in terms a reader can hold: customers, lenders, owners, asset sales.
- Define share, stock exchange, public company and private company in passing; send IPO, audit and reporting-category detail to the glossary.
- Present venture, growth, buyout and corporate labels as orientation in one compact table and hand the consequences to the funding chapter.
- Keep historical findings and reading annotations within their source scope; label the Larkspur figures as fictional.
- Keep the summary and comic on the same argument as the article, including the completed decision; end with 3–4 questions and a curated reading list.
- Make the Larkspur example traceable: say what the €100,000 buys beyond salaries the existing subscriptions already pay, when the cash arrives and the work starts, how many engineers are assigned, how a late-delivery refund would be paid, and in what order customer orders, board approval and the CEO's countersignature happen, so that the zero-, one-, two- and three-customer cases can each be followed. State the loan as a fixed yearly rate with equal monthly payments; distinguish the funding gap, the amount actually borrowed and the most the surplus could repay; keep the investor's consent right to the spending it covers. State when the customers would pay under the loan route, separating the contractual payment deadline from the date Sam's forecast counts on (the deadline itself, with every repayment due before then provided for), and present the rejection of the full loan as Larkspur's stated policy (no fixed monthly repayment that existing income cannot cover, resting on uncollected customer money and the contingency reserve) rather than as arithmetic impossibility. Give the two-customer fallback a complete timeline as separate steps: affordability; the 16 January execution (board approval on 15 January, loan signed and orders countersigned, cash by 30 January, first repayment at the end of February); the 23 January latest execution (cash by 6 February, first repayment at the end of March, orders one week later, which the eight-engineer-week margin absorbs and which a larger estimate up to twelve engineer-weeks allows only if the CTO confirms the licence-dependent work and onboarding still fit 1 March); whether the engineers' 19 January start is authorized while financing is pending; and the replanning step if approval is late, declined or the fit is not confirmed. Say in every format that two customers keep the original release only if the board approves the loan, the loan and both orders are signed by the cutoff and the schedule still fits. Keep one event order in every format: build finished 13 February, launch 1 March, customer payment, first loan repayment at the end of the month after the money arrives. Assign the customers' baseline hosting and support to the existing running costs and the additional licence and capacity to the €100,000.
- Explain every finance term at first use in every format, including the summary and the comic, without relying on the glossary (public company, release and licence included; explain any label the summary artwork shows, such as the operating company, in its caption); place the public/private and investor-label orientation after the completed decision and keep the decision, its authority, the rejected alternatives and the reopening conditions in short separate paragraphs, one reasoning step per paragraph, with complete conditional sentences in the summary. The comic follows the same €100,000 through one page per route and keeps the offer, the choice and the fallback on separate pages: the prepayment page shows the refund consequence, the choice page shows why the loan and the share issue were declined and who may decide, and the final decision page shows the two checks made before anything is signed, including the effort-limit condition that reopens the date, and the two-customer fallback.
