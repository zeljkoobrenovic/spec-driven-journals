**Financing means arranging the money a company needs.** Understanding where that money comes from helps explain what the company must pay, provide or give up in return. Product and engineering leaders usually meet money as a budget; once investors are involved, the rights, repayment dates and expected sale attached to the money decide what that budget can be spent on and for how long.

Four common sources are customer payments, borrowing, money from owners and asset sales. Customers pay for a promised product or service; the company still has to deliver it. A lender supplies a loan whose borrowed amount, the **principal**, must be repaid under agreed terms. **Interest** is the charge for using that money. Owners supply money for **equity**, an ownership interest. Selling an asset, such as a building or product line, raises cash while giving up that asset. These are common routes, not the only ones; eligible grants can also provide funding.

A **share** is a unit of ownership. Holding 10 of 1,000 identical shares means holding 1% of those shares. Voting and payment rights depend on the share class and agreements. Owners may receive dividends, payments to shareholders, or sell their shares; neither a payment nor a gain is guaranteed.

The distinction between **new and existing shares** is essential. Buying new shares from a company can fund the company. Buying a founder’s existing shares pays the founder. An ownership announcement therefore does not establish a new product budget.

A **stock exchange** is an organized market for trading shares. A publicly traded company has shares available for public trading; a private company does not. Private companies can still have many owners and reporting obligations. An **initial public offering (IPO)** is the first public offering of a company’s shares and may raise money for the company, selling owners, or both. The book compares different investor arrangements. Venture funding can support uncertain learning; growth funding can support expansion. A **buyout** purchases control and may use substantial borrowing. A **corporate investor** is another operating business with financial or commercial aims. These are orientations, not guarantees of rights, support or time. Founder funding and public markets provide comparisons. A product leader must establish what money arrives, what changes in authority and which expectations the company can actually support.
