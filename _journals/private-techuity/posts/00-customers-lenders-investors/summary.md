**Financing means arranging the money a company needs.** Understanding where that money comes from helps explain what the company must pay, provide or give up in return. Product and engineering leaders usually meet money as a budget. A budget tells you how much you may spend; the funding terms tell you when you may spend it, which approvals you need and what obligations the company must meet. Affordability depends on both.

The chapter follows one fictional need. Larkspur, a scheduling-software company, needs €100,000 to finish a dispatch feature by 1 March and pay its team until customers pay. Four common sources could supply it: customer payments, borrowing, money from owners and asset sales. Customers pay for a promised product; the company still has to deliver it. A lender supplies a loan whose borrowed amount, the **principal**, must be repaid with **interest** under agreed terms. Owners supply money for **equity**, an ownership interest. Selling an asset raises cash once. Grants can also fund eligible work.

A **share** is a unit of ownership. Holding 10 of 1,000 identical shares means holding 1%; rights depend on the share class and agreements. The distinction between **new and existing shares** is essential. Buying new shares from a company can fund the company. Buying a founder’s existing shares pays the founder. An ownership announcement therefore does not establish a new product budget.

A **stock exchange** is an organized market for trading shares; trades between investors pay one another, not the company. A publicly traded company publishes financial information under the rules that apply to it; a private company’s shares do not trade publicly, but it can still have many shareholders and reporting obligations. [S55: Public-company reporting](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/how-stock-markets-work/public-companies) The [[glossary]] carries the detail on initial public offerings and audited statements.

Investor labels — venture, growth, buyout, corporate — are orientation, not terms. Each points to a different question: whether further funding depends on evidence, which rights the agreement contains, how much cash must service borrowing, or which group entity funds future work. [[raise-what-you-need]] compares the arrangements against the work to be funded.

**The same €100,000 three ways.** A customer prepayment turns the 1 March date into a contract term with a refund if it slips. A bank loan starts repayments of about €3,000 a month before the feature earns anything. New shares for about 10% of the company add a board seat, a consent right and the expectation of a sale. Larkspur chose the prepayment: the feature fits in eight engineer-weeks before 1 March, the board approved the contracts and the CEO signed them. The loan was rejected because repayments would arrive before customer cash; the share issue because it gives up rights that outlast the feature. If fewer than three customers sign by 15 January, or the estimate exceeds twelve engineer-weeks, the loan becomes the fallback.
