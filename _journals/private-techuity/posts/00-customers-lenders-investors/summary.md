**Financing means arranging the money a company needs.** The source decides what the company must pay, provide or give up in return. Product and engineering leaders usually meet money as a budget: how much they may spend. The funding terms add when, with which approvals and what the company must deliver. Affordability depends on both.

Larkspur, a small fictional scheduling-software company with no outside investor yet, needs €100,000 to finish a dispatch feature by 1 March and onboard the customers who want it. Four common sources could supply it: customer payments, borrowing, money from owners and asset sales. Customers pay for a product the company still has to deliver. A lender, an investor too, supplies a loan whose **principal** must be repaid with **interest**. Owners supply money for **equity**, an ownership interest. An asset sale raises cash once; grants can fund eligible work.

A **share** is a unit of ownership. Holding 10 of 1,000 identical shares is 1%; rights depend on share class and agreements. Buying **new shares** from a company can fund the company; buying a founder’s **existing shares** pays the founder. An ownership announcement does not establish a product budget.

A **stock exchange** is an organized market for trading shares; trades between investors pay one another, not the company. A publicly traded company publishes financial information under its rules; a private company’s shares do not trade publicly, yet it can still have many shareholders and reporting obligations. [S55: Public-company reporting](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/how-stock-markets-work/public-companies) The [[glossary]] covers initial public offerings and audited statements.

Investor labels — venture, growth, buyout, corporate — are orientation, not terms. Each points to a question: whether further funding depends on evidence, which rights the agreement contains, how much cash must service borrowing, or which group entity funds the work. [[raise-what-you-need]] compares them against the work to be funded.

**The same €100,000 three ways.** A customer prepayment turns the 1 March date into a contract term with a one-month refund if it slips. A bank loan starts repayments of about €3,040 a month before the feature earns anything. New shares, 111 added to 1,000 existing, give an investor about 10% plus a board seat, a consent right and the expectation of a sale. Larkspur chose the prepayment: the feature fits in eight engineer-weeks before 1 March, and the contracts sit within the CEO’s delegated authority; she still took the comparison to the board. The loan was rejected because its repayments are twice the €1,500 a month that existing subscriptions leave spare; the share issue because it gives up rights that outlast the feature. If fewer than three customers sign by 15 January, or the estimate exceeds twelve engineer-weeks, the commitment pauses and the release is reforecast to what signed customers’ cash funds; any loan is limited to what that surplus can repay, about €35,000, and needs board approval.

Begin every funding conversation by asking **who receives the cash**, **what obligations accompany it** and who can approve its use. The next chapter asks the same of a €100 million announcement: [[announcement-is-not-a-budget]].
