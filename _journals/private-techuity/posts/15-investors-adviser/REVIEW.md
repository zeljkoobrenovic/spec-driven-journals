# Editorial review: Is the Investor's Adviser Helping, Assessing or Deciding?

Reviewed 14 September 2026. Read the full article, summary and reader-facing comic text.

## Overall assessment

The chapter helps a company leader distinguish an investor adviser's assessment, advice, coaching, oversight and delivery responsibilities. Its strongest contribution is the tension between informal influence and explicit authority. The opening planning meeting and assignment table make that tension understandable without assuming the adviser is hostile.

It overlaps heavily with governance and the other two support chapters. Availability, same-facts reporting, internal capacity and handover receive another full treatment, while the most distinctive and difficult issue—an adviser simultaneously coaching and assessing the CTO—remains largely a request to agree boundaries.

## Detailed feedback

- **Introduction and precision:** The meeting scene is effective. “No such person exists” without an investor is true only of the narrowly defined investor employee; it does not establish a unique kind of influence. “Information and influence a supplier or consultant wouldn't have” is an unsupported categorical contrast. A board-appointed consultant can also have privileged access and multiple responsibilities. Explain the actual reporting and incentive relationship instead.
- **Organization and flow:** The role-specific caveat appropriately prevents “Technology Principal” becoming a universal job description. The five-assignment table is the chapter's best organizing material. Subsequent sections largely reproduce an engagement charter in prose, shifting attention away from how the leader recognizes and handles a change of role.
- **Terminology and audience:** The title foregrounds helping, assessing and deciding; the table includes five overlapping assignments. Make clear that deciding is authority that may attach to an assignment, rather than a sixth kind of help. Introduce “investor's technology adviser” as the general term and retain “Technology Principal” for this book's supplied example.
- **Examples and pacing:** The three-week billing-code example is concrete but already used in the engineering and capability chapters. This article needs a scene only it can teach: a coaching discussion becomes a board assessment, or the adviser is formally asked to lead delivery.
- **Evidence and tone:** The private role brief supports the existence of one role, not the effectiveness of the proposed relationship or claims about all advisers. The article largely respects this boundary. Preserve its reciprocal expectation that both the adviser and company leader can revise their judgment. Avoid presenting a professional coaching code as automatically binding on an adviser who has not adopted it.
- **Conclusion:** “Judge What the Company Can Do Better” is sound but repeats the following capability chapter's thesis. End instead with the agreed purpose and authority of the opening meeting.
- **Other formats:** Summary and comic introductions repeat the unsupported consultant contrast. Comic panels 1 and 6 ask almost the same role question; one could show a role change and its consequence.

## Recommended changes

- **High impact — Make dual-role conflict the central case.** Show what Alex can ask before sharing sensitive information, what Morgan must explain, who can authorize a changed assignment and how employees are informed. Do not promise confidentiality beyond the agreed obligations.
- **High impact — Correct the uniqueness claim across formats.** Suggested rewrite: “This adviser reports to the investment firm, so the company needs to understand both the operating assignment and how findings may inform the investor's decisions.”
- **Medium impact — Move the engagement mechanics to “Useful Engagement.”** Retain the distinction between advice and delivery; shorten availability, internal resources, ongoing service and handover to links. Let this chapter own purpose, influence, information use and role changes.
- **Medium impact — Use the assignment table as a diagnostic.** Add a column or short example showing what employees should hear: “recommendation,” “assessment finding,” or “authorized instruction.” This translates governance into the conversation the opening raises.
- **Low impact — Consolidate repeated comic panels and responsibility terms.** Replace “Who owns this decision?” with “Who is authorized to decide?” where share ownership might be confused with accountability.

## Proposed structure

The ambiguous planning meeting → identify assignment and reporting relationship → distinguish influence from authority → work through coaching/assessment or interim-delivery conflict → communicate the agreed role → link to support selection and engagement design. Preserve this as a separate chapter if its distinctive role conflict is developed.
