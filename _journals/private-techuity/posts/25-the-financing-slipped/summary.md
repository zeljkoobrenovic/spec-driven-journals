Larkspur, a fictional scheduling-software company, expected €4 million of new investment on 15 September; it now looks likely in mid-December. It is a **round**: investors put in money and receive **shares**, stakes in the company’s ownership, at **closing**, when the money arrives. **The work did not slip; a funding assumption did.**

First, name the money’s stage: an expression of interest (a non-binding wish to invest), approval pending, a signed commitment with conditions, or cash received. Only the last can be spent; in July the round is at the first.

Next, put cash on a calendar: €1.6 million on 1 July and a monthly **burn** (cash paid out minus cash collected) of €200,000. The board of directors wants at least €400,000 kept, the **reserve**. Every alternative funds customer contracts, including restoring the software that assigns field staff to jobs within four hours of a failure.

Two hires (€30,000 a month together) and a €120,000 self-service customer portal can be stopped until 15 August, the **latest useful decision date**; offers and the portal contract are due 16 August.

Proceeding as planned (A) breaches the reserve in late November; deferring both (B) holds it until 31 December. C adds a **bridge**: a €600,000 short-term loan from the existing investors that turns into shares when the round closes. C reaches 31 March only if the money arrives by 30 November and no **interest** (the charge for borrowing) or repayment is paid in cash before it falls due a year later.

On 10 August the board chooses **B now**. Sam, the chief financial officer, must secure a signed bridge agreement by 1 September. **Reduction 1 (R1)**, not renewing outside contract engineers, saves €50,000 a month from 1 November. Its notice goes out on 1 October, securing November’s saving, unless received cash makes it unnecessary.

A **term sheet**, a summary of proposed terms, does not count as incoming cash in Larkspur’s forecast; a signed commitment with conditions may be counted if the board allows, but not spent. A new hire or contract may start only if cash already received, with its salaries or payments included, stays at or above the €400,000 reserve until 30 June, allowing two more three-month slips. That forecast leaves out unreceived investment and loans but keeps the burn’s customer payments and costs. With R1, cash reaches the reserve about 20 January, or 20 May with the bridge’s cash; both fail, so **the offers and portal wait until Sam confirms the round’s cash has arrived**.

If money fails, a deeper cut needs its own board decision. At the terms Larkspur later costs, its leaving payments come before its savings, so it cannot prevent the breach without financing. Without the bridge, the next step (another lender or a sale) is undecided. If the bridge arrives but the round fails, the board must decide in December how much Larkspur can keep spending and how to repay or renegotiate the loan. No customer promise depends on the hires; the team knows it is executing B.
