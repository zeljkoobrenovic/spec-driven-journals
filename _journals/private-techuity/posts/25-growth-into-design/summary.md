**A valuation assumption is a belief used when estimating company value**, such as expected sales growth. An investor’s growth assumption has reached fictional Larkspur’s team as a request for flexibility, with the reasoning left behind. Recover the business requirement before comparing designs.

“Valued on growth” is incomplete until it says which customers, what they need and **what it will cost to serve them**. Larkspur’s recovered requirement: invoice, contract with, support and set up customers in a second country under its tax and pricing rules, with **the first customer payments received within twelve months**, and each further country cheaper to add than the last, because the investor’s plan assumes a third.

Earlier chapters found the constraint and relieved the queue around it: the country-specific rules are written into the same code as the rest of the invoicing module, so a change for one country can disturb everything else, and only two specialists can safely make one.

An **implementation choice** is how the company meets a requirement in its technology. **Modularity** — dividing software into parts that each have a clear job — can make selected changes easier, without splitting the software into many separately run services, which is a different decision. Judge dependence on one supplier or one person by its likely impact against the cost of the alternatives, not by how rarely it bites.

Three fictional options are compared against the same requirement, date and a €300,000 approved budget for additional spending (a scenario separate from the hundred-day plan), on one basis: first-year cash, then the recurring cost each year after. Dates are **first invoice** dates; payment follows about a month later, and the payment must land inside twelve months.

| Option | Additional cash | First invoice | What it leaves behind |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **A. Buy a supplier’s service** | €60,000 setup plus €40,000 a year, per country | Month four to five | The country rules stay tangled into the existing code, and each country adds a second billing route for finance to **reconcile** — compare the two sets of records every month and explain any difference |
| **B. Keep the rules ourselves** | €140,000 once, then no supplier fee; upkeep comes out of the billing engineer’s already funded time | Month eight | The rules are separated, so each later country is a settings change. Costs six **specialist-weeks** (six weeks of work by the two people who can safely change the billing code — effort, not elapsed time) and risks breaking invoices that already work |
| **C. Replace the system underneath** | €1.3 million, several times over budget | About month twelve | Broadest capability, but too late and too costly. It stays in as the benchmark, not a candidate |

Larkspur chooses **B**. It costs €80,000 more upfront, and the two options are level at two subscription years (€140,000 each): A is cheaper over one year, B from the third onwards. What decides it is **how long the design will be used**, not the second country or a third. The investor expects to hold its ownership share for about four more years, so on that horizon A’s repeating subscription reaches €220,000 against B’s €140,000 — B is cheaper even if no third country arrives. The expected third country is a separate reason pointing the same way: it would add A’s setup fee and another €40,000 a year, bringing B’s advantage forward. The choice also assumes the specialist time holds, and that last year’s invoices can be **replayed** — rerun through the new code and checked line by line against the bills actually sent — to catch mistakes before a customer sees one.

Until the replay passes, the existing module stays the approved source of every bill. A is the standby plan, priced with its yearly subscription, payable from the money left over and staffed by the billing engineer plus two protected specialist-weeks; if that time cannot be freed, the launch is deferred rather than the date assumed.

Alex, who leads technology, is answerable for delivery; Priya, who leads product, for the requirement being met; and Ines, the chief executive, authorizes the spending inside the budget the board set. Three signals are written down in advance, each calling for a different response:

* **Switch** to A on either of two signals: the month-four replay shows differences the specialists cannot explain within two weeks, or incidents have eaten the specialist time B depends on. Switching is not automatic relief — A still needs two specialist-weeks to check the invoice data it hands the supplier, and a supplier schedule that reaches customer payment inside twelve months. If either is missing, the launch is deferred rather than the date assumed.
* **Defer** the second-country launch if fewer than five customers have signed by month six, while the work continues for the first country.
* **Reassess** — not switch automatically — if the third country leaves the investor’s plan, judging the money and time still ahead. Once €80,000 is spent, finishing B costs €60,000 against €100,000 to start A, so finishing is the cheaper answer whatever the horizon.

A **separate illustration**, not part of that decision. **When the cash comes back:** €200,000 spent now avoids €100,000 of cost a year, but only once the work is finished a year later. Adding the savings as they arrive, the €200,000 comes back about three years after it went out. That is **simple payback**, and it ignores that money arriving later is worth less today.

**Then beware the bigger number.** Businesses like Larkspur are often valued at a multiple of **EBITDA** — earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization. It is a profit measure that deliberately leaves out four things: the cost of borrowing, income taxes, and two accounting charges that spread the cost of things bought earlier — a building, a server, purchased software — across the years they are used. Leaving them out describes the trading business on its own, but it is not cash available to spend; a company can report healthy EBITDA in a year when its bank balance falls. If the saved €100,000 raises EBITDA by that full amount every year, and the buyer still values the company at ten times EBITDA, the arithmetic gives €1 million of extra estimated value for the **operating business** — the whole business as a going concern, of which lenders have first claim on a fixed amount and the owners keep whatever remains. It is not cash anyone receives, and it is larger than the value of the owners' shares, because the lenders' claim must come out of it first. Each of those conditions can fail; the number shows how an estimate moves when one input moves.

**Do not add the two.** Valuing the savings by what those future €100,000s are worth today, and valuing them through the €1 million multiple, are two ways of pricing the same saving. Summing them counts one benefit twice.
