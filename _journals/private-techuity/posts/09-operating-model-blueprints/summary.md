**Choose investor support from the work and the capability missing from your company.** An operating model arranges people, responsibilities, decisions and information; a blueprint makes the investor's contribution and the company's commitments specific enough to test.

Start with the business priority: growth; profitability, meaning the business earns more than the relevant costs; buying other businesses; or scaling, so the company handles more work without the founder deciding everything. Translate the investment thesis—why the investment should succeed—into company work. The same priority can need different support, depending on what blocks progress.

The chapter proposes **four blueprints that can be combined**. **Management delivery with board oversight** fits a capable team with a funded plan and clear approval routes; the board is the group of directors that governs the company. **An operating adviser** brings experience and challenge when management can implement the decision. **Shared specialists** supply scarce expertise through named assignments, dates and costs. **Dedicated execution inside the company** adds sustained leadership, with a reporting line, delegated decisions and a handover condition. These are adaptable arrangements, with no ranking.

Choose **company organization separately**: local delivery, common standards and shared services can coexist. A shared service is one team or system doing a task — running sign-in, say — for several businesses instead of each doing it alone. Sharing expertise across an investor's portfolio—the businesses it has invested in—differs from integrating functions inside a corporate group, where one parent company controls its subsidiaries and can direct them. Sharing an investor does not by itself create a common management structure or give one portfolio company authority over another, and common ownership alone does not permit pooling customer data. Establish the benefit, service owner, funding and information access for each shared activity.

Write one agreement covering purpose, accountable leaders, decision rights, people, funding, displaced work, evidence and review. Advice, assessment, approval and executive direction need distinct scopes. A specialist's availability helps only when company employees have time to work with them. Include implementation and continuing service costs alongside any adviser fees.

In the **independent fictional example**, customers of three software businesses sign in twice to use two products and want one sign-in and one help desk. The businesses keep their own product plans and trial a shared sign-in service. A specialist commits six weeks; a company program leader commits twelve. Two engineers each reserve one day weekly through those six weeks—twelve engineer-days—with other work deferred and the cost already inside the approved budget, the financial plan the board signed off. The technology officer approves live customer use; a named service owner, the manager of an existing support team, then runs the service and answers its customers.

At six weeks, the trial works, but moving every customer onto it would take the engineers promised to a customer release. The group chief executive approves a smaller step within the agreed authority: the same two engineers, one day weekly, weeks seven to ten, running the service for two volunteer customers inside the approved budget, with the same internal improvements still deferred. The specialist is not extended. The trial is approved to week ten and closes there: both customers return to their original sign-ins, which takes about one of those eight reserved engineer-days.

The board receives a revised forecast—the current estimate, as against what the budget assumed—moving general availability, when the offer is open to every intended customer, from the last quarter of this financial year into the first quarter of the next, and the expected **revenue** with it. Revenue is the income a company earns by supplying its products or services, counted when it delivers them rather than when the money arrives. That date is conditional: it assumes the wider migration is approved and funded at the twelve-week review, which has not happened. At twelve weeks, management reviews whether to end, narrow or make the assignment permanent; each needs its own approval and resources, and weeks eleven and twelve carry no build work and no customer service. **Technical progress informs the next commitment; it establishes neither revenue actually earned nor the investor's return, the gain or loss on the money put in.**
