# Spec: Working With Investors: Operating Model Blueprints

## Intent

Open Part IV with a chapter based on `_drafts/operating-models/INPUT-1.md` and `INPUT-2.md`. Help a product or technology leader choose and agree a workable relationship between investor involvement and company delivery. Combine business priorities and forms of investor involvement in a practical selection guide. Make blueprints usable through their decisions, people, funding, review practices and conditions for change.

## Audience

Product and engineering leaders inside investor-backed companies; chief executives, finance leaders and operating partners are secondary readers. Assume no finance or investment background. Expand every executive abbreviation at first use, and explain operating model, investment thesis, value creation plan, shared service, federated arrangements, private equity, portfolio, diligence, subsidiary, budget, forecast, revenue, profit, cash, runway and the technical terms of the worked example (platform, pilot, migration, rollout, production acceptance, shared identity, roadmap) in plain language at their first meaningful use. Carry the same essential explanations into the summary and the comic, each of which must read independently. Use private-equity evidence with brief, qualified venture and growth comparisons.

## Success criteria

- Hand off to [[learn-through-investors-network]] before sourcing a specific capability gap; keep the existing operating-model argument and all artwork.

- Distinguish the investor’s own internal operations, its relationship with portfolio companies, and the company’s internal organization. Concentrate on the latter two.
- Present four proposed blueprints: management delivery with board oversight, an operating adviser, shared specialists, and dedicated execution inside the company. They are combinations to adapt, with no universal ranking or maturity ladder.
- Provide a selection guide covering growth, profitability, acquisitions and scaling. For each priority, connect the missing capability to possible investor contributions and the company's internal design; explain why the same priority can need different combinations.
- For each blueprint, explain suitable conditions, decision rights, delivery capacity, funding, review evidence, failure signs and a condition for changing or ending the arrangement. Include concrete product or technology consequences.
- Treat local autonomy, common standards, shared services and integration as a separate design choice. Distinguish sharing expertise across a fund’s portfolio from integrating functions inside an acquired corporate group.
- Tie formal authority to the actual appointment, delegation and governing arrangements. Separate advice, assessment, approval and executive direction; make company and investor commitments visible.
- Include a reusable blueprint record, a suggested review rhythm and one explicitly fictional acquisition example independent of Larkspur’s existing scenarios. State what is funded, what company capacity is used and what changes at review. Every activity between week one and week twelve has a named person and a stated funding arrangement; name the role that approves live customer use, distinguish it from the continuing service owner, and make any extension or permanent capability conditional on renewed approval and resources. Do not imply specialist availability after week six or a guaranteed delivery date. The volunteer-customer trial runs weeks seven to ten and closes at week ten, with both customers returned to their original sign-ins inside the same eight reserved engineer-days and the deferred internal improvements held through week ten; weeks eleven and twelve carry no customer service and no build work, only preparation of the next proposal. No week-ten obligation may depend on the later twelve-week decision, and all three formats state the same sequence.
- Explain where AI affects capability, data access, implementation and accountability without making a common tool or specialist team a universal prescription. Link the technology operating partner chapter and existing AI, hiring, governance and acquisition chapters.
- Review the supplied links directly, identify the sections actually consulted, and separate source observations from the author’s blueprint designs. Date the 2018 survey and current firm examples; do not infer causal performance, universal board behavior, promised savings or current market prevalence from them.
- Preserve the book’s learning objective, three KEY POINTS, questions and further reading. Aim for 3,000–4,000 words of article prose, readable captions, and no more than two substantial tables. Where the plain-language requirement in *Audience* conflicts with that ceiling, the explanations win: after the September 2026 reviews the article stands at about 5,900 words, with the byline unchanged at 22 minutes.
- Add a chapter logo, navigation icon and seven explanatory figures in the book’s ivory, navy, muted teal and ochre style. Retain the connected-model, support-choice and pilot-review figures. Add one distinct visual within each of the four blueprint sections: board oversight with company-led delivery; an adviser supporting a company decision; a booked specialist assignment with a company owner; and a dedicated company assignment with agreed reporting and handover. Depict advice and shared expertise without implying additional executive authority. Use accurate labels, descriptive alt text and numbered captions; name the fourth blueprint consistently across the prose and the figures; preserve generation prompts and provenance. Where a figure or comic page letters an executive abbreviation, the surrounding prose, caption or alt text expands it.
- Place the article immediately after the Part IV introduction, before `learn-through-investors-network`. End with a transition to learning through the investor’s network. Retain the permalink and artwork, number its source folder `09-operating-model-blueprints`, and align the reading guide, contents and manuscript with 34 main chapters.
- Add a TL;DR and a five-page illustrated comic. The TL;DR targets 300–500 words, but the plain-language requirement in *Audience* wins where the two conflict: after the round-3 review it stands at about 740 words, because it must explain profitability, revenue, shared services, budget, forecast, investor return and the trial's closure and forecast conditions to a reader who sees only this page. Both preserve the distinction between investor support and company organization, all four proposed blueprints, explicit authority and committed capacity, and the fictional pilot's six-week and twelve-week reviews. The comic uses the journal's familiar cast to act out this independent example, with three strips per page, accurate dialogue and labels in the artwork, descriptive alt text, short captions and rendered transcripts. Alt text describes a prospective capacity conflict as prospective, never as an obstruction that already happened. Preserve the scripts and image provenance; inspect every generated page against its script.

## Non-goals

A fund-administration manual, company-wide reorganization instructions, a jurisdiction-specific governance document, an investment-return model or a replacement for the existing engagement charter. Do not assume that all portfolio companies form a corporate group or can share customer data. Do not repeat the main chapters’ financial calculations or restore the discontinued Productscapes appendix.

## Modalities

- Article: the existing illustrated draft, with its chapter logo, navigation icon and seven explanatory figures.
- TL;DR: `summary.md`, a self-contained 300–500-word summary in the journal's concise prose style, reusing the support-choice overview after the opening paragraph.
- Comic: `comics.md`, five illustrated pages of three strips each, following the independent fictional identity pilot from the proposal through its review and possible handover. The page scripts are the source for the artwork, captions and transcripts.
