Durable success requires **more than a favorable funding valuation** (a company’s estimated worth when raising investment) or exit price. Can the company keep serving customers, fund its obligations, reinvest and explain what is unfinished? An exit, the investor’s sale, is decisive for some investors but not their only horizon; customers, employees and suppliers live with the company afterwards.

In a fictional Larkspur decision, thirty customers use an old **onboarding** (customer setup) path where one specialist maintains their settings by hand; migration moves them to the newer setup process. Ending that path at the close of month seven would remove about €220,000 a year: €150,000 in cash (the specialist’s fully loaded cost: pay plus other employment costs) and €70,000 of a support engineer’s time, freed capacity, not cash. Several contracts run past that date, so the cut-off is a breach, not an option. Consent-based early migration cannot remove the specialist’s cash cost until the last customer leaves the path, though support time may free up earlier.

The board chooses a migration in months seven to twelve. It starts only if the next customer group passes both month-six tests: average setup effort of 50 hours or less including all data work, and a median (middle) wait of eight weeks or less, from contract signing to first scheduled real work in the product. Unfinished results postpone it; a failed test stops the start. The specialist stays full-time to month twelve (five extra months, €62,500); a funded half-time data service at €75,000 a year then cuts cash cost by €75,000 if they accept. Month twelve is a target: accounts still on the path use the half-time hours, or funded full-time months (€6,250 each above plan) cut the saving. The disagreement is reviewed at month nine (fewer than fifteen migrated reopens it) and recorded for the next owner.

Wider research tests an investor-only view. Two years after **buyouts** (purchases giving the buyer control), employment averaged about 12% lower for stock-market-listed companies and 15% higher for private ones, against similar companies: averages, not forecasts. [S08: Davis et al., 2024 revision](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w26371/w26371.pdf) Nursing-home and hospital studies report harmful patient outcomes in specific populations; another finds some quality gains; neither cancels the other. [S11: Gupta et al., nursing homes](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w28474/w28474.pdf) [S12: Kannan et al., adverse events](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/2813379) [S39: Bruch et al., hospital measures](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/fullarticle/2769549) Eighteen hospitals resold to **private equity** owners (firms buying companies with investors’ pooled money) had lower operating margins (sales income left after running costs, before financing costs and tax) than 18 sold to other for-profit owners: a small, financial, not clinical, result. [S42: Kannan and Song, after sale](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama-health-forum/fullarticle/2837043) Where customers cannot judge quality or switch, incentives may not protect them.

When the standard does not settle a question, name who decides, who loses, which funded steps reduce harm and when it is reviewed, so the next team inherits a company that can serve customers and fund its obligations.
