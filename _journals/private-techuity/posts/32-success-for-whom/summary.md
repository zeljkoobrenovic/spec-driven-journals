Durable success requires **more than a favorable funding valuation** or exit price: a funding valuation is the estimate of what a company is worth when it raises investment, and an exit is the sale that turns value into money received. The book asks whether the company can keep serving customers, fund its obligations, reinvest and explain what is unfinished. An exit is decisive for some investors, not their only horizon; customers, employees and suppliers live with the company afterwards.

A fictional Larkspur decision makes the trade-off visible. Thirty customers still run on an old **onboarding** path, the way new customers are set up, where one specialist enters and maintains their settings by hand; migration moves them onto the newer setup process. Ending the old path at the close of month seven would remove about €220,000 a year: €150,000 of the specialist’s fully loaded cost (pay plus the other costs of employing someone), which is cash, and €70,000 of a support engineer’s time, which is freed capacity. Several contracts run past that date, so the cut-off is a breach, not an option. In a consent-based early migration, the specialist’s cash cost cannot be removed until the last customer has left the path; support time may be freed earlier.

The board, the directors who oversee the company’s major decisions, chooses a migration in months seven to twelve. It starts only if the next group of customers passes both month-six tests: average setup effort of 50 hours or less including all data work, and a median (middle) wait of eight weeks or less, counted from contract signing to the customer first scheduling real work in the product. Unfinished customers that could change the result postpone the decision; a failed test stops the start. The specialist stays full-time to month twelve (five extra months, about €62,500), then becomes a funded half-time data service at €75,000 a year, cutting cash cost by €75,000 a year if the specialist accepts. Month twelve is a target: accounts still on the path are served from the half-time hours, or funded full-time months (€6,250 each above plan) cut the saving. The disagreement and its review evidence (fewer than fifteen customers migrated by month nine) go into the handover record.

Wider research tests an investor-only view. Davis and colleagues find two-year employment effects of about −12% after **buyouts** (purchases giving the buyer control) of stock-market-listed companies and +15% after buyouts of already private ones, averages against similar companies, not forecasts. [S08: Davis et al., 2024 revision](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w26371/w26371.pdf) Nursing-home and hospital studies report harmful patient outcomes in specific populations; another finds gains on some quality measures, and different outcomes cannot cancel. [S11: Gupta et al., nursing homes](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w28474/w28474.pdf) [S12: Kannan et al., adverse events](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/2813379) [S39: Bruch et al., hospital measures](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/fullarticle/2769549) Eighteen hospitals sold a second time to **private equity** owners (investment firms that buy companies with money pooled from their investors) showed lower operating margins than 18 hospitals sold to other for-profit owners; operating margin is the share of sales income left after running costs, before financing costs and tax. The sample is small, and the result is financial, not a patient outcome. [S42: Kannan and Song, after sale](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama-health-forum/fullarticle/2837043) Where customers cannot observe quality or switch providers, financial incentives may not protect them.

When the standard does not settle a question, name the authorized decision-maker, the groups that lose, the funded steps that reduce harm and the review date. The next team should inherit a company that can serve its customers, fund its obligations and explain what still needs to change.
