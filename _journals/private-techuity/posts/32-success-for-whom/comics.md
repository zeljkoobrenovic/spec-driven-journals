**Comic.** An exit, a sale through which an investor receives value, is a decisive reporting event for some investors, not the only horizon for judging the work: product and engineering leaders, customers, employees and suppliers live with the company through and after each funding or ownership event. Seven pages put the book’s five cases side by side in four columns, set the proposed standard of durable success beside the investor’s result, follow one final fictional Larkspur decision about thirty customers on an old onboarding path — the manual way one specialist sets customers up on the product and keeps their settings working — from the option that breaks their contracts, through the feasible ones, to the chosen transition onto the newer setup process, funded, dated and reviewed, and end with what wider research adds and what a company leader can change. Each page is three strips; the figures and conditions are drawn into the artwork, and a transcript follows every page.

Morgan is an investor’s adviser; Alex leads technology, Priya leads product, Sam leads finance as chief financial officer, and Ines is the chief executive. All are fictional, and so are the Larkspur figures. The five case figures are as their chapters report them, in each source’s currency. Historical cases are discussed through documents; the scenes do not reenact real events.

**Page 7: What wider studies add, and what a leader can change.** The cases are five histories; research on many buyouts tests whether an investor-only view of success is enough, comparing businesses bought with similar businesses that were not — statistical adjustment improves the comparison but does not remove every difference, and investors choose which companies to buy. Davis and colleagues’ 2024 revision, on United States buyouts from 1980 to 2013, reports two-year employment effects of approximately −12% for public-to-private buyouts and +15% for private-to-private ones relative to comparison groups: estimated average differences, not predictions for an individual company, and a reason to identify who loses work and who gains it. Bernstein, Lerner and Mezzanotti find greater investment around the 2008 crisis among UK firms owned by private equity investors than among comparison firms. Healthcare evidence is the sharper test because it shows outcomes earnings cannot represent: Gupta and colleagues report higher death rates for a subset of nursing-home patients after private equity ownership; Kannan, Bruch and Song report 4.6 more hospital-acquired conditions per 10,000 Medicare stays at acquired hospitals, an adjusted excess against comparison hospitals equal to 25.4% of the pre-acquisition rate, not a 25-point rise in any patient’s risk, while an earlier study found improvements on some other measures. For a software company the relevance is a mechanism, not an analogy: where customers cannot readily observe quality or change providers, financial incentives may not protect them, and the question is who bears the cost of reduced service and whether they can respond. As a leader you cannot control the price of investment or remove every conflict; you can explain what the company can deliver and needs, expose unfunded dependencies, propose feasible alternatives, keep a record of outcomes and bring decisions to the people authorized to make them.
