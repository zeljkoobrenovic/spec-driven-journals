# Editorial review: TeamSystem: Each New Owner Buys the Last Owner’s Progress

Reviewed 14 September 2026. Read the complete article, summary and reader-facing comic text.

## Overall assessment

The chapter follows selected TeamSystem ownership episodes to show that each investor starts with a different business, price and inherited workload. Product and engineering leaders gain a useful way to separate transaction boundaries from continuing development, integration and customer obligations. The comparison of statutory and pro forma periods is especially effective.

Two important repairs are needed. The opening promises to explain how positive adjusted earnings coexist with a statutory loss, but the body gives the gap without completing its component-by-component explanation. A further-reading annotation also treats a 2024 announced sale valuation as realized cash and links it to a 2016 retained stake more conclusively than the cited announcement permits.

## Detailed feedback

- **Title and introduction:** The title is memorable, but “progress” covers only half the argument: owners inherit unfinished work and costs too. The opening's general claim that buy-and-build “is set by investors and financed largely with debt” is broader than the transaction-specific evidence. Describe the documented case or say such strategies can create these conditions. The summary and comic repeat the same generalization.
- **Organization and flow:** Distinct ownership episodes, reported interventions, partial exit and later financial statements form a sound sequence. Keep the separation between manager accounts and independently established facts. A small timeline would reduce the effort of tracking owners, announcement dates and completion dates.
- **Missing explanatory payoff:** “Profitable and Loss-Making in the Same Year” gives adjusted EBITDA of €113.010m and a net loss of €56.788m, leaving €169.798m to explain. Naming possible categories and directing readers to a reconciliation is weaker than showing the actual reported components. This is missing context, not evidence that the figures are wrong. Complete the bridge from the report before interpreting why the gap matters.
- **Examples and technical clarity:** The difference between apparent 37.7% growth and a roughly 8.9% comparable full-year calculation is a strong lesson. Preserve the warning that the latter is still not an organic-growth calculation. The distinction between capitalized development and cash spending, and between a retained stake and cash proceeds, is also well taught.
- **Confirmed source-status error:** The July 2024 annotation says the retained interest “finally became a cash figure of about £24.3 million.” The [8 July announcement](https://www.hgcapitaltrust.com/news-insights/news/2024/08-07-2024) reports an agreed sale and a value for HgCapital Trust's investment; it does not establish receipt of cash. Correct that status. Separately, the continuity of the precise 2016 interest through 2024 needs verification, including the [2021 investment announcement](https://www.hgcapitaltrust.com/news-insights/news/2021/18-01-2021-131229553). The existence of that later investment does not itself prove the stakes differ, but it prevents assuming an uninterrupted simple bridge.
- **Tone and repetition:** “What Remains Unknown About Customers and Employees?” is fair. The next two sections restate inherited obligations and incomplete evidence. Combine the practical implications, retaining one explicit distinction between observed company history and proposed leadership practice.
- **Ending and companions:** The ending's accumulation of products, knowledge, dependencies and continuing work is stronger than a generic return warning. Use it as the final sentence. The summary appropriately declines to compare different owners' reported multiples, but should include the main financial bridge once completed; it currently repeats the promise more than the explanation.

## Recommended changes

- **High impact — Correct the 2024 further-reading annotation.** Describe an announced transaction value, with completion and stake continuity unverified. Do not claim it closes the 2016 realization question without a documented cash-and-holdings history. This is essential because the current annotation contradicts the chapter's own principal lesson.
- **High impact — Complete the adjusted-earnings-to-loss bridge.** Add the actual reported adjustment, depreciation/amortization, financing, tax and other relevant lines in their proper order. Reconcile to the exact loss measure and distinguish noncash charges from cash needs. Do not invent a bridge from generic categories.
- **Medium impact — Make ownership episodes easier to follow.** Use a short table with owner, documented event, date/status and what the evidence establishes. Keep mixed currencies and reported multiples out of a synthetic performance ranking.
- **Medium impact — Narrow the opening claim.** Suggested rewrite: “Each ownership change creates a new financial starting point. Product work carries on across it: migrations, integrations and dependencies remain somebody's responsibility.”
- **Low impact — Consider “Each New Owner Inherits Progress and Unfinished Work.”** This optional title revision better represents the full conclusion. Retain the current title if the opening immediately supplies its missing half.

## Proposed structure

Keep one case: ownership timeline → documented interventions → partial-exit cash and retained value → comparable-period growth → complete earnings-to-loss bridge → development and integration funding → obligations handed to the next owner. Give Visma ownership of the recurring-acquisition-adjustment lesson; use a brief cross-reference here so TeamSystem can concentrate on reporting periods and inherited work.
