# Spec: Headcount Is Not Capacity: Trace the Work Before You Hire

## Intent

Turn investor growth, earnings and integration expectations into a credible team plan by letting a diagnosis produce the staffing decision. Start from the finding in [[can-the-team-deliver]], trace one fictional Larkspur pricing change through its queue, separate decision, knowledge and staffing constraints, change the decisions first, and show whether hiring proceeds, changes shape or is deferred. This chapter is the book’s home for leadership assessment (the leadership-change hypothesis absorbed from [[different-bets]], combined with the role assessment, including a case where replacement or added leadership is justified) and for investor involvement in key appointments: proposing a role, introducing candidates, influencing selection, approving compensation and assessing incumbents. Work one fictional proposal to appoint a chief product officer through recruitment, developing the existing leader and adding narrower support, and decide it. Keep the full-cost location comparison and the knowledge-transfer test. Reductions are handed to [[anatomy-of-a-layoff]].

## Audience

Product and engineering leaders inside companies working under investors, including leaders who inherit an ownership arrangement. Assume no specialist finance background; explain necessary terms before use. Investor-side readers are secondary.

Keep every existing qualification. Present the leadership counterexample as evidence, interpretation and remedy. Keep each table cell scannable: the reversing-evidence row states the trigger and the next action only, and the reasoning behind it — how the alternative causes are told apart, what the reopening trace tests, and when a chief product officer becomes a relevant option — follows the table as short paragraphs, keeping the checks for both timely decisions and checked results.
- In the TL;DR, identify the board as the directors who oversee the company and explain a funding condition as something that must happen before investors provide the money, so the appointment example reads without the article. Trace the second team from proposed, to deferred, to approved: introduce the appointment example with its changed assumptions (demand shown, financing completed, board approval and funding of the five roles) and keep the approval right’s full scope (hiring, dismissal and pay). Say what the proposed chief product officer would do (set product priorities across teams) and what the senior product manager does instead (handle the second country, freeing Priya to prioritize across both teams with coaching), and state the company’s choice directly. Keep each review interval’s starting event, stated explicitly rather than elliptically: sixty days after the delegation changes, six months after the billing engineer starts. Keep the TL;DR’s prose within its 300–500-word target while preserving every first-use explanation, the forecast condition, all three hiring gates and the first-year cost assumption; tighten phrasing and drop repeated framing rather than cutting a required qualification.
- In the comic, call the second team proposed, and let the artwork of the closing page name the one billing engineer funded now and all three conditions for the second team (proven demand, committed funding, board approval). The comic must reach that hire causally rather than by assertion: before the closing page it states that two approval waits are delegated first and are forecast to remove days without a new hire, that a specialist knowledge gap remains after them, and that the billing engineer changes the software preparing customer bills and learns from the existing specialists in protected time. Labels drawn in the artwork are real, correctly spelled words that match the comparison the prose makes (supplier spending against the full cost of an employed team, not salary against salary). The stage order drawn in any comic chart of the trace matches the article's table and Figure 1: rule approval, specialist review, build and test, invoice sign-off, release. The comic introduces Ines as the chief executive who leads Larkspur alongside the other named people, names who now approves the invoices, and explains release as putting a change into the live customer system at its first meaningful use. Authority in the comic passes to people, not to documents: say that Priya lets her team implement the listed pricing-rule types without asking her again, and that Ines gives invoice approval to Sam's finance team, which already checks customer bills against the underlying records. Neither "catalogue" nor "reconcile" may stand unexplained in that standalone format, and the storyboard caption, visible caption and alt text stay synchronized.
