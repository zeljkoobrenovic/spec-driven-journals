Every euro entering a business comes through one of four doors. **Customers** buy the product — the only money that never has to be repaid or bought back, which makes it the most valuable in the business. **Lenders** supply cash to be repaid with interest on a schedule, regardless of how the quarter went; they own nothing and cannot direct the roadmap, but they attach conditions and gain rights quickly if those conditions break. **Owners** buy a piece of the company itself, are not repaid on a schedule, and make money if the company becomes more valuable — in exchange for a say in every future decision. **Selling an asset** raises cash once and leaves the business smaller. The mix of these four is the capital structure, and it decides how much can go wrong before someone outside starts making decisions inside.

**A public company is one whose shares anyone can buy through a stock exchange.** Its owners are strangers who change constantly and cannot be convened; results are published quarterly; a price sits on a screen and moves on news and mood. That visibility shortens time horizons — a project that depresses earnings for six quarters is hard to defend when the scoreboard updates every ninety days. A private company has none of this: no public price, lighter reporting, and owners who can be named and sat down with. Most companies in the world are private; public ones are the visible minority.

**Private equity is one kind of private owner.** Literally, it means ownership stakes in companies not traded on an exchange. In practice it means firms that raise a pool of money from large institutions, buy whole companies, usually borrow a substantial part of the purchase price, hold for a few years, and sell. That combination — a fund, borrowed money, control and a planned sale — is this book's subject.

Two features matter most for what follows. **The borrowing** means a company can be bought partly with debt its own cash flows then service, so ownership can change how much money reaches engineering without anything about the product changing. **The deadline** means the owner intends to sell, usually within three to seven years, because the fund has an end date. Neither makes for a worse owner, but both make for a different one.

Taking on an investor is a choice rather than a stage of growth; funding a business from its own profits remains legitimate. [[pt-investment-fit]] works through which arrangement suits which situation.

This matters because the question engineering leaders actually care about — can we fund this work? — is answered inside this structure, not on a budget line. A public company may decline a two-year platform investment over quarterly earnings; a private-equity-owned company may decline it because interest consumes the cash first; a founder-owned company may approve it and then be unable to pay. The engineering merits were identical. Knowing which kind of company you work for tells you which argument will land.
