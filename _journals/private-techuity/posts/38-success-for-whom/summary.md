**Durable success asks more than whether the investors did well: after an ownership decision, can the company keep serving customers, paying its obligations and funding necessary work?**

![Investors, the company, customers, employees and suppliers each hold a separate ledger; thirty Rotaline customers on an old path face a crossed-out cut-off at month seven stamped as a breach and a chosen migration in months seven to twelve behind month-six tests, with a review at month nine; €150,000 of cash is set apart from €70,000 of freed time, leading to a €75,000 half-time service.](assets/images/38-success-for-whom/summary-at-a-glance.jpeg)
**Figure 1:** *Each group keeps its own account; Rotaline refuses a cut-off that breaks contracts, migrates behind tests, and keeps saved cash apart from freed time.*

Put the book's cases side by side and investor and company results rarely move together. Blackstone reported about 3.1 times its money on Hilton at its 2018 exit. Microsoft paid $8.5 billion for Skype in 2011 and retired it in 2025. Toys R Us reported $460 million of operating earnings beside almost no operating cash, and its US stores closed in 2018 with about 33,000 employees affected. TeamSystem paired positive adjusted earnings with a loss in its formal accounts. Investor results are the best reported; evidence about customers, employees and suppliers is the thinnest.

So judge each group on its own terms. **A gain for one group does not offset harm to another.** When interests conflict, whoever is authorized to decide should say who loses, why, and how the harm is addressed.

At fictional Rotaline, thirty customers still depend on an old manual setup path, where one specialist maintains their settings by hand. Sam, the chief financial officer, proposes ending it at the close of month seven. That would remove about €220,000 a year: €150,000 of the specialist's cost, plus €70,000 of a support engineer's time, which is freed capacity rather than cash. But Priya, accountable for the customer commitments, points out that several contracts run past that date and name the manual path. The cut-off is a breach, and **a saving built on a breach is not a saving**. Asking customers to move early is allowed, but the specialist's cost goes only when the last one leaves, which depends on consents Rotaline does not control.

The board chooses a slower migration in months seven to twelve. It starts only if the next group of new customers passes both month-six tests: average setup effort of 50 hours or less, and a median wait of eight weeks or less. The specialist stays full-time to month twelve, about €62,500 more than the quick option, then moves to a funded half-time data service at €75,000 a year. If customers accept it, cash cost falls by €75,000 from year two. The disagreement is recorded for review at month nine, and the decision passes to whoever owns Rotaline next.

Wider research supports looking beyond the investor. Two years after buyouts, employment averaged about 12% lower at companies taken off the stock market and 15% higher at those already private, against similar companies: averages, not forecasts. Healthcare studies found harm to some patients alongside some quality gains; neither cancels the other. **Where customers cannot judge quality or switch, financial incentives may not protect them.**

A leader can separate cash from freed time, name who loses, fund the transition and record what would reopen it, so the next team inherits a company that can serve its customers and fund its obligations. The [[toolkit]] holds the records for that work.
