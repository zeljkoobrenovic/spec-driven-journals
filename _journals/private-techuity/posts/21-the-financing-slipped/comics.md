**Comic.** Larkspur, a fictional company, sells scheduling software to firms whose staff work in the field. It expected €4 million of new investment in September. That investment is a **round**: investors put in money and receive **shares**, stakes in the company’s ownership. The work is on schedule, but the money now looks likely in December. Two pieces of work continue: an **onboarding pilot**, a trial of a faster way to set up new customers, and **recovery work**, which makes sure the dispatch service that sends field staff to their jobs can be restored within four hours after a failure. Two hires and a **customer portal**, a website where customers handle routine tasks themselves, were planned but are not yet committed. Eight pages follow the company as it changes that funding assumption before it becomes a delivery promise: how far the money has got, cash by date, the latest useful decision date, three alternatives, one authorized fallback, a first reduction decided in time, the rule for restarting deferred spending, and what happens if the money never comes. Each page is three strips; the amounts and dates are drawn into the artwork, and a transcript follows every page.

Alex leads technology and Priya leads product. Sam, the chief financial officer, leads finance, and Ines, the chief executive, leads the company. Morgan advises the investors on technology and sits in on the board meeting. All are fictional, and every figure is invented. This example is deliberately separate from the Larkspur figures in other chapters.

**Page 1: The funding slipped, not the work.** Every figure in this comic is invented, and this example stands on its own; its cash, spending, hires and dates are not the Larkspur figures of other chapters. Larkspur sells scheduling software to businesses whose staff work in the field. In June it was in serious talks with a lead investor about a €4 million round — new investment for which investors receive shares, stakes in the company’s ownership — with closing, the day the investment completes and the money is transferred, expected on 15 September. The plan for the second half of the year was built on that date. In early July the talks still look serious, but closing now looks likely in mid-December. The work itself is on schedule: the onboarding pilot, a trial of a faster way to get new customers set up, and the recovery work that makes sure the dispatch service can be restored quickly after a failure. An assumption about money changed, so the first task is to name the stage the money has reached: an expression of interest binds nobody; approval pending means it waits on the investor’s own decision body; a signed commitment with conditions pays only after those are met; only cash received can be spent. In July the round is at the first stage. Naming the stage keeps blame out of the response: this is an unfunded dependency, not poor execution, and treating it as execution produces the wrong fix.

- *Strip 1.* **Sam:** “The €4 million round was pointing at September. Now it looks like December.” **Ines:** “The talks are still serious. But 'likely in December' is a forecast, not cash.”
- *Strip 2.* **Alex:** “The work itself is fine. Nothing here slipped.” **Priya:** “What changed is a money assumption. Fix it before it becomes a delivery promise.”
- *Strip 3.* **Sam:** “In July the round is at stage one. Nothing binds the investor.” **Ines:** “An unfunded dependency, not poor execution. That needs a different response.”

**Page 2: Cash and commitments by date.** Sam, the chief financial officer, starts with two numbers and one limit. On 1 July Larkspur holds €1.6 million. Its net monthly burn, the cash it pays out each month minus the cash customers pay in, is €200,000. The board — the directors who oversee the company and approve its plan — has agreed a minimum operating reserve of €400,000, the smallest cash balance it wants kept. Falling below it breaks the board’s own safety limit; it does not yet mean the company has run out of money. Without new money the reserve is reached after six months, (€1.6 million − €0.4 million) ÷ €200,000, on 31 December: €1.0 million at 30 September, €0.6 million at 30 November, the reserve at 31 December. That is the baseline, the current plan with nothing added or removed. The second step sorts spending by whether it can still be changed and until when. Protected obligations — customer contracts and the agreed recovery requirement that dispatch resumes within four hours — are not optional, and every alternative must pay for them. The onboarding pilot and the recovery work are committed and already inside the €200,000 burn. Two hires starting 1 September, €30,000 a month for the two together, and the portal contract at €120,000 are planned but not yet committed, and can still be changed.

**Page 8: If the money does not come.** The board must also know what happens if the money never gets there. Three cases differ. Case 1: nothing signed by 1 October — R1’s notice goes out that day under the 10 August instruction; €500,000 on 31 December; reserve about 20 January; the board keeps pursuing the bridge, whose cash must now arrive before then, and looks for other money. Case 2: the bridge agreement is signed but a condition fails and no money arrives in November — R1’s saving runs from 1 November; reserve about 20 January; as soon as the failure is known, other financing or a sale. Case 3: the bridge’s €600,000 arrives by 30 November but the round fails in December — €1.1 million on 31 December, reserve about 20 May, and €600,000 plus added interest owed at the loan’s maturity twelve months after the money arrived; the decision on the spending level Larkspur can continue at and on the loan belongs in December, not May. R1’s saving appears in every case only because its notice went out on 1 October while the money was still uncertain. The deeper reduction appears in no row as a remedy: it needs its own board decision, and its notice periods and leaving payments come before its savings. What Larkspur does if neither the bridge nor the round arrives is not decided here, and saying so is part of an honest fallback: on 10 August the board knows that B and R1 carry the company to about 20 January and no further.
