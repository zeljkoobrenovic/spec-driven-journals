> **IN THIS SECTION, YOU WILL:** Follow one fictional company, Larkspur, whose new investment was expected in September and now looks likely in December. Learn to name how far the money has got, put cash and commitments on a calendar, and choose one fallback in time.

> **WHY INVESTORS CARE:** New investment can slip on the investors’ side as well as the company’s, for example while it waits for an internal approval or a second investor. Investors need management to have a dated fallback ready, not a hope.

> **WHY YOU SHOULD CARE:** A plan that spends money that has not arrived falls below its cash safety limit on a predictable date. Decide before job offers and signed contracts decide for you.


> **KEY POINTS:**
>
> * **Expected money is not cash.** Name how far it has got, from an investor’s stated interest to money in the bank. Only cash received can be spent. When its date moves, the plan that depends on it must change.
> * **Work the decision by date.** Put cash, monthly spending and every planned commitment on a calendar. Find the last date a decision can still change them, and compare the alternatives by when each falls below the minimum cash the board wants to keep.
> * **Authorize one fallback.** The board chooses the plan that runs now, names who pursues the missing money and sets in advance what must happen before deferred spending restarts. Customers and the team are told which plan is running.

<br>
Plans are often built on **money that has not arrived yet**. A new investment is expected by a certain date, and hires, contracts and customer promises are lined up to start when it lands. Then the date moves. The work may be going well, but the plan now spends money the company does not have, and every week of waiting brings it closer to its cash safety limit. The real risk is not the delay itself; it is letting job offers and signed contracts make the decision before anyone has made it deliberately.

## Setting Up the Example: Larkspur’s Round Moves to December

Larkspur, the fictional company this book follows, sells scheduling software to businesses whose staff work in the field, such as maintenance firms. In June it was in serious talks with a **lead investor**, the investor who negotiates the terms and usually puts in the largest share, about a €4 million **round**: new investment raised from investors, who receive **shares**, stakes in the ownership of the company, in exchange. The talks pointed to **closing**, the day the investment completes and the money is transferred, on 15 September.

The plan for the second half of the year was built on that date. It included two hires starting on 1 September and a contract for a **customer portal**, a website where customers handle routine tasks themselves. By early July the talks still look serious, but closing now looks likely in mid-December.

The work itself is on schedule. The **onboarding pilot**, a trial of a faster way to get new customers set up, continues. So does the **recovery work**, which makes sure the **dispatch service**, the software that sends field staff to their jobs, can be restored quickly after a failure. The funding has slipped, not the work, and the company must change its funding assumption before that assumption turns into a delivery promise it cannot keep.

Every figure here is invented, and this example stands on its own: its cash, spending, hires and dates differ from the Larkspur figures in the chapters [[first-hundred-days]] and [[handover-of-obligations]].

## The Funding Slipped, Not the Work

The plan is what the existing investors backed when they put money in. They could also lend the company a **bridge**, a short-term loan meant to carry it until the round arrives, but lending more is an investment decision for them and needs their own approval. So before committing to anything that spends the expected money, establish three facts: what stage the money has reached, who has the authority to change the plan, and the date by which the choice must be made. Here that date is set by two job offers and a contract signature, described below.

An assumption about money changed, so the first task is to name the stage that money has reached:

- **Expression of interest:** the investor says it wants to invest, but nothing binds it.
- **Approval pending:** the proposal is waiting for the investor’s own decision body, such as its **investment committee**, the group inside an investment firm that approves each investment.
- **Signed commitment with conditions:** a contract is signed, but the money is paid only after listed conditions are met, for example a completed legal review or a shareholder vote.
- **Cash received:** the money is in the company’s bank account.

In July the round is at the first stage. “Likely in December” is a forecast of a conversation, not a date on which cash arrives.

Naming the stage also keeps blame out of the response. A project can fail because its basic assumption was wrong, because the work was done badly, or because something it depended on was never supplied. Here the delayed round is the third kind: an **unfunded dependency**, money the plan needed that has not arrived. In other cases a missed business target also contributes to a delay, and then part of the response is about execution too. Treating a missing dependency as poor execution produces the wrong fix, such as more oversight or a leadership change, and hides the decision that actually needs making.

## Cash and Commitments by Date

Sam, the chief financial officer (**CFO**), leads finance. On 1 July Larkspur holds **€1.6 million**. Its **net monthly burn**, the cash it pays out each month minus the cash customers pay in, is **€200,000**. The **board**, the group of directors who oversee the company and approve its plan, has agreed a **minimum operating reserve of €400,000**: the smallest cash balance it wants the company to keep. Falling below it breaks the board’s own safety limit; it does not yet mean the company has run out of money.

Without new money the reserve is reached after six months: (€1.6 million − €0.4 million) ÷ €200,000 = 6, which is **31 December**. That is the **baseline**, the current plan with nothing added or removed.

| Date | Cash without new money, baseline plan |
| --- | ---: |
| 1 July | €1.6m |
| 30 September | €1.0m |
| 30 November | €0.6m |
| 31 December | €0.4m (the reserve) |

The second step sorts spending commitments by whether they can still be changed, and until when.

| Group | Items | Status |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Protected obligations | Customer contracts; the agreed recovery requirement (dispatch resumes within four hours, see [[prove-you-can-restore]]) | Not optional; every alternative must pay for them |
| Committed and continuing | The onboarding pilot and the recovery work | Already inside the €200,000 monthly burn |
| Planned, not yet committed | Two hires starting 1 September (€30,000 a month for the two together); the portal contract, €120,000 | Can still be changed until 16 August |

The two candidates have been told to expect written offers on 16 August, so they can give notice to their current employers and start on 1 September. The portal supplier’s proposal, which holds its price and a delivery slot, expires on 16 August. Signing that day commits Larkspur to €120,000, invoiced at signature and paid on 15 September, with delivery over the following three months.

Once an offer is accepted or the contract signed, stepping back costs money and trust. Larkspur treats both as commitments it will not reverse; the exact legal position depends on the country and the contract. So **15 August is the latest useful decision date**: the last day on which a decision still changes what happens on the 16th.

## Three Alternatives and the Latest Useful Decision Date

Alex, the chief technology officer (**CTO**), leads technology. He and Priya, who leads product, identify what can be delayed, stopped or finished safely. Sam prices three alternatives by the date each one reaches the reserve.

| Alternative | What it does | Reserve reached | Time left if closing slips again |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **A: proceed as planned** | Offers sent and portal signed on 16 August | Late November | None; breached before a mid-December closing |
| **B: defer the hires and the portal** | Keep the onboarding pilot and the recovery work; start nothing new | 31 December | None for a slip past December |
| **C: B plus a €600,000 bridge from the existing investors, not yet agreed** | In July, an expression of interest; approval by the investors’ investment committees pending | 31 March, only if the money arrives by 30 November | Covers a slip to March |

The arithmetic behind A: by 30 November the baseline €600,000, less the €120,000 portal payment and three months of the two hires (€90,000), leaves about €390,000, below the reserve. With a mid-December closing, the round’s cash would arrive after the breach, and every week spent meeting closing conditions would be paid from the reserve.

The March date for C rests on fictional loan terms, stated so it can be checked:

- **Who pays, and when:** the existing investors lend €600,000, paid in one transfer that reaches Larkspur’s account by 30 November, before the December reserve date.
- **What kind of loan:** a **convertible loan**, one that turns into shares when the round closes instead of being repaid in cash.
- **Charges:** no **arrangement fee**, the one-off charge some lenders make for setting up a loan. Interest, the cost of borrowing, is added to the amount owed and converts into shares with it; none is paid in cash.
- **Repayment:** nothing, neither the €600,000 nor interest nor any other charge, is payable in cash before the loan’s **maturity**, its due date, twelve months after the money arrives. That is later than every date in this chapter, including the late-May date below.
- **If the round never closes:** there is nothing to convert. At maturity Larkspur owes the €600,000 plus the added interest, unless the investors agree to extend the loan or convert it on other terms. That debt falls outside the months tracked here, but any plan to continue without the round must pay for it.

On those terms cash on 31 December is €1.0 million (€400,000 plus €600,000), and three further months at €200,000 reach the reserve on 31 March. Any fee or cash payment before March moves that date earlier by the amount paid divided by the monthly burn. Money arriving after 31 December does not prevent the breach. A promised amount is not a financing schedule until the payer, the amount that will actually arrive and the date are written down.

C adds three months to B if the money arrives in time, but in July C is not cash. The bridge is an expression of interest, and it needs approval by the existing investors’ investment committees, the right bodies because lending more is an investment decision for them. A signed loan agreement would move it one stage, to a signed commitment with conditions. It would still not be cash received. So the company pursues C but plans as if it is executing B.

## The Board’s Authorized Fallback

The decision needs one escalation, a request that takes it to the people with authority to make it, not a progress update. Ines, the chief executive officer (**CEO**), leads the company overall. She and Sam take the decision to the Larkspur board, which approves the operating plan under the approval map in the chapter [[decide-who-decides]], the record of which body approves which decisions. The escalation states three things: the **decision required** (choose A, B or C), the **latest useful date** (15 August) and the **consequence of delay** (A by default, with the reserve breached in late November). “The round has slipped” is not enough; the board must know which action changes the outcome and who carries it out.

The board decides on 10 August:

- **Chosen:** B now. The hires and the portal are deferred, not canceled: no offers go out and the portal contract is not signed on 16 August. The onboarding pilot and the recovery work continue.
- **Rejected:** A, because it breaches the reserve before the earliest realistic closing. C on its own, because the bridge is not yet agreed; it is pursued, not counted.
- **Funding:** existing cash at the €200,000 monthly burn, reaching the reserve on 31 December.
- **Who pursues the money:** Sam, accountable for a **signed bridge agreement by 1 September**. Signing moves the bridge to a signed commitment with conditions; what that permits is set by the restart rule below.
- **Scarce capacity:** the implementation specialist’s time, spent setting the software up for new customers, and the engineers’ working weeks stay on the onboarding pilot and the recovery work. The work the two hires would have started is not started.
- **Authorized:** the Larkspur board, on 10 August. Ines is accountable for telling customers and the team; Alex and Priya for **staging** the deferred work, preparing it so it can restart without rework.
- **Review:** 1 October. The question is what stage the bridge and the round have each reached, not whether the team is busy.
- **What would change the decision:** the money moving a stage, judged by the restart rule below, or burn rising above €200,000. The board asks Alex and Priya to cost reductions as a named list for 1 October, rather than decide them under pressure later.

The first reduction on that list is costed and decided now, because its own deadline is the review itself.

**Reduction 1 (R1):** Larkspur uses **contract engineers**, outside workers engaged through agreements rather than employed, on its **internal tooling**, the software its own staff use. Their agreements end on 31 October, and notice of non-renewal is due on 1 October, the day of the review. Not renewing saves **€50,000 a month from 1 November**. The onboarding pilot and the recovery work are staffed by employees and are unaffected; the customer contracts and the four-hour recovery requirement stay protected.

The board does not leave R1 open until the review. It decides on 10 August that **R1’s notice goes out on 1 October unless cash already received by then passes the restart test below without it**. A signed agreement is still uncertain money, so it does not stop the notice. Serving the notice on time secures November’s saving: a failure discovered in November or December cannot bring back a month that has already been paid for.

| With R1 | Without the bridge | With the bridge received by 30 November |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Cash on 31 December | €500,000 | €1.1m |
| Burn from 1 January | €150,000 | €150,000 |
| Reserve reached | About 20 January (instead of 31 December) | About 20 May (instead of 31 March) |

Without the bridge, R1 buys about three weeks, not a quarter: the €100,000 left above the reserve lasts two-thirds of a month at €150,000. With it, €700,000 above the reserve lasts four and two-thirds months. A reduction list lowers the bridge the company needs; it does not replace it, so **R1 alone is not a complete fallback** if no money arrives.

The deeper step is the spending level at which Larkspur could continue on **collected revenue** alone, meaning money customers have actually paid, not merely been billed. It is not decided in this chapter, and it is not a fallback that can be switched on when money fails. It needs its own board decision, and it costs money before it saves any: people who leave are paid through their notice periods and receive leaving payments first. Alex and Priya cost it for 1 October, and the chapter [[anatomy-of-a-layoff]] follows what the board does with it when the bridge commitment arrives carrying a condition of its own.

## Tell Customers and Teams Which Plan Is Running

No customer receives a promise that depends on the hires or the portal. Onboarding commitments stay at the volume the pilot evidence supports, not the volume the round would have funded. The portal is described as research, which is what it is.

The team is told it is executing B: what is deferred, what continues, what would restore the original plan and when the next review is. A team that hears “the round is nearly done” and then sees two offers withdrawn draws its own conclusions, often worse than the facts. The board narrative, the investor update and the team briefing may differ in detail, but they come from the same dated cash table.

## The Restart Rule: What Each Stage of the Money Permits

A **term sheet** is a short document setting out the proposed terms of an investment. In a common arrangement it does not bind the investor to invest. It may state an expected closing date, but it does not guarantee that the investment will complete or that cash will arrive then. A signed **subscription agreement**, the contract in which the investor agrees to buy the new shares, is one stage further: a signed commitment, still with conditions. Its cash date remains a forecast until closing happens.

**Counting** the money means showing it in the dated cash forecast. **Spending** against it means taking on a new obligation, such as sending an offer or signing a contract, before the money arrives. The spending test below governs the deferred spending: the hires and the portal. The bridge is different. It is money being pursued, not spending, so the only question for it is when it may be counted; the table below answers that for the bridge and the round alike.

A spending test needs an end date, or nobody can apply it. Larkspur’s board sets it at **30 June**. The round has already slipped once by three months, from September to December, and 30 June leaves room for two more slips of that size, to March and then to June. The test: **a forecast that counts only cash already received must stay at or above the €400,000 reserve until 30 June, with the new obligation included.**

“Cash already received” is about financing, not about customers. The forecast starts from the cash in the bank on the day of the test. It leaves out every investment or loan payment not yet received: the round, and the bridge until its money arrives. It keeps the plan’s stated assumptions about the business itself: customers go on paying as forecast and operating costs continue. Both are already inside the net monthly burn of €200,000, or €150,000 once R1’s saving starts. Every date below uses that burn, plus the cost of whatever new obligation is being tested.

| Stage of the money | May the forecast show it? | May Larkspur take on new obligations against it? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Expression of interest, approval pending, or a term sheet | No | No. Pursue the money; B stays the plan. |
| Signed commitment, conditions still outstanding | Only if the board specifically authorizes it, as a separate line with its conditions and expected date, next to a forecast without it | Only if the test passes on cash already received. Otherwise prepare, but do not commit. |
| Cash received, confirmed by Sam | Yes | Yes, if the test passes with that cash included, within the plan the board approves |
| The money fails to arrive: an agreement lapses, a condition fails or closing fails | No; the line is removed | No. Follow the matching case in the next section. |

At Larkspur’s figures the test fails before anything new is added. With R1 and no new money, cash on 31 December is €500,000 and the reserve is reached about 20 January, more than five months short of 30 June. With the bridge’s cash received as well, the reserve is reached about 20 May, still about six weeks short.

Each deferred item makes it worse. Take the case without the bridge first. Suppose the portal contract were signed in mid-November and, as in the supplier’s original proposal, paid a month later, in mid-December. Cash on 31 December would be €380,000 (€500,000 − €120,000), below the reserve before the year ends. Starting the two hires on 1 November would instead cost €60,000 by 31 December and leave €440,000. It would also raise the monthly burn to €180,000, bringing the reserve date forward from about 20 January to about 7 January.

With the bridge’s cash received, the same portal payment leaves €980,000 on 31 December (€1.1 million − €120,000). That is no breach this year, but the reserve date moves from about 20 May to about 26 April, further short of 30 June.

So at Larkspur **the offers and the portal signature wait until the round’s cash is in the account and Sam confirms it**. The bridge’s cash gives B its extra time but does not restart them, because it does not pass the test. A signed agreement the board authorizes counting lets Alex and Priya prepare: draft the offers, agree the portal terms and plan the work. If the agreement carries a closing condition that could take six weeks, the offers wait six weeks.

## If the Money Does Not Come

The restart rule says what the money must reach before Larkspur spends more. The board must also know what happens if the money never gets there. “Run the fallback” is not an answer until it says which notices have already gone out, who decides next, what cash is left and when the next decision is due. Reaching the reserve is the board’s own limit, not an empty bank account, but the next decision belongs before that date, not after it.

| Case | Already done | Cash | Who decides next | Next decision |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Nothing signed on 1 October.** The bridge agreement has missed its 1 September date; the round is still an expression of interest. | R1’s notice goes out that day, under the 10 August instruction | €1.0m on 1 October, €500,000 on 31 December; reserve reached about 20 January | The Larkspur board for the plan; the existing investors’ committees for the bridge | At the review: keep pursuing the bridge, whose cash must now arrive before about 20 January, and look for other money |
| **2. A signed agreement fails after 1 October.** The bridge agreement is signed, but a condition is not met and no money arrives in November. | R1’s notice went out on 1 October; its saving runs from 1 November | €500,000 on 31 December; reserve reached about 20 January | The Larkspur board | As soon as the failure is known: other financing or a sale. Nothing decided in December adds back November’s saving. |
| **3. The bridge’s cash arrives; the round fails in December.** | R1’s notice went out on 1 October; €600,000 received by 30 November | €1.1m on 31 December; reserve reached about 20 May. €600,000 plus added interest is owed when the loan falls due, twelve months after the money arrived | The Larkspur board; the existing investors for any extension or conversion of the loan | In December, not in May: the spending level Larkspur can continue at, and how the loan will be repaid or renegotiated |

First, R1’s saving appears in every case only because its notice went out on 1 October, while the money was still uncertain. Had the board waited to see whether the bridge came, a failure found in November or December could not have recovered that month’s saving.

Second, the deeper reduction appears in no row as a remedy. It needs its own board decision, and its notice periods and leaving payments come before its savings, so it must be paid for from cash in hand first. The chapter [[anatomy-of-a-layoff]] shows that, at the terms Larkspur later costs, such a reduction makes the bridge last longer but cannot replace it. What Larkspur does if neither the bridge nor the round arrives, whether it finds another lender, sells the company or something else, is not decided in this chapter. Saying so is part of an honest fallback: on 10 August the board knows that B and R1 carry the company to about 20 January and no further.

## Two Variations on the Same Decision

**A refinancing that leaves less cash.** A loan is due to be repaid, and the company plans to **refinance** it: take a new loan that pays off the old one. The new loan on offer leaves less cash for development. The trigger is a financing change, not evidence that engineering has become less valuable. The method is unchanged: the dated cash position after the refinancing, the protected obligations, the commitments still changeable, the latest useful date (here, the refinancing signature) and the alternatives priced by when each reaches the reserve. The chapter [[obligations-before-budget]] shows how loan payments and taxes come before the **discretionary budget**, the money left for optional work, in that calculation.

**A corporate parent withdrawing budget.** Here the product belongs to a larger group, and the **parent company**, the company that owns it, decides how much money each of its businesses receives. A new group strategy reduces the allocation to the product. Reconfirm which part of the parent now funds the product and answers for it, what remains funded, and which promises to external customers continue regardless. A reorganization inside the parent is not an instruction to let local obligations become ownerless. The latest useful date is set by the parent’s budget cycle and the company’s own contract renewals, and the escalation goes to whoever in the parent is authorized to fund or release the obligation.

![Funding, refinancing, continued ownership and sale are possible paths from a review; company operations continue through them.](assets/images/23-the-financing-slipped/ownership-paths-and-replanning.jpeg)
**Figure 1:** *A funding delay sends the operating plan back to a review junction. Further funding, refinancing, continued ownership and a sale are all possible paths, and the company’s service to customers continues through every one of them, so the plan needs a fallback that works on each.*

In every variation, **continued ownership**, the same owners carrying on without the new money, is a real scenario. If the round never closes, the refinancing fails or the parent does not restore the budget, what useful business remains and what can it afford? B answers that question for Larkspur until 31 December. R1, with notice served on 1 October, carries it to about 20 January, and the bridge, once its cash is received, to about 20 May. What Larkspur does if neither arrives is still open. The level at which it could continue on collected revenue alone is costed for 1 October, and the board takes it up with that list in front of it in the chapter [[anatomy-of-a-layoff]].

The decision leaves a record: the assumption, the trigger, the alternatives, who decided, and the evidence that would change it. The next decision-maker inherits that record, whether the round closes, the existing investors continue, or the company changes hands. The chapter [[anatomy-of-a-layoff]] takes this scenario’s next decision, the reduction, through the people and the work it affects. The chapter [[handover-of-obligations]] then follows the record through an ownership change: what evidence to keep during the work, who receives cash and who keeps a share of ownership in each type of deal, and how to hand over the unfinished obligations rather than only the company.

## Questions to Consider

1. *What stage has your next expected money reached: expression of interest, approval pending, signed commitment with conditions, or cash received? Which plan is already spending it?*
2. *On which date does your next planned commitment stop being changeable, and is the decision it depends on scheduled before that date?*
3. *If the expected money never arrived, on what date would you reach the agreed reserve, and what would you stop first?*

- **[Term Sheet for Series A Preferred Stock Financing (model document)](https://nvca.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/06/NVCA-Model-Term-Sheet-1.doc)** — National Venture Capital Association, NVCA Model Legal Documents, last updated January 2019 (Word document; the overview page is [nvca.org/model-legal-documents](https://nvca.org/model-legal-documents/)).<br>*A primary financing document rather than a study. **Series A** is usually a company’s first large round from professional investors, and **preferred stock** is a class of shares that carries agreed advantages over ordinary shares: for example, if the company is sold, its holders are paid from the sale proceeds before ordinary shareholders, on the terms agreed. The model’s opening paragraph makes only two provisions binding on the company: a **no-shop**, a promise not to seek competing investors for a period, and confidentiality (optionally also who pays the lawyers’ fees and other costs). It states that “no other legally binding obligations will be created until definitive agreements are executed and delivered by all parties”, that is, until the final contracts are signed and exchanged. That is why Larkspur’s rule treats a term sheet as neither cash received nor a commitment a plan may count.*
- **[Do Private Equity Funds Manipulate Reported Returns?](https://www.nber.org/papers/w22493)** — Gregory W. Brown, Oleg R. Gredil and Steven N. Kaplan, National Bureau of Economic Research (NBER) Working Paper 22493, 2016 (later published in the Journal of Financial Economics).<br>*The paper studies **private equity** firms, which invest money pooled from outside investors in companies that are not listed on a stock market. Each pool is a **fund**: the firm manages it for a fixed period and raises a new fund every few years. A fund’s **reported returns** are the investment gains or losses it reports to its own investors. The paper finds that some of these firms whose past results were weak inflate their **interim valuations**, their own estimates of what the companies a fund still owns are worth, while raising their next fund. That fundraising is the investment firm’s own, not a company’s round. The relevance here is indirect: an investor’s reported view, like a term sheet, is not cash, and a company that plans against either can find its funding date moving.*
- **[Earnings Management in Private Equity Portfolio Firms During Fundraising](https://www.hbs.edu/ris/Publication%20Files/23-013_ced08e2c-ad57-4130-a2df-a5798bbfbb6f.pdf)** — Brian K. Baik, Journal of Accounting and Economics, in press, meaning accepted and published online in July 2026 but not yet in a printed issue (article 101926, [doi:10.1016/j.jacceco.2026.101926](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacceco.2026.101926)); the link is the Harvard Business School working-paper version, revised July 2026.<br>*Using European **buyout funds**, funds that buy a **controlling stake** in established companies, meaning enough of the ownership and votes to decide the company’s major matters, and the **portfolio companies** they own, the study finds more **earnings management**, accounting choices or business actions that make reported profits look better, while the firm managing the fund raises its next one. The effect appears mainly in companies backed by what the study calls low-reputation investors, not across the whole sample. The label describes a thin financial track record, not misconduct: an investment firm with no earlier fund among the best performers that has also raised few funds or little money in total. The study shows an association rather than proof of cause. It says nothing about delayed rounds. The link to this chapter is the author’s analogy: the same pressure to present a favorable picture can keep a slipping round looking “nearly done” longer than the cash table should tolerate.*
