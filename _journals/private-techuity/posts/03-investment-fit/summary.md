Choose capital around the company's problem rather than assuming every business should progress from venture funding to growth equity to a buyout. Ownership change, new company funding, founder liquidity, and restructuring are different needs.

Venture investing can fit uncertain growth before a company funds itself. Growth equity can support expansion where there is evidence of demand. A buyout can support succession or a change in control. A carve-out needs independent operating capabilities. A turnaround may be governed first by liquidity and continuity. These are orientations, with overlap and variation across actual agreements. [S01: SEC investor guide](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/investment-products/private-investment-funds/private-equity)

The fictional Larkspur example shows why the same product can require different capital under different circumstances. A prototype needs learning. An established product with an implementation backlog needs to resolve delivery capacity. A founder's retirement creates an ownership problem even if the business already generates cash. None requires every company to follow the same route.

**Control, ownership percentage, and cash destination are separate questions.** A minority stake can carry material approval rights. A founder can retain shares while changing role. A large transaction can provide little new money to the company. Read the actual decision and financing boundaries before translating a deal into a roadmap.

Investor fit also depends on available support and constraints. Sector expertise does not guarantee follow-on capacity. A broad operating platform does not guarantee that relevant people are available. Stated growth ambitions do not prove that the financing funds the work required.

A practical assessment asks:

- What business or ownership need does this transaction solve?
- Which outcomes does the investor require, and are they feasible?
- What capital, skills, and time does the company need to deliver them?
- What changes if growth is slower or implementation costs more?

The conclusion can be to change the transaction, revise the operating plan, or choose another financing route. A company does not become more mature by accepting a form of ownership that does not fit its work. The useful standard is whether the arrangement gives the business and its owners a credible path through their next difficult decisions.
