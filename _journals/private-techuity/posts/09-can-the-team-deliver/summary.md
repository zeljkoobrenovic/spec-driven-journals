An engineering assessment earns its place by **testing a plan against what the systems and the team can currently do**, whether the systems are built, bought or both. An old system is not automatically the binding constraint, and a new one is not automatically a useful outcome. The condition this chapter starts from is common: an expansion date has been proposed before the dependencies were assessed. Fictional Larkspur plans first paying customers in a second country within twelve months, and Alex has to establish what the systems and team can support, what must change in the plan and what the change would cost.

Begin with the required capability, not a technology score. For Larkspur it is concrete: invoice, contract with, support and onboard customers under a second country’s tax and pricing rules. The investor’s growth thesis identifies which assumptions need investigation; it does not choose an implementation. An emphasis on **EBITDA**, earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, would direct attention to operating costs instead.

Then find the constraint. A generic review would report tight coupling. The useful finding is sharper: the country rules live in the invoicing module, and contracts, support tooling and product configuration all read them from there, so market entry depends on coordinated change across all four. Record strengths in the same finding: a stable scheduling core, deep customer knowledge, one deployment path that releases weekly.

**Technical debt** is future effort or risk arising from earlier choices or postponed work. Describe it through consequences for the plan, not as a loan balance. The invoicing module is the item that matters: the same two specialists review every pricing change, and a change takes about three weeks from request to production. The second country’s rules will pass through that queue.

Evidence about delivery needs the right measure. The **SPACE** framework examines several dimensions of developer productivity; **DORA** uses five delivery metrics and warns against comparing unlike services. [S13: SPACE framework](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/the-space-of-developer-productivity-theres-more-to-it-than-you-think/) [S14: DORA metrics guide](https://dora.dev/guides/dora-metrics/) Two lead times are easy to confuse. DORA change lead time runs from commit to production, under two days at Larkspur; end-to-end work lead time runs from request to customer use, about three weeks. The difference is the diagnosis: the time is spent waiting, not building.

A replacement needs a funded transition. Alex’s fictional estimate for replacing the billing and configuration core is €1.3m over eighteen months, additional to existing payroll: extra people, running both systems together from month seven, and migration. The first benefit could appear around month twelve; the maintenance saving only after the old core retires. Bought and built transitions share these cost categories, but contracts, exit rights and vendor timing differ.

The chapter ends with a compact finding: required capability, current evidence, preserved strength, constraint, uncertainty and transition resources. It hands the organizational question, whether to change roles or hire to relieve the two-person queue, to the next chapter, and the design choice among a supplier’s configuration, a company-owned boundary and a core replacement to the one after.
