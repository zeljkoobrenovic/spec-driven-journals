**Size the work before you name the investor.** Funding that does not match the work either starves the plan or buys it with rights and expectations the company cannot afford, and the people who deliver feel the mismatch either way.

**Figure 1:** *Size the whole need, pick the arrangement whose terms fit the work, and set a dated review that decides what happens next.*

"We need an investor" can hide very different needs, from funding expansion to paying a retiring founder. Two growth investors can offer different rights and different patience; **the terms, not the category**, set the pace, the losses the owners will tolerate and how long the plan has before it is judged.

Start with the work. In a fictional Larkspur scenario, customers are set up by hand and the queue is growing. Alex, who leads engineering, and Priya, the product leader, cost the fix. A reusable setup step costs €180,000 to build, but asking for €180,000 would fund the software and nothing that makes it work. The full need adds a year of maintenance, running the old and new processes side by side, a contract specialist to hold the queue for a year, an allowance for wrong estimates, and six months of runway in case the evidence arrives late. **The total is €610,000**, about €160,000 of it allowance and runway.

Sam, who runs finance, has two arrangements that cover it. A growth investor offers €2.5 million for a minority stake, with approval over the budget and senior hires, raised on a plan to enter a second country within eighteen months. The bank offers a €750,000 four-year loan at 8%: interest only in year one, then €250,000 of the loan repaid each year. Larkspur generates about €350,000 of operating cash a year.

**Ines, the chief executive, and Sam recommend the loan.** Each year's payment is covered by that year's operating cash alone; year two is the tightest, leaving €40,000 after a €310,000 payment. The equity is rejected on pace, not money: it would be raised on an expansion the pilot has not yet earned, and the investor's budget approval would turn a slower plan into a negotiation. The loan's risk is that repayments stay fixed whatever the pilot shows.

The plan also sets a date. At day 90 Priya measures the first eight customers set up with the new step, and at day 100 the board decides. Setup takes 80 staff hours per customer today; the target is 50. Under 60 hours, request the expansion. Between 60 and 70, fund a data-quality step and keep hiring deferred. Above 70, reopen hiring, the target or the equity conversation while cash remains.

Alex and Priya do not sign the financing, but they shape it: they bring the work and its cost, an alternative of two hires at about €300,000 a year, and the dated review. Ines and Sam negotiate; the board approves. If demand, funding or time shifts, revisit the fit; declining outside ownership is an answer, not a failure.
