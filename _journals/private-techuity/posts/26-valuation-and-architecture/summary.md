"The company is worth €60 million" sounds like a fact. It is an estimate, made on a date for a purpose — and a reporting investor, a negotiating buyer and a lender can all produce different numbers for the same company without any of them being wrong.

Those estimates decide what gets funded, and they rest on three quantities that conversation treats as interchangeable. **Revenue** is income recognized from sales in a period, which is not the same as cash received: customers may pay later, or pay in advance. **Earnings** is what remains after the costs a particular profit measure includes — and there are several measures because readers are answering different questions. **Cash** is what actually arrived and can be spent.

**EBITDA** — earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization — exists to compare operating performance across businesses with different financing, tax positions and asset histories. A heavily borrowed company pays more interest without necessarily operating less efficiently; an acquisition adds amortization without worsening the acquired product. Stripping those out shows the operating business before deciding how to finance or own it. What EBITDA cannot do is tell you how much cash is available: interest must still be paid, tax settled, assets replaced. A worked income statement runs €20.0m revenue → €4.0m EBITDA → €3.0m operating profit → €2.0m before tax → €1.5m net profit, an EBITDA margin of 20% — an earnings relationship, not a bank balance.

**Enterprise value** is what the operating business is worth; **equity value** is what remains for shareholders after lenders are paid. Net debt is borrowings minus cash held: owe €25 million, hold €5 million, and net debt is €20 million, because a buyer inherits the loans but also gets the bank balance.

**Three approaches put a number on a business.** Market comparisons ask what similar businesses sold for, which is harder than it sounds. Discounted cash flow asks what expected future cash is worth today, making time and reinvestment explicit but hiding unachievable plans inside plausible assumptions. Asset-based valuation asks what the underlying assets are worth — informative when assets drive value, but blind to the worth of a working organization.

The same company can be described as 3× revenue or 15× EBITDA — two ways of expressing one price, not two kinds of company. Reversed into a valuation, assumed multiples disagree: 3× on €20m revenue gives €60m; 12× on €4m EBITDA gives €48m. The disagreement is the useful part, and the answer is to examine the assumptions rather than pick the larger number.

Growth is a business characteristic, not a fourth method: it can influence any of the three. And growth consumes resources before it produces cash, so every growth assumption implies product, engineering and selling effort that someone must fund.

What carries forward is that the valuation method encodes an assumption about where value comes from — comparable prices, future cash, or separable assets. That assumption is what eventually reaches a roadmap, and [[pt-valuation-and-design]] makes the translation.
