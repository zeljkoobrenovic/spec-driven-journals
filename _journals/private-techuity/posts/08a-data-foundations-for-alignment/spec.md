# Spec: Build the Evidence Everyone Trusts: Data Foundations for Alignment

## Intent

Help product and engineering leaders assemble, maintain and share the data that makes alignment with investors productive: financial and business results, planning and execution evidence, and product and technology evidence. Treat shared data as a working foundation for discussing priorities, testing assumptions and agreeing realistic commitments, not as a reporting duty or a pile assembled for diligence. Extend the author's Lightweight Architectural Analytics practice from Grounded Architecture — curate existing sources into a current, credible picture — from the technology landscape to the investor relationship.

## Audience

Company product and engineering leaders, including people who inherited an investor relationship and have no financial background. Secondary: chief executives and finance leaders who assemble the same material. Assume the reader owns part of the evidence, not all of it.

## Success criteria

- Place the chapter last in Part II, after [[investor-under-pressure]], and hand off to [[part-3]] and [[cannot-fund-everything]], where the evidence is used to choose and fund work. Insert on disk as `08a-` so existing chapter paths stay stable; displayed numbering comes from configuration order.
- Cover the three requested areas with enough specificity to act on, and make their connection the argument rather than three separate lists:
  - **Financial and business:** revenue, profitability, cash flow, cost structure, unit economics, customer retention, sales pipeline, and the assumptions behind growth forecasts.
  - **Planning and execution:** strategic priorities, investment plans, roadmaps, milestones, resource allocation, dependencies, delivery performance, and the gap between commitments and outcomes.
  - **Product and technology:** product adoption, usage, customer outcomes, portfolio performance, architecture, technical debt, reliability, security, scalability, technology cost, and engineering delivery capability.
- Connect explicitly to Lightweight Architectural Analytics: bring together existing sources (source code repositories, cloud billing, incident reports, business metrics) rather than buying a platform or building a warehouse; distil five qualities — curated, current, credible, actionable, accessible — from the practice's account of its data and apply them to investor alignment. Present the five as this chapter's adaptation, not as the source's own enumeration (the source's headings differ). Name the author's own prior work plainly, as the journal does for CTO Starter Kit, and label the extension to investor alignment as a proposal rather than demonstrated practice. Register S106–S108.
- Connect the chapter to **product operations** as described by Melissa Perri and Denise Tilles, mapping its three pillars — business data and insights, customer and market insights, process and governance — onto the chapter's three evidence areas. Make the point that the work falls between existing roles and must be assigned, that standardized definitions are the named mechanism, and that the function supplies evidence without taking over decisions. Register S109 and keep the mapping identified as the book's own.
- Stay practical: start from existing sources, agree one definition per measure and record it, assign a named owner per measure, state a refresh frequency, and give context (baseline, comparison, direction, uncertainty) instead of isolated numbers.
- Make the actual/target/forecast/assumption distinction operational — a labelling rule applied to a real figure, not an exhortation — and show what goes wrong when a projection is read as a result. A target is the desired result by a stated date, not a contractual promise. The four labels cover every figure that describes a result or a future; resources authorized to the work (money, engineer-weeks) are inputs that carry a fifth, explicit label, *committed*, with the part already consumed reported as an actual (incurred), so the rule can be applied to every figure in the chapter's own example (62 h actual, 50 h target, €135,000 forecast, €75 assumption, €180,000 committed, €90,000 incurred). The capacity valuation's assumptions stay separate from the further conditions a cash forecast would need, and there are three of them, not two: the future volume, the hourly valuation and the continuation of the observed 18-hour difference across those future customers. Distinguish an agreed decision rule (which settles the recommendation) from spending authorization (which the board still gives).
- Address the sharing boundary: what should be readily available internally, what is appropriate to share with an investor and at what level of detail; distinguish an investor's information rights from courtesy disclosure; cover raw-access requests, cadence and confidentiality. Do not imply a universal entitlement or a legal standard: entitlement depends on applicable law, the governing documents, the contracts and the recipient's role (an investor-appointed director, an investor with a consent right and a shareholder with a statutory inspection right are different cases), and the board's approval of the plan is distinct from any separately negotiated investor consent. Keep the recipient explicit whenever the chapter says who sees what: an investor-appointed director receives board evidence as a director, the investor receives what its own rights and any permitted sharing arrangement provide, and a board seat is never described as giving the investor itself access. Aggregation and keeping personal data internal are default practices, subject to those obligations.
- Use the canonical shared Larkspur ledger (`_research/shared-scenario-record.md`) without changing any number: baseline about 80 h reconstructed from twelve implementations, day-90 cohort of eight customers averaging 62 h (8 × 62 = 496 h worked, against 640 h at the baseline, so 144 h fewer — never "144 hours of work"), the 1,800 h / €135,000 a year as a projection (100 × 18 × €75) that revises the original plan's 3,000 h / €225,000 at 50 h, capacity versus cash (the contract implementation specialist runs to month twelve, so no payment could be avoided by day 100; the two-specialist hire is spending avoided, not reduced), gate 2 at ≤ 50 h with median waiting time ≤ 8 weeks (passing permits a request and approves nothing), ONB-1 €180,000 committed / ≈€90,000 incurred by day 100 (incurred is not paid), and the €40,000 / four-engineer-week data-quality step as a reserve draw the board approves. Keep the observed difference, its causal explanation and the annual projection apart: the pilot group averaged fewer hours than the earlier group, and the comparison's limits (sales chose the pilot customers, the same specialist served both groups, the baseline was reconstructed) travel with the figure, as in [[roadmap-to-revenue]]. The chapter's contribution is the labelling and definition of these figures, not new ones.
- Include a small reusable measure record and review questions. Add Tool 15 without changing existing tool numbers.
- Use the journal's opening convention (IN THIS SECTION / WHY INVESTORS CARE / WHY YOU SHOULD CARE, then exactly three KEY POINTS), Title Case headings, restrained bold, plain language with terms defined before use, and cross-links via `[[permalink]]`. Target 2,500–3,000 words. Explain finance and technology terms at first meaningful use in every format that must stand alone (article, summary, comic): among others cohort, engineer-week, incurred versus paid, fully loaded cost, debt service, capitalization table, conversion rate, discretionary cost, financial close, diligence, repositories and version control; introduce ONB-1 as the onboarding pilot's identifier or omit it. Define revenue as the amount earned from supplying goods or services in a period, whichever way payment timing falls, illustrated with a service that has been fully delivered but not yet paid for; do not present completed setup work that merely enables a continuing subscription as revenue earned on completion.
- Ship an independently readable 300–500-word summary (three plain-English questions with examples rather than a catalogue of terms; the hours example says what work the hours measure, shows the 18-hour reduction and the €135,000 arithmetic with its three assumptions, and says why freed time is not cash; information rights and aggregation explained locally) and a short illustrated comic of generated comic pages with captions and dialogue transcripts. Illustrate the article with three Gemini figures in the journal's ivory, navy, teal and ochre palette: the three connected areas over shared sources; the labelling of actual, target, forecast and assumption; and the internal-versus-shared boundary. Descriptive alt text and captions; no invented statistics in artwork.
- Update Part II's intro to four chapters, the neighbouring handoffs, the reading guide, glossary, bibliography and toolkit. Preserve existing public permalinks and asset namespaces. Build and inspect the rendered article and reading formats.

## Non-goals

A metrics catalogue or KPI taxonomy; a tooling or vendor recommendation; a data-governance or warehouse programme; a reporting-pack template for finance; legal advice on information rights; any claim that better data resolves disagreement or that the proposed extension has measured results. Not a repeat of [[roadmap-to-revenue]]'s benefit arithmetic or [[can-the-team-deliver]]'s delivery assessment — this chapter says what evidence must exist and how it is defined, owned and labelled, then points to those chapters for the reasoning applied to it.

## Modalities

- `index.md`: article with three explanatory Gemini figures.
- `summary.md`: independent summary reusing one figure. Runs to about 700 words, a justified exception to the 300–500 target: to stand alone for a lay reader it must carry the three evidence questions with examples, the product-operations mapping, the worked hours example (496 h over eight customers, 62 average, 18 fewer, 100 × 18 × €75 = €135,000) and why freed time is not cash, plus local explanations of information rights and aggregation (round-1 review, R08A-006).
- `comics.md`: six generated comic pages (three strips each, numbers lettered in the artwork) following one measure from ambiguity to a labelled, owned figure. Every page is generated and inspected against its script; the under-60 threshold reads as permission to request a further stage; the threshold page's caption and alt text carry all three bands as average onboarding hours per customer and say what the data-quality step does, so the accessible text alone recovers the decision rule; the calculation page labels the continuation of the 18-hour difference as an assumption; the measured page's board states the 62 hours as an average, not as each customer's result; and the cash page names the events that actually turn freed time into money — an outgoing payment avoided or extra incoming receipts above their cash costs — tied to the dates payments fall due rather than to the decision not to hire.

## Open questions

Whether a later chapter should carry a worked investor data pack. The consulted sources describe analytics practice and reporting conventions; they do not establish that shared definitions improve investor relationships, and the chapter says so.
