An **acquisition** purchases a business; **integration** makes chosen activities work together. A **carve-out** separates a business from a larger owner. Both the purchase and the separation can legally complete before all operating dependencies have been resolved. Both require deliberate choices about product, data, people, contracts, and operating continuity.

Ask what the investment thesis actually requires. Financial reporting can be consolidated while products remain separate. Commercial cooperation may need shared customer information without a common application. A unified product may need a deeper integration, with migration and compatibility costs. Each choice should have a value mechanism, an owner, and a feasible sequence.

**Do not treat integration as an automatic benefit.** Combining systems can reduce some duplication while damaging customer fit or slowing development. Preserving autonomy can protect local effectiveness while leaving expensive or risky dependencies. Choose the depth of integration by testing those trade-offs rather than applying a portfolio-wide preference.

Growth from buying businesses must be separated from **organic growth**, changes within an existing business under a stated comparison. A larger revenue number can reflect purchased businesses. Buying smaller firms at one multiple and valuing the group at another creates a valuation mechanism that is distinct from product improvement. Actual returns still depend on financing, costs, timing, and exit conditions.

For carve-outs, distinguish continuity on the first day of separate ownership from a sustainable independent operation. Transition service agreements temporarily provide functions the separated company cannot yet perform alone. Record their scope, service expectations, cost, dependencies, expiration, and exit plan. An allocated historical technology cost is not a reliable forecast of standalone cost.

The fictional example in the full article shows how a €400,000 allocation can become €700,000 of annual standalone spending plus €300,000 of separation cost. The numbers are illustrative; the lesson is to build a dependency-based budget before claiming a margin opportunity.

A Technology Principal helps connect diligence findings to funded integration or separation work. Contracts and ownership boundaries matter as much as interfaces. Confirm rights before moving data, assuming licenses transfer, or consolidating platforms. Success is an economically coherent operating business that can keep customer promises through the transition.
