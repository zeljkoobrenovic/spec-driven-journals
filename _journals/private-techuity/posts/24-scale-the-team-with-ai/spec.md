# Spec: Scale the Team With AI: Capacity Claims Need the Same Evidence as Headcount

## Intent

Give a leader under investors a way to answer the claim that artificial intelligence (AI) changes what the existing team can complete, whether the claim arrives as a reason to reorganize teams to leverage AI better, hire fewer people, as a reason to cut, or as a reason to buy tools. The chapter treats an AI capacity claim exactly as [[fix-decisions-before-hiring]] treats a headcount claim: trace the work, find which constraint the tool touches (decision, knowledge or staffing), measure the change in the work that actually completes, count the cost before payback, and record a decision with a dated gate and the evidence that would reverse it. The reader leaves able to say, for one piece of work, what an AI tool would change, what it would not, what it costs, and what an investor's "AI means fewer people" argument is really asking for.

The chapter is the third team chapter of Part V: scaling up ([[fix-decisions-before-hiring]]), scaling down ([[anatomy-of-a-layoff]]), and scaling with AI. It complements [[ai-strategy-three-questions]] rather than repeating it: that chapter sorts an AI strategy request into three investments; this one takes the capacity question inside the second of them and puts it through the sizing discipline of this part.

## Context

Larkspur, the book's fictional scheduling-software company, is where [[fix-decisions-before-hiring]] left it: the two invoicing specialists' queue remains after the delegation changes, the forecast end-to-end lead time for a pricing change is about ten working days of which seven wait for the two specialists, one billing engineer is being brought into the module, and a second team is deferred pending demand, committed funding and board approval. This chapter continues that scenario with the same people (Alex technology, Priya product, Ines the company, Sam finance, Morgan the investor's adviser) and the same figures, and says so once. One timeline governs every month count: the billing engineer moved into the billing area on the day the hire was funded (day 0 of the hiring chapter's decision); Morgan's note arrives ninety days later; the pilots start about four weeks after the note, once the data review is done, so their first day falls about four months (roughly day 118) after the engineer started. The hiring chapter's six-month transfer review is a reassessment deadline at about day 182 (about pilot day 65, early in the pilot's third month); a solo release in the pilot's first month has its thirty-day invoice follow-up read at the pilot's day-60 reconciliation, about day 177, so its evidence is available by the reassessment. No format assigns the whole first pilot month to one tenure month. The ten-day forecast is the standard case, a change inside Priya's rule catalogue with no decision wait; a rule outside the catalogue adds up to three days (10 to 13). The seven-day queue is the wait for one of the two specialists to be free to take the change up; the specialists build and test it themselves. The shared Larkspur ledger (`_research/shared-scenario-record.md`) is not touched; no new recurring cost is invented.

## Audience

Product and engineering leaders inside companies working under investors, including leaders who inherit an ownership arrangement. Assume no specialist finance background; explain necessary terms before use. Investor-side readers are secondary.

## Success criteria

- **Same trace, new question.** Reuse the hiring chapter's traced pricing change and its three constraints. For each constraint, say whether an AI tool can touch it: decision waits (no; they are authority), the specialists' knowledge queue (only if the tool lets someone other than the two specialists change the module safely, which is a knowledge-transfer question tested the way the hiring chapter tests it), building and release (yes, and measurably). The reader can repeat this sorting on their own trace.
- **Capacity, not activity.** Define capacity as the work that completes, as in the hiring chapter, and reject activity measures (lines written, requests handled, tokens used, tool seats active) as evidence of capacity. Every claimed gain in every format is stated as work completed per period with the same quality checks that applied before the tool.
- **Product and engineering, not developers only.** Give one engineering example (the pricing change) and one implementation or support example (customer setup work, which the book has traced since Part III), and show that the measurement method is the same while the risks differ (code defects versus wrong customer configuration).
- **The evidence box, kept in scope.** Reuse the dated coding-study evidence already registered ([S19] Copilot experiment 2022/2023, [S20] METR early-2025 randomized study, [S21] METR 2026 update) with the distinction between the date an experiment ran and its publication date, the measured quantity and the participant group, and say what none of them measures (Larkspur's own work). Add newer registered evidence only if verified; do not cite from memory.
- **The investor argument as a capacity claim.** Give the "AI means fewer people" argument its own section: separate a formal condition (a funding condition or reserved matter), a director's vote and an adviser's benchmark, as [[anatomy-of-a-layoff]] does for reduction pressure, and answer each with the response it requires. Show one Larkspur version: Morgan proposes that the deferred second team is no longer needed because of AI tools; the chapter tests that claim against the trace instead of accepting or refusing it.
- **Cost before payback, in full.** For the tool pilot, separate additional cash (licences, usage-based charges, any data or security work required by [S18]) from existing employees' time (training, review of generated work, the specialists' time in the pilot), one-off from recurring, and label estimates built from internal time as cost-based estimates rather than cash. No option is described as free because no licence is bought.
- **A recorded decision with a dated gate.** End the Larkspur example with the chosen option, alternatives rejected, funding, scarce capacity, who is authorized, a dated gate with what happens when the condition is met, missed or falls between thresholds, and the evidence that would reopen it. The gate is executable: the measured period is days 1 to 90 of tool use; the gate's sample is defined by completion date (a change that reaches production, or a setup that goes live, on or before day 90), work still open on day 90 is recorded as carried over rather than counted or dropped, and the decision is taken on day 120, when every item in the sample has had thirty days of live evidence (the setup's thirty-day correction window; the reconciliation of every invoice issued up to day 120, run on the pilot's clock). The tools stay on for the same seats and the same work through day 120; items completed in days 91 to 120 are followed the same way and reported beside the gate as interim evidence, outside the sample. The extension runs on the same shape (sixty measured days, days 121 to 180, thirty days of observation, decision on day 210) and a month lost to a tool-free check moves every later date back thirty days; each of these is funded (licences for the observation month, management time to the decision day) and authorized by name. The gain is defined as the pilot's monthly average minus the baseline's, divided by the baseline's, as a percentage, with a worked example and a rule for a zero baseline. The rules are mutually exclusive and cover missing evidence, worse quality, worse lead time, a gain of 0% or less, a gain above 0% and below 20% (one sixty-day extension with a stated terminal rule) and a gain of 20% or more. Quality is a prerequisite for every continuation branch and is defined as a rate against the baseline rate: faulty items per hundred delivered items, faulty items included in the denominator, an item being faulty if it caused at least one error in the same thirty days after delivery (a failed invoice for a change, a customer correction for a setup). The rate counts items, not wrong invoices, because one change can touch many invoices; the number of wrong invoices (the correction burden) and wrong invoices per hundred checked are reported beside it, and a change that breaks many invoices is investigated, but neither is the gate's test, since both depend on invoice volume and on how many invoices a change touches. Every customer setup is followed for thirty days and the engineering check is the full monthly reconciliation with each error traced to the change that caused it and to whether the tool was used on it. Delayed evidence is handled by delivery month: a month's completed rate exists at the end of the following month, when every item in it has had its thirty days; before that its rate is provisional (faulty items found so far over every item delivered that month), which can only rise, so a provisional rate above the baseline at a month-end check is already a breach and triggers the tool-free month from the next day; nothing is switched off retrospectively, and a late error found during a pause joins its own delivery month's count for the restart decision without starting a second pause (worked cases: a setup delivered on day 29 corrected on day 55; a correction found during a pause). The company leader's written restart decision marks the breach it answers as investigated: every tool month delivered before the pause is complete by then, so its rate can no longer change; it stays in the final sample with its faulty items but does not trigger another pause. After a restart only a tool month delivered after it can start another pause, by the same steps, each further tool-free month moving later dates back thirty more days (worked case: a 10% baseline and two faulty setups in ten in the first month; tool off days 31 to 60; restart on day 60; the unchanged 20% does not pause the pilot again on day 90; decision on day 150). A tool-free month after a quality failure is diagnostic evidence, not proof of cause, and the pilot restarts only on an explicit written decision by the company leader. Expected spend, the authorized ceiling and who authorizes an extension or a lengthened month are stated separately. Review burden has a decision attached: a throughput gain that meets the numerical rule is technical eligibility, and the tool continues only if the added reviewer hours (pilot minus baseline, per month) are funded on the gate day by the company leader naming the work they displace, which may not be the specialists' transfer day or committed customer dates; a gain the company cannot afford to review is recorded as such and the tool withdrawn, and every visual that shows the 20% band shows it as conditional on funded review, never as automatic continuation or extra seats (worked case: ten to twelve changes a month with review rising from two to ten hours an item is a 20% gain and twenty to 120 reviewer-hours a month). An interruption is defined precisely: deliveries in the tool-free month go into a diagnostic record outside the sample, marked by whether the tool was used on them; work finished after a restart counts in the month of completion; the sample stays three measured months of tool use (the monthly average divides by three) and every later date moves back thirty days. The month-end check runs at every month end while a tool is in use (days 30, 60, 90 and 120, the extension's month ends, and after the gate for as long as the tool continues), so the observation months are monitored too; on a gate day the sample's own months are judged by the quality rule and the check reads only the months outside the sample. A breach in an observation month never enters the gain calculation, but it holds the gate result: the tool is off for that work for a diagnostic month, and the company leader then decides in writing whether to withdraw it or let the gate result take effect (worked case: a passing sample with a 20% gain, then ten observation-month deliveries with two faulty against a 10% baseline; the gate result is held, the tool is off for days 121 to 150, and the decision falls on day 150). Alex or Priya apply a pause, which the rules make automatic, and report it the same day; only the company leader authorizes an added month, a restart, an extension or the release of a held result. The extension has its own calculation: its sample is the items of the narrowed task types delivered in its two measured months (no initial-pilot or observation deliveries), its monthly average divides by two, and its baseline is the same three pre-pilot months restricted to those task types, divided by three (worked case: 24 qualifying deliveries in two months against ten a month is 20%); a pause keeps two measured months and moves dates back thirty days; task types with no baseline deliveries cannot enter the narrowed measure, and with none left the extension is not granted. Worse lead time withdraws the tool and is recorded as slower delivery; a moved constraint, and review as its place, are named only when the stage-by-stage trace and the reviewers' hours show it. Equally, when building falls, review rises and lead time is unchanged, every format describes review absorbing the building saving and adding pressure to the existing specialist constraint (the reviewers are the two specialists), not a constraint that has moved; the comic's dialogue, caption, transcript and artwork say the same. The review-burden vignette reaches the affordability condition only after the gain, quality and lead-time rules have been met. The decision record puts what a newcomer needs first, in short cells: the normal schedule, the six rules and the affordability condition, with a short safety-stop paragraph; the detailed interruption steps, the shifted schedule, the delayed-evidence cases, the observation-month case and the extension calculation sit in a clearly labelled supporting note at the end of the section. The decision about the deferred second team is explicitly *not* changed by the pilot until the gate is passed.
- **House rules.** Status highlight and key points that read without prior study; terms explained at first use in each format (AI, capacity, lead time, pilot, licence, seat, gate, throughput, delegation, operating budget, payback, invoice reconciliation, invoicing module, customer configuration, board and director, funding condition, term sheet as a proposal that is not itself a funding guarantee); review effort compared as the difference between baseline and pilot reviewer hours, elapsed lead time reported directly, euros, hours, days and percentages kept apart (a throughput gain is never netted against tool charges or review hours; a euro value for saved time needs a stated period and an assumed hourly cost); the seven-day queue stated in every format as a fixed assumption of the calculation that the pilot's lead-time measure tests, never as something a tool can move only through knowledge transfer; a funding round, committed funding, delegation, median, lines accepted and one concrete pricing-rule and customer-setting example explained at first use, with the example reused across formats; the closing quality-failure vignette stated as a rise in the error rate that fails the gate, not as a fall in successful items, with the cause investigated (review, requirements or tests) rather than asserted, and small error differences treated as possible chance to investigate, not as chance; one counting rule in every format (every delivered item counted, errors reported beside the count as a rate, a rise in throughput recognized as capacity only while the rate holds); before/after figures recorded with task mix, demand, staffing and other workflow changes so that the engineer's own learning or easier work is not credited to the tool; the company leader's decision, authority and funding assumptions explicit; historical findings within source scope; fictional scenarios labelled; a meaningful counterargument (the tool may raise output while lowering the quality of what completes, or shift the constraint to review) with the evidence that would change the judgment; hand-off to [[growth-into-design]] with the system constraint still open; a trace (the record of the stages and waits a piece of work passes through), release (putting a finished change into the live system, as distinct from building it) and tokens (small pieces of text or code an AI service processes and bills by, a measure of activity rather than completed work) explained at first use in each format that uses them; the TL;DR states the extension's terminal rule (judged after thirty more days of follow-up; 20% or more with quality, lead time and funded review, or the tool is withdrawn); the TL;DR and the comic each state the month-end safety stop and its consequence (a quality breach in any month of tool use pauses the tool for a month, moves later dates back, and holds even a passing gate result until the company leader's written restart decision) and call days 120 and 210 the normal, uninterrupted dates; the DevEx reading note presents developers' perceptions as a complement to the pilot's workflow measures, not as something reviewer-recorded hours already supply; TL;DR and comic consistent with the article.

## Non-goals

- Not a second treatment of the three AI investment questions, the customer-facing AI product or the substitute threat; those stay in [[ai-strategy-three-questions]] and are linked.
- Not a tool comparison, a vendor recommendation or a prediction of how good AI tools will be; the chapter's claims are about measurement and decision, not about the technology's trajectory.
- Not a reduction method; when the pilot result bears on staffing, the chapter hands the reduction question to [[anatomy-of-a-layoff]].
- No prevalence claims about what companies "usually" gain; no invented study results; no new figures in the shared Larkspur ledger.
