**A positive earnings figure does not establish the cash available to commit.** Work from it to the cash left after investment, taxes, financing payments and any increase in unpaid customer invoices; add bank cash; check dates and restrictions. Borrowing, owners’ agreements and a parent company, the company that owns this one, change the conditions, not the gap.

**EBITDA** is earnings before interest (the cost of borrowing), taxes, depreciation and amortization (charges spreading an asset’s cost, such as equipment or software, over its useful years), a profit measure. **Working capital** here is unpaid customer invoices plus inventory, goods held for sale or use, less unpaid supplier bills.

Fictional Larkspur’s €10 million EBITDA, after €4 million of interest, €1 million of tax, €2 million of capital expenditure (spending on long-lived assets), €1 million of additional working capital and €1.5 million of loan repayment, leaves €0.5 million. The bridge is a **planning calculation, not a payment priority**: financing payments move only under the loan’s terms or an agreed change, tax is required by law, investment is a choice, and salaries sit inside EBITDA.

Adding €0.3 million of cash above the operating reserve, money kept for operating needs, gives about €0.8 million to commit. Alex, who leads engineering, wants €1 million to automate onboarding, the manual setup of each new customer (about 80 staff hours; target 50). Three priced options: a contract specialist for two peak quarters (€75,000); a €210,000 pilot, a small trial of one reusable setup step (€180,000 build, €30,000 first-year maintenance); the full €1 million program. **Ines, the chief executive, approves the pilot** within her delegated limit, the spending the board lets her approve alone; about €0.6 million stays untouched; the full request exceeds it.

Priya, the product leader, measures the first eight customers at day 90; at day 100 the board decides what the evidence funds next, releasing no second-stage money. It may set a month-six gate, a review to pass before more spending; a second stage needs a further eight customers averaging no more than 50 staff hours each, waiting time down, sound cash timing and its own priced approval. A poor result or weak first-half collections means extending the specialist one quarter (€37,500), if available and if Sam, who leads finance, forecasts cash above the reserve at each payment.

A separate debt-free Larkspur scenario has €3 million and spends €250,000 a month more than it collects, so **runway**, how long cash lasts, is 12 months. Hires adding €100,000 a month cut it to about 8.6 months, before funding expected in month nine.

**Covenants**, conditions in a loan agreement, can give lenders rights after one weak quarter. A floating interest rate, which moves with market rates, can raise borrowing costs as sales weaken. **Capitalization**, recording qualifying development spending as an asset, changes when the cost enters profit; the cash still leaves.
