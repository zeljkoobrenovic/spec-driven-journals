Durable success requires **more than a favorable funding valuation** or exit price. The book proposes judging an ownership arrangement by whether the company can keep serving its customers, fund its obligations, reinvest and explain what is unfinished, with the investor’s result alongside those questions. This is an explicit evaluative standard, not an accounting measure. An exit is a decisive reporting event for some investors, not their only measurement horizon; remaining estimated value and continued ownership are also measured. Leaders, customers, employees and suppliers live with the company through and after each event.

The five cases show why. Set side by side as investor result, company result, stakeholder evidence and what is not established, the investor column is the best documented, the stakeholder column the thinnest, and the two results do not move together. Skype paired a valuable sale with a net loss and a later retirement. Toys R Us paired positive operating earnings with a reported net loss and a company that could not fund its trading. TeamSystem paired positive adjusted earnings with a statutory loss under successive owners.

Applied to one fictional Larkspur decision, the standard makes a trade-off visible without removing it. Ending a manual onboarding path in 90 days would remove about €220,000 a year of cost, but ten to twelve of thirty customers could not move in time and the support team and the specialist would carry the cost. The Larkspur board, authorized for the company plan, chooses a two-quarter migration with a funded half-time data service (about €70,000 a year; net saving about €150,000 a year from the following year), names who is accountable, funds the mitigation and records the disagreement, cost and delay against customer commitments, with the evidence that reopens it: fewer than fifteen customers migrated at the first review, or effort still above 50 hours. The decision goes into the handover record so the next owner inherits the obligation, not only the saving.

Wider research tests an investor-only view. Davis and colleagues’ 2024 revision reports two-year employment effects of about −12% after public-to-private buyouts and +15% after private-to-private buyouts, relative to comparison groups over 1980–2013; averages for studied groups, not forecasts. [S08: Davis et al., 2024 revision](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w26371/w26371.pdf) Hospital and nursing-home studies report adverse patient outcomes in specific populations, including 4.6 additional hospital-acquired conditions per 10,000 hospitalizations, a 25.4% relative increase, while another finds improvement on some quality measures; different outcomes cannot cancel. [S12: Kannan et al., adverse events](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/2813379) [S39: Bruch et al., hospital measures](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/fullarticle/2769549) A small 2025 study of resold hospitals finds lower margins after a second private equity sale, a financial result, not a patient one. [S42: Kannan and Song, after sale](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama-health-forum/fullarticle/2837043) For software the point is a mechanism: where customers cannot observe quality or switch providers, financial incentives may not protect them.

Durable capability can include a deliberately funded continuing specialist service the company chose and can inspect. When the standard does not settle a question, name the authorized decision-maker, the groups that lose, the funded mitigation and the review date. The next team should inherit a company that can serve its customers, fund its obligations and explain what still needs to change.
