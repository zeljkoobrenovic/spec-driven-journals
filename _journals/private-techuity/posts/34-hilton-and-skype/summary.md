**A successful exit still needs explaining.** Hilton and Skype both ended in large sales, but by very different paths: Hilton’s ran through financing and time, Skype’s through technology rights and a buyer who wanted the product.

![Two timelines: Hilton, with the OnQ system in place since 2003, bought with debt in 2007, its debt cut by $4.0 billion in 2010 and the exit completed in 2018 at 3.1 times; Skype, valued at $2.75 billion in 2009, its technology rights settled in 2009 and bought by Microsoft for $8.5 billion in 2011; a scale shows that a company price is not a fund return, and two question cards ask about financing and time for Hilton and about rights and buyer value for Skype.](assets/images/34-hilton-and-skype/summary-at-a-glance.jpeg)
**Figure 1:** *Two valuable sales by different paths: Hilton’s ran through financing and time, Skype’s through technology rights and a buyer who wanted it.*

Blackstone bought the Hilton hotel group in 2007, largely with borrowed money, just before the financial crisis. In 2010 a restructuring cut Hilton’s debt by $4.0 billion. About $3.9 billion of that came from the owner choosing to keep supporting the company: it put in $819 million to buy back loans below the amount owed and cancelled about $2.0 billion of loans it held itself. **The cut lowered debt and interest; it was not $4.0 billion to spend on technology.** [S23: Hilton registration filing](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1585689/000119312513364703/d593452ds1.htm)

OnQ, Hilton’s own system for running each hotel’s daily operations, links a network in which most hotels belong to other owners. But Hilton introduced it in 2003, four years before the purchase. [S64: Hilton 2003 annual filing](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/47580/000104746904007535/a2128996z10-k.htm) Blackstone inherited the platform; later filings describe improvements without measuring what they added.

Hilton’s shares returned to a stock market in 2013, and Blackstone’s funds sold theirs from 2014 to 2018. Blackstone then reported about 3.1 times investors’ money and $14 billion of profit. [S24: Blackstone 2018 investor call](https://ir.blackstone.com/files/doc_events/BLACKSTONE-Second-Quarter-2018-Earnings-Investor-Call.pdf) Those are the sponsor’s own figures, not what each outside investor in its fund received after fees and the manager’s share of the profits.

Skype’s path was shorter. In 2009 eBay sold about 70% of the internet-calling business to a Silver Lake-led group at a $2.75 billion valuation, and kept about 30%. [S26: eBay Skype sale announcement](https://investors.ebayinc.com/investor-news/press-release-details/2009/EBay-Inc-Completes-Sale-of-Skype/default.aspx) That November a legal settlement brought the rights to Skype’s core technology into the company. Until then, the product rested on technology someone else owned, a risk no code review would have caught. Group video calling and the purchase of Qik, a mobile video company, followed in January 2011. [S27: Skype registration filing](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1498209/000119312511096544/ds1a.htm) That October, Microsoft, which wanted Skype inside its own communications business, bought it for $8.5 billion. [S28: Microsoft Skype completion](https://news.microsoft.com/source/2011/10/13/microsoft-officially-welcomes-skype/)

**Two company prices cannot give a fund’s return.** Dividing $8.5 billion by $2.75 billion ignores that the fund owned only part of Skype, that lenders were repaid first, and the costs and dates in between. And Skype’s retirement in May 2025 belongs to its later product history; it neither erases the 2011 result nor shows that earlier decisions caused it. [S29: Microsoft Skype retirement](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/skype/22ccebb6-0cf9-4e1d-916f-aaf978e1b129)

Neither case offers complete fund cash flows or an independent split of the gain between operations, financing, markets and the buyer. Each does offer a question for your own company. **Hilton asks whether you have the financing and time your operating plan needs. Skype asks whether you control the rights your product depends on, and what a buyer values.**
