**When an investor arrives, more people seem to have a say, and a passing remark can sound like an instruction.** Before committing, establish who is authorized to decide, who funds the work and by when.

![An adviser's coffee remark is marked advice only and becomes an option to cost; a decision record lists recommend, decide, fund and deliver, with the chief executive approving up to €500,000 and the board keeping reserve draws and new hires; a timeline forks at the May board decision on hires into October with the hires or January without them.](assets/images/06-clarify-authority/summary-at-a-glance.jpeg)
**Figure 1:** *Treat advice as an option, write down who recommends, decides, funds and delivers, and make any date that depends on a pending approval conditional.*

In a fictional case, Morgan, the investor's technology adviser, mentions over coffee that Larkspur should probably change its cloud provider. Alex, who leads technology, hears an instruction and starts planning a migration. Morgan meant it as an idea. Nobody had written down who decides.

Start with what the investment changed: who holds which rights, and which decisions need whose approval. At Larkspur, the board lets Ines, the chief executive, approve up to €500,000 inside the approved plan. Whatever the amount, the board keeps two kinds of decision: drawing on its reserve or changing what an approved budget funds, and any permanent hire outside the headcount plan. Morgan holds no approval right; the investor acts through its board seat. The cloud idea becomes an option for Alex to cost, not a plan.

Then fill in a decision record with **names, thresholds and dates**, because "management recommends, the board approves" tells nobody whom to call, or by when. For a €1 million proposal to automate customer onboarding, Priya, the product leader, and Alex recommend; Ines resolves trade-offs; the board must decide within two weeks, because the amount exceeds her limit. When investors ask for different things, take the choice to the body that can settle it. **Do not carry conflicting requests as parallel plans.**

The board declines the €1 million program this year but confirms a €210,000 pilot that Ines had already approved within her limit. The new setup step works for common customer types from February, and the first eight customers are measured by 1 April. Covering every customer type takes until next January with the current team, or until October if two implementation specialists start in June. Those hires are permanent headcount, so the board decides them in May. **"October, subject to the May decision and two June starts"** is an honest commitment; an unconditional October promise hides both dependencies.

Plan the missed deadline, too. If May passes without a decision, or the hires cannot start in June, Alex takes the January option back to Ines, the team is told in writing that January is the plan, and every customer promise tied to October gets an explicit decision.

Reports should ask for a decision, not just show a traffic light. At the April review the update takes three sentences: setup effort fell from 80 to 62 hours, short of the 50 assumed; much of the rest comes from poor customer data; management asks the board to approve a €40,000 data-quality step from the reserve. It goes to the board, far below Ines's limit, because drawing on the reserve for new work changes what the budget funds.
