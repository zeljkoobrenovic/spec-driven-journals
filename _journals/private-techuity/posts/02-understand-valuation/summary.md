**A valuation estimates what a business, or its shares, is worth at a date and for a purpose.** You will rarely produce one, but its assumptions can become your team's growth, margin and cost targets, so it pays to know how to read it.

Suppose a buyer tells you Rotaline is worth €60 million. Two questions unlock that number: what is being valued, and how was the estimate made?

Start with the business's own numbers, which answer different questions. **Revenue** is what the company records as sales in a period, whether or not the customer has paid yet. Profit is what remains after the costs a particular measure includes. **Cash flow** is money actually moving in and out. In a fictional year, €20 million of revenue leaves €4 million of EBITDA, a profit measure taken before interest, tax and the accounting charges that spread an asset's cost over time. After those, net profit is €1.5 million. None of these figures is a bank balance.

Next, what is being valued. Enterprise value is the worth of the operating business; equity value is the worth of its shares, after subtracting net debt, the borrowing minus the cash the valuation counts. If €60 million is the business and net debt is €20 million, the shares are worth €40 million, before other adjustments. Neither figure is money the company has received.

Then, how the estimate was made. Three common approaches each have a weakness. **Market comparisons** borrow multiples from similar businesses: €60 million is 3 times this company's revenue or 15 times its EBITDA, but at 12 times EBITDA it would be €48 million. **Discounted cash flow** turns expected future cash into today's value and is only as good as its forecast. **Asset-based valuation** adds up what the business owns, less what it owes, and can miss the value of a working organization. Growth is not a fourth method; it shapes the assumptions of all three.

A funding-round price answers a different question again. In a separate scenario, a smaller Rotaline raises its first outside money. Its shares are worth €8 million before the round and an investor pays €2 million for new shares, so all the shares are worth €10 million afterward and the investor owns 20%. The company receives €2 million, not €10 million.

For you, the assumptions are the useful part. Suppose a buyer's model reaches €60 million by expecting revenue to double over four years while the cost of setting up each new customer falls by a third. At Rotaline each setup takes about 80 hours, much of it manual configuration, and more sales will not shrink that. **Only a specific change will.** So ask which change produces the saving, when it becomes usable and who funds it. If the answers are "nothing specific", "not yet" and "nobody", **revise the target before the plan**.
