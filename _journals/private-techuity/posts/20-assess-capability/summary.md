**Before a plan commits money or makes promises, check whether the systems and the team can actually carry it.** Start from what the plan needs, judge the technology by its consequences for that work, and end with a written finding the next decisions can use.

Take Rotaline, the fictional scheduling-software company this book follows. The board wants its first paying customers in a second country within twelve months, a date set before anyone checked what it depends on. So Alex, the technology leader, starts with the capability the plan requires: invoice, contract with, support and set up customers under that country’s tax and pricing rules.

A generic review would report that parts of the system depend too closely on each other. That is true, but it doesn’t help. The useful version is specific: Rotaline’s country rules live inside the invoicing module, and the contract tool, the support software and the product settings all read them there. Entering a new market therefore needs coordinated change in billing, legal work, support and configuration, not just engineering. And every pricing change passes through **the same two specialists** and takes about three weeks from request to the live system.

Strengths belong in the assessment too. Rotaline’s scheduling product is stable, the team knows its customers’ operations deeply, and it releases weekly without disruption. **Old does not mean inadequate**; a report that lists only defects invites a new owner to dismantle what it doesn’t yet understand.

The evidence needs care. Measured from the moment code is recorded, a pricing change reaches customers in under two days. Measured from the request, it takes three weeks. Most of the time sits before the code, but that alone doesn’t show whether it is waiting or building. Someone has to trace one real change to find out.

Then there is the tempting answer: replace the core. Alex’s fictional estimate is **€1.3 million of additional spending over eighteen months**, for four extra people, running old and new systems side by side, and moving customer data. Even if it were funded tomorrow, first invoices from the new system would arrive around month twelve at the earliest, with no margin against the board’s date and about €720,000 already paid. The maintenance saving comes only once the old system retires. If demand in the new country is still untested, a small, bounded trial may be the wiser commitment.

The finding pulls this together: the capability required, the evidence, the strengths, the constraint, what is uncertain, and what a transition would cost and when. Why a small change takes three weeks is the question [[scale-the-team-up]] answers next.
