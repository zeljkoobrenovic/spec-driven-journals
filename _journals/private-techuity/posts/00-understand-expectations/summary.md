**Every euro a company spends comes from someone who expects something back.** A budget tells you how much your team may spend; the financing behind it decides when, with whose approval and what the company owes in return.

Customers pay for the product, sometimes in advance. Lenders expect repayment with interest. Owners buy shares, units of ownership, and get rights over future decisions instead of a repayment schedule. Each route comes with different strings.

Take Larkspur, a fictional scheduling-software company still owned by its founders. Three customers want a new dispatch feature by 1 March, and delivering it takes €100,000 that Larkspur does not have. Its subscriptions leave only about €1,500 a month after running costs. Sam, who manages the company's money, has three offers for the same amount:

- **A customer prepayment.** The three customers pay a year in advance. The 1 March date becomes a contract term, and a late feature costs one month's service back.
- **A bank loan.** Three years at a fixed 6%: about €3,040 a month, with repayments starting before the customers have paid anything.
- **New shares.** An investor pays €100,000 for about 10% of the company, a board seat, a veto over unplanned spending above €50,000 and the expectation that Larkspur will eventually be sold.

**The same money buys very different promises.** The prepayment is the cheapest money and the tightest promise, and Larkspur chose it. Two engineers, already paid from subscriptions, build the feature in four weeks and finish by 13 February. The customers' cash pays for everything else, including a €10,000 reserve. The loan failed Sam's rule: its repayments were twice the monthly surplus, so they would have rested on the reserve and on customer money not yet collected. The shares would have given away rights that outlast the feature, for a need customers were willing to fund.

Timing mattered as much as the choice. Ines, the chief executive, countersigns nothing until the evidence is in: three signed orders and an estimate of no more than twelve engineer-weeks. With only two customers, a €35,000 loan would fit within the surplus if the board approved it in time. With one, no affordable loan closes the gap, so the release shrinks to what that customer pays for. Until she signs, no date is promised.

One more distinction matters once investors appear. **Only newly issued shares put money into the company.** When an investor buys a founder's existing shares, or shares change hands on a stock exchange, the seller is paid, not the business. Labels such as venture capital or private equity help you get your bearings, but only the actual agreement tells you what the money requires.

So for any funding source, ask three questions: **who receives the cash, what obligations come with it and who approves how it is spent**. [[understand-funding-control]] applies them to a €100 million announcement.
