**Technical debt** is the extra cost a company carries because its software is harder to change than it needs to be. At Larkspur, a fictional software company, an investor’s adviser asks the **board** (its directors): how much debt, and why not rewrite? The answer: a **carrying cost** per item, in three columns, and fixes funded in **tranches**, separately approved stages, each released by a **gate**, an agreed check that the stage delivered and met its safeguards.

**Figure 1:** *Three columns per item; gated tranches; close dates weighed first.*

Larkspur shortlists five debt items: tangled invoicing code; outdated reporting software; a scheduling engine running each region on one server; artificial intelligence (AI) instructions built into the code; and a vendor AI model being retired. **Cost carried** counts workaround hours and rented servers, as supplier cash or existing staff time. **Risk carried** counts service failures, software without security fixes and vendor retirements. **Speed lost** counts delivery delays and customers the product cannot serve, as sales not won. The five cost about €10,000 a month, €5,500 of it cash; recovering staff time frees no cash. The engine takes 45 minutes to **fail over** to a replacement server and blocks customers above 400 technicians: €12,000 a month not won.

A **rewrite** of the engine means nine months with the old one **frozen** (unchanged) and about €340,000 (€300,000 of staff time, €40,000 cash). Tranche 1 instead splits the schedule computation from the part answering customers, so it can fail over to a second server and servers can shrink to fit: in hand at month three for about €58,000. Tranche 2 adds the largest customers at month seven for €80,000.

**Reporting first.** The reporting software lost its security fixes in March 2027; the largest prospect’s security review demands supported software by March 2028. Close deadlines are weighed first: the cost of missing them against harm undated items do now. So the move to the supported version (six engineer-weeks, €5,000) runs in November–December 2027, approved by Ines, the chief executive, under her delegation.

**The retiring model.** The AI model that sorts customers’ documents retires on 31 March 2028. Larkspur had approved, not yet signed, a discounted agreement to pay the vendor fixed monthly amounts through 2028. Alex tests the successor model by 29 October; Ines signs only if it passes. If it fails, nothing is signed and a replacement is chosen by 29 November. The feature switches in February, leaving March spare. From then, a passing model sorts automatically; on a failed one, planners who agreed the time confirm each sort; other customers’ sorting is suspended. Spending beyond the approved AI budget needs the board.

**The engine.** Tranche 1 is in the 2028 plan the board approves in January; tranche 2 is released only if the gate passes on 31 March 2028: failover under five minutes, hosting down €1,500 a month, no incident from the split, delivery time no worse. Invoicing tangles are carried deliberately at about €3,000 a month, with a written reason and review date.
