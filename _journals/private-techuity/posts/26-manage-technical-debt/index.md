> **IN THIS SECTION, YOU WILL:** Learn to answer “how much technical debt do we have?” with figures the board can act on. Technical debt is the extra cost, risk and delay a company carries because its software is harder to change than it needs to be. You will put each item on three columns (what it costs to keep, what risk it carries, what it slows), present the fix by what it buys, and fund a large project in stages that each have to earn the next.

> **WHY INVESTORS CARE:** An investor hears “technical debt” as spending that changes nothing a customer can see, and “rewrite” as a year of engineering with the benefit at the end. A register that shows what the debt costs, risks and slows each month, and what each stage of spending removes, makes both fundable.

> **WHY YOU SHOULD CARE:** An issue count or a percentage gives the board nothing to decide with. Three figures per item let the board fund the fix the way it funds anything else, and let you say which debt you are choosing to carry, until when, and why.


> **KEY POINTS:**
>
> * **Debt is a carrying cost with three columns.** What it costs to keep, in euros a month; what risk it carries; what it slows. The last two keep their own units. Never a count, a percentage or a ratio.
> * **Present the fix by what it buys.** The revenue it protects or enables, the cost or risk it removes and, only for a real dated decision, the choice it keeps open. Show the register before and after, because the board never sees the failures a fix prevented.
> * **Large projects are transitions, funded in tranches.** A tranche is a separately approved stage of work and spending; a gate is the agreed check that the stage delivered what it promised and worsened nothing it had to protect. A rewrite with its whole benefit at the end is right only when no useful half-way state exists.

<br>
**Technical debt** is the extra cost a company carries because its software was built, or has aged, in ways that make it slower, riskier or more expensive to change than it needs to be. It is not a loan from anyone; the word is a metaphor for a burden that keeps being paid. A concrete example: a scheduling company whose software runs each region on one large server cannot sell to customers above a certain size, and turned two of them away last year. Nothing on any bill records that loss.

The word suggests a single number that could be paid off. What the company actually carries is three costs every month, and the work that reduces them is a project: a **refactor** (reworking software without changing what it does for customers), a **migration** (moving software or data from one platform, system or vendor to another) or a **rewrite** (replacing a system with a new one). Where each item came from matters as much as its size, because each origin has a different owner and a different remedy. The register this chapter builds records both.

The three chapters that follow in this part each deepen one column of the register built here: [[prove-you-can-restore]] tests the risk behind a saving before the saving is counted, and [[cheaper-cloud-bill]] and [[ai-worth-its-cost]] put a metered bill (the cloud bill and the bill for artificial intelligence (AI) services) on a unit of useful work before deciding what to commit. Technical debt is the bill without an invoice, and it gets the same treatment first.

## Setting Up the Example: Larkspur’s Annual Technology Review

Larkspur is the fictional scheduling-software company this book follows. Alex leads technology as chief technology officer (CTO), Priya leads product, Sam leads finance, and Ines is the chief executive who leads the company. Morgan is the technology adviser to Larkspur’s investor, the firm that owns a large share of the company. The **board** is the group of directors that oversees the company and reserves certain decisions, larger spending among them, for itself. Every figure in what follows is fictional.

It is October 2027. Morgan, preparing the board’s annual technology review, has asked Alex two questions in one sentence: how much technical debt does Larkspur have, and would it not be simpler to rewrite the old parts?

Larkspur has already paid one such debt down. In the chapter [[growth-into-design]], it chose to move the country-specific billing rules out of the **invoicing module**, the part of the software that prepares customer invoices, into a separate component that Larkspur owns. The month-four **replay**, in which last year’s invoices were run through the new code and compared line by line with the bills actually sent, passed. The second country went live in month eight, the billing engineer now maintains the component, and adding a new country is a settings change rather than a change to fragile code. That project is the model for what follows: a stated requirement, a gate, a fallback and a benefit that arrived when the stage did, not at the end of a rewrite.

## A Carrying Cost With Three Columns

A **static-analysis tool**, software that checks code for known problems without running it, will produce an issue count; a consultant will produce a percentage of the code; both put a number on something the board cannot act on. The useful answer is a **carrying cost**: what the debt costs the company while it remains, in three columns that the board already knows how to read. The first column is money per month. The other two keep their own units, incidents, minutes, dates, days of delay and revenue per month, and are read alongside it, not added to it.

| Column | What it counts | Where the figure comes from |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Cost carried** | Engineering hours spent on workarounds, manual steps and repeated fixes; software licences (paid permission to use software) and hosting (the servers that run it) kept for the old component that a current design would not need | The trace from [[fix-decisions-before-hiring]], the record of how one change moved through the team from request to delivery; the cloud bill’s unit figures from [[cheaper-cloud-bill]]; each labelled cash or cost-based estimate |
| **Risk carried** | Incidents (service failures) the component causes; components no longer supported or patched; a **restore**, bringing data and service back from a backup after a failure, that fails or takes too long; a vendor’s retirement with a date | The incident log; the vendor’s support dates; the restore evidence from [[prove-you-can-restore]]; the retirement notice from [[ai-worth-its-cost]] |
| **Speed lost** | Lead time on the change types customers pay for; releases that cannot go out; deals the product cannot take because of a limit the design imposes | The same trace, per change type; the sales log of declined or delayed deals, stated as revenue not won, never as a loss |

**Cash**, in this chapter’s comparisons, is shorthand for *additional supplier cash*: money paid to someone outside the company, a hosting provider or a vendor, on top of what the company already spends. Salaries are paid in cash too, but they are paid whether or not this work is done, so they are counted differently. A **cost-based estimate** is existing staff time valued at what the company already pays to employ that staff, expressed in euros. The engineers are paid whichever work they do, so the estimate allocates salary that is already in the budget to this work; freeing the time does not by itself reduce payroll or release cash, unless a person leaves or is not hired. What it does is make the time available for work that earns something.

Larkspur values engineering time at about €2,000 an **engineer-week** (one person’s working week; six engineer-weeks can be one engineer for six weeks or two engineers for three), roughly €400 an **engineer-day**, its average fully loaded cost of an engineer, salary and employer charges together; every cost-based figure in this chapter uses that rate. **Lead time** is the elapsed working days from an agreed change request to its delivery to customers, and **revenue** is sales income before any costs are deducted.

The third column is the one a cost discussion leaves out, and it is often the largest. A design that caps the size of customer the product can serve does not appear on any bill. It appears as **prospects**, potential customers, that the sales team stopped calling.

Added up, the five cost about €5,500 a month of cash and about 11 engineer-days of time, valued at about €4,600 (the three rounded rows added; eleven days at the flat rate would be €4,400): about €10,000 a month in all. The €5,500 is external hosting payments attributable to these items; the rest is employment cost already in the budget, allocated to them. Recovering the eleven days would not by itself create a €4,600 cash saving; it would give the company eleven engineer-days a month for work that earns something. The risk and speed columns are not added: item 1 causes two invoicing incidents a quarter, item 3 caused the engine’s last two incidents of the year and fails over in 45 minutes, and item 3 alone keeps about €12,000 a month of revenue from being won.

**Origin decides the owner and the usual remedy**, and the next section takes the four origins one at a time. And **a deliberate shortcut with a date and an owner is not a failure of record**: item 4 was recorded as a choice in December and is being revisited on its own terms. The record does not prove the shortcut was wise; it is what lets the company say so, on the date, if it was not. An undated shortcut is a failure of record, whatever the code looks like.

## Where the Debt Came From

The origins deserve a moment each, because the board will ask, reasonably, how the company got here, and the answer is different for each item.

**A deliberate, dated shortcut** is a decision the company made with its eyes open: ship the trial with the prompt in code, revisit when the feature has thirty customers. The record is what makes it debt rather than neglect, and what makes it accountable: the revisit is the moment to say whether the shortcut was the right call, and the register carries the revisit condition next to the item so that the date is not forgotten.

**Growth that outran a design** is the ordinary condition of a company that is doing well, and it is the product and engineering leaders’ to fix, in stages, as the invoicing extraction was. The single-node scheduling engine was the right design for customers with forty technicians; it was not wrong when it was built, and it is wrong now. The invoicing module coupled its country rules because there was one country. This is the debt the chapter [[growth-into-design]] taught the company to treat as a design choice among options, and it is the debt this chapter funds in stages, because it has no external date and its benefit can be taken in stages.

**An acquired system not yet integrated** carries the previous owner’s shortcuts and its own, and belongs to whoever owns the integration decision; the chapter [[acquisition-adds-work-first]] treats the integration, making the bought company’s systems work with the buyer’s, as a transition with its own funding and dates, and the case chapter [[teamsystem]] shows that work continuing across several changes of owner. Larkspur has none today. The register would gain a row for it if the purchase in the investor’s plan happens, with the deal’s date as its date.

**An ageing platform or a retiring vendor** is the one origin with a date the company does not control; Alex owns it, because the notice lands with technology. Its remedy is mostly a scheduling question: the date decides when the work happens, and the register decides how much of it is worth doing. It goes first when the date is close enough that waiting would miss it; a distant date with little work behind it can wait.

## Present the Fix by What It Buys

Morgan’s second question, about rewriting, is answered by first answering one Morgan did not ask: what would a fix buy? The board reads a technology investment the way it reads any other. **Software architecture** is how a system’s parts are organised, and so how easily they can be changed. The author’s [Grounded Architecture](https://grounded-architecture.io/economics) framework, applied to a group of companies in the appendix chapter [[grounded-architecture-portfolio]], sorts the benefits of architecture work into three kinds: what raises or protects revenue, what reduces cost or risk, and the decision a design keeps open. The third is easy to overstate.

- **Revenue protected or enabled.** Two prospects above 400 technicians, about €12,000 a month between them, that the scheduling engine cannot serve; the largest prospect’s security review, which the reporting stack fails. Revenue not won is stated as such: it is a reason to fund the work, not a loss the company has recorded in its accounts, and removing the limit does not guarantee the sale.
- **Cost or risk removed.** €1,500 a month of hosting kept for the old reporting stack; about €4,000 a month of oversized servers; a 45-minute failover, discussed below; an unsupported component with a known class of vulnerability.
- **A decision kept open.** Splitting the schedule computation from the rest of the engine keeps open the later decision to replace the engine’s core, which Larkspur would take only if the evidence after the split says the ceiling remains, and whose cost would be estimated then. Gregor Hohpe describes architecture as selling options: the right, but not the obligation, to take a later decision. In finance an option fixes the price of that later decision in advance; an architectural option only keeps the door open, and the price is estimated when the decision is taken. Larkspur uses the looser sense and says so. The value of such a choice rises with uncertainty, and Larkspur has plenty. [Architecture: Selling Options](https://architectelevator.com/architecture/architecture-options/)

The chapter [[cheaper-cloud-bill]] records two promises: an appointment is confirmed within two seconds, which is **response time**, and the service is usable 99.9% of the month, which is **availability**. A failover is a third thing: how long the service is down while work moves to a replacement server. Availability of 99.9% allows about 43 minutes of downtime in a 30-day month, so one real failover of 45 minutes would use up the whole month’s allowance on its own. Tranche 1’s target of under five minutes is set against that allowance, not against the two-second response time, which the split does not change.

The third kind of benefit is used only with a named decision and a date, never as a general virtue, because it cannot be priced. Neither can the first two with precision.

Martin Fowler argues that high **internal quality**, code that is easy to understand and change, makes later change cheaper. That argument is why Larkspur chose to own the billing boundary in the design chapter. That chapter’s figures, under stated assumptions, show the owned design reaching the same cumulative cash as the supplier option after two years of the supplier’s subscription fees, and costing less from the third year.

That point is a **crossover** between two alternatives. It is not a **payback**, the point at which a project’s benefits have repaid what it cost. Fowler’s own caveat is that nobody can measure the functionality a team delivers well enough to draw such a crossover exactly. [Is High Quality Software Worth the Cost?](https://martinfowler.com/articles/is-quality-worth-cost.html) So the fix is judged on the columns it moves in the register, before and after, with the dates the moves are measured.

Nelson Repenning and John Sterman showed why improvement programmes are starved: the benefit of a problem that never happened is invisible, while the effort to prevent it is on this quarter’s budget, so organisations keep working harder on the symptoms instead of working better on the cause. [Nobody Ever Gets Credit for Fixing Problems that Never Happened](https://web.mit.edu/nelsonr/www/Repenning=Sterman_CMR_su01_.pdf) The register is the answer. It turns “nothing happened” into a measured difference: incidents per quarter before and after, failover time before and after, lead time before and after. A board that is shown the after column is not being asked to take the fix on faith.

## Large Projects Are Transitions

Now the rewrite question, on the item where it is most tempting: the scheduling engine. Alex compares three ways to reduce item 3, on one basis. Effort is counted in engineer-weeks and valued at the stated rate as a cost-based estimate; cash is listed separately, as the neighbouring chapters do. A **tranche** is a separately approved stage of the work and its spending, and a **gate** is the check, agreed before the stage starts, that has to pass before the next tranche is funded.

| | A. Rewrite the engine | B. Tranches with gates | C. Live with it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| What it is | A new engine that spreads the work across servers, built alongside the old one, switched over when complete | Tranche 1: split the schedule computation from the **web layer**, the part that answers customers’ requests, so the computation can run on a second server and fail over. Tranche 2: **partition** the computations by customer, dividing them into separate groups, so the largest customers run on their own **workers**, servers dedicated to their jobs. Tranche 3, only if the evidence after tranche 2 says the ceiling remains: replace the computation core | Keep the design; state its cost and review it |
| Effort and cash | Four engineers for nine months, about 150 engineer-weeks, a cost-based estimate of about €300,000, plus €40,000 of cash: €10,000 of tooling at the start and €30,000 for three months of **dual running**, operating the old and new engines side by side, in months seven to nine. About €340,000 in all | Tranche 1: two engineers for three months, about 26 engineer-weeks, about €50,000 cost-based plus €8,000 cash, about €58,000. Tranche 2: two engineers for four months, about 35 engineer-weeks, about €70,000 cost-based plus €10,000 cash, about €80,000. Both stages, if both are funded: about €138,000. Tranche 3: estimated only if reached | Nothing new; the carrying cost continues |
| When the benefit arrives | Month nine at the earliest, all three columns at once, and nothing customer-visible before then | Tranche 1 at month three: failover and rightsizing. Tranche 2 at month seven: large customers. Each measured before the next is funded | Never |
| What waits | The customer portal redesign, the rebuild of the website customers use, for the whole nine months: the rewrite needs four engineers from January 2028, the two named for the redesign and two taken from customer-facing feature work, whose features wait the same nine months. Every performance fix during the build, because the old engine is **frozen**, still running but no longer changed | The customer portal redesign: three months if the work stops after tranche 1 (its build moves from January to March to April to June), seven months if tranche 2 starts in April (August to October), longer if approval or the gate slips | Nothing; the declined prospects keep declining |
| Risk | The switch-over is the moment every risk arrives together; the old engine is frozen for nine months; if the new one is late, both are paid for | Each tranche touches the live engine, so each carries **regression risk**, the danger of breaking what already worked, and each is tested against last year’s schedules in replay before it goes live, as the invoicing extraction was | The next incident, and the next declined prospect |
| Money already gone | At month five, about €170,000 of effort and the €10,000 of tooling are gone whether the rewrite finishes or stops: about €180,000. The decision to continue is about the remaining €130,000 of effort and €30,000 of dual running, about €160,000, and four more frozen months, set against the benefit still expected | After tranche 1, about €58,000 is gone and the failover benefit is already in hand; the decision to fund tranche 2 is about tranche 2’s €80,000 and its benefit | Nothing is spent |

Money that has been spent is gone the moment it is spent, whichever option is chosen and whether or not any benefit has arrived; that is what makes it a **sunk cost**, and a sunk cost should not tip the next decision either way. What each review compares is the remaining cost, the remaining risk and the benefit still to come. The chapter [[growth-into-design]] made the same point at its own month four. What the rewrite defers is not the counting of its cost but the arrival of its benefit: at month five, €180,000 is gone and nothing has been bought yet, and the same is true at month eight. The tranche plan is the shape that keeps each remaining decision small and puts a benefit in hand before the next sum is committed.

**When the benefit-at-the-end shape is right anyway.** The reporting stack’s framework has been unsupported since March, a prospect’s security review requires supported components by the end of March 2028, and there is no useful half-way state: the supported version builds reports differently, so upgrading in place is the same work as moving to it, and the stack is either on the old version or on the new one. Its migration is a small project with the benefit at the end, six engineer-weeks (about €12,000 cost-based) and about €5,000 of cash, and it is done first, in November and December 2027, ahead of item 3 whose carrying cost is larger, because its date is close and its effort is small. Of the three shapes compared here, the benefit-at-the-end project is right only when no intermediate state is worth having.

The **sequencing rule** is written into the register in three steps.

1. **Find the items whose external date is close.** An item qualifies when the time left before its date is not much more than the work needs: the estimate plus a margin for the work running over. It must start while finishing on time is still possible. A distant retirement that needs little work does not qualify yet; its latest start is written next to it.
2. **Weigh each of them against the harm now.** Whether a close-dated item goes ahead of an undated one is decided by setting what missing the date would cost against the harm the undated item is doing now. Items brought forward go in date order.
3. **Order the rest by what each tranche removes for its effort.** Read across all three columns, not money alone. A large repair that removes a large cost can rank below a small repair that removes a smaller one, if the small repair removes more per engineer-week.

**Figure 3:** *The sequencing rule written into the register: an item whose external date leaves little more time than the work needs, plus a margin for overrun, is weighed first, by what missing the date would cost against the harm undated items do now; items brought forward go in date order. The rest follow in order of the cost, risk and delay each tranche of effort would remove, read across all three columns. A distant date with little work behind it can wait.*

Item 5 is the same rule with a longer fuse, and its dates have to be kept in order, because the notice arrived before the vendor agreement was signed. At its September review the chapter [[ai-worth-its-cost]] approved a **commitment**, an agreement to pay the vendor fixed monthly amounts through 2028 in return for a discount. Ines is to sign it in November, and the vendor holds the offer open until 30 November. That chapter also lists a retirement notice among the evidence that reopens the decision. The vendor’s notice of 30 September 2027 named the successor model and gave usable access to it the same day. So the order is: test first, sign only on a pass.

- **Samples by 29 October 2027.** Alex and one engineer from the AI construction work in [[ai-worth-its-cost]] (the changes to how the feature is built and sends requests) run the release-gate samples on the successor: about two engineer-days inside work already funded, with the two migration engineers untouched. That is a month before Larkspur’s decision date and the end of the offer, so that a failure leaves time to test an alternative.
- **Signature by 30 November 2027, only if the samples pass.** Sam first prices the successor’s expected usage, because its price may differ from the old model’s, and Ines signs once the cost chapter’s own conditions are also met. From then on, the contract’s retirement rights, to test a successor and to end the commitment if it fails, protect Larkspur against any later retirement in 2028.
- **Decision by 29 November 2027, if the samples fail.** Ines signs nothing, so there is nothing to terminate and no fixed payment is owed; usage stays at flexible list prices, as today. Larkspur’s own sixty-day rule from the cost chapter, counted from access, sets the date. The choice is between another vendor’s model tested on the same samples in November (about two engineer-weeks from the same AI construction line) and the failed successor carrying the review-aid mode described below. A commitment to another vendor would be sized afresh by the cost chapter’s method.
- **If access comes later.** Every date moves with access: samples within twenty-nine days of it, the decision within sixty. Access on 30 October would mean samples by 28 November, still inside the offer, and the decision by 29 December. Ines never signs on an untested successor; if the offer lapses first, usage stays at list prices and any commitment is sized afresh. While access is missing, Alex tests another vendor’s model, the same two engineer-weeks. The decision cannot wait past 31 January 2028, sixty days before retirement, so that February is left for the switch. Without usable access by then, Larkspur decides that day without the successor.
- **Switch in February 2028; retirement on 31 March 2028.** The old model stays available until it retires, but Larkspur does not wait for that day. The switch, construction work in the AI line, is planned for February, which leaves March to put right anything that goes wrong; it happens on 31 March at the latest. The decision date is not the delivery date, which is why the samples are not left until March.

**The fallback, branch by branch.** A failed test in October changes nothing customers see until the switch day. From that day, what the feature runs on depends on what has been measured:

- **A model that passed the samples.** Automated sorting continues on it: the successor, or another vendor’s model tested on the same samples. This is the plan, not the fallback.
- **Only models that were tested and failed.** The **review-aid mode** the trial built can run on one of them: the feature proposes a category and the customer’s own planner confirms it before scheduling. The mode still needs a model; it just stops trusting that model on its own. It switches on per customer without engineering work, from the switch day, but only for a customer who has confirmed the planner time. A customer without that time has its sorting suspended from the same day, because after the switch the feature no longer runs on the old model.
- **No model measured at all.** This happens only if neither the successor nor another vendor’s model could be tested in time. There is then nothing to switch to: the feature stays on the old model until it retires, and on 31 March sorting is suspended for every customer. Documents go back to the sorting by hand each planner did before the trial, and Priya agrees the changed service and its fee with each customer before that day, rather than assuming the customer can absorb the work.

Alex confirms at the October test that the review-aid mode works on the successor, and again at the decision on whichever tested model was chosen. Priya re-measures, at today’s document volumes rather than the trial’s three customers, how many planner hours a week confirming would take each customer. Before the switch day she confirms with each customer that a planner has that time and agrees the changed service and its fee, as the cost chapter requires; she owns that customer communication throughout.

The review-aid mode needs no new Larkspur staff, because the extra work falls on the customers’ planners. That does not make it free for Larkspur. A successor that passed runs under the signed commitment, at its discount. Otherwise nothing is signed: usage is billed at flexible list prices, and another vendor’s model has its own price. So at the decision Sam prices the chosen model’s expected usage and checks it against the 2028 cash provision for AI that the board has already approved. Inside the provision, Ines authorizes the switch under her delegation. Spending beyond it is the board’s. The board’s January meeting is on 14 January 2028, so when the decision comes sets the route:

Alex and Priya propose, Sam tests the cash, and Ines decides within the authority the board has delegated to her: spending inside the approved plan and below a stated limit is hers, as the chapter [[decide-who-decides]] records. The board is not asked for money now; it is told what the register says and what it will be asked for in the 2028 plan.

**Who does the work, and what waits.** The six engineer-weeks for item 2 are three weeks each from the two engineers named for the customer portal redesign, in November and the first half of December, taken from the redesign’s preparation. The same two build tranche 1 from January to March. The redesign’s build, originally January to March 2028, moves to April to June if the work stops after tranche 1, and the portal’s owner is told so. If tranche 2 is released in April, the same two engineers carry it from April to July for continuity and the redesign moves to August to October, seven months after its original start; those four further months are part of what is weighed at the gate. A later release (May if the board declines the conditional line and approves in April, later still if the gate slips) moves the redesign by the same amount.

**If a third engine incident comes before January.** It does not create capacity, because the two engineers are on the migration until mid-December; what it could change is the start. An early start spends money on work the 2027 plan does not fund, and the chapter [[decide-who-decides]] reserves any change to what an approved plan funds for the board. So Ines asks the board for a written approval between meetings, not her delegation, and asks only on strong incident evidence. The approval is narrow: the two named engineers, full time on tranche 1, from Monday 20 December 2027 until the board’s January meeting on Friday 14 January 2028, and up to €8,000 of cash from the 2027 budget. Nothing after that meeting is covered.

- *The board declines the early start.* Nothing changes; tranche 1 waits for the January plan.
- *The board approves the early start and the January meeting approves the 2028 line.* Tranche 1 continues; the 2028 line is reduced by what was already spent, and the go-live and gate dates stay as recorded.
- *The board approves the early start and the January meeting refuses the 2028 line.* The work stops at that meeting. With the office closed from 24 December to 2 January, each engineer will have worked four days in December and ten in January: 28 engineer-days, about 5.6 engineer-weeks, a cost-based estimate of about €11,000, plus the cash already spent, with the failover benefit not yet in hand. The engineers return to the portal redesign on Monday 17 January, two weeks after its planned start, so its build moves from January to March to mid-January to mid-April.

**What the gate measures.** Tranche 1 is meant to reduce cost and recovery risk while keeping delivery speed; the customer-size limit is tranche 2’s. Conditions 1 to 3 are the improvements the stage promised and the incident check; condition 4 is a safeguard that the split has not made changes harder, not a claimed gain.

**If the gate is missed.** Any unmet condition blocks tranche 2 until every condition is re-read and passes at one reading:

- *An incident attributable to the split.* Its cause is fixed, one further clean month is observed, then all four conditions are re-read.
- *Failover over five minutes.* The split is reviewed for design, not rushed; tranche 2 is not released until a later reading passes all four.
- *Hosting saving not reached.* The rightsizing is finished first; if the split cannot deliver the saving, the same design review.
- *Lead time worse.* The split has added complexity, and the design review comes before tranche 2.
- *Go-live slips.* The reading moves to one month after the actual go-live, and tranche 2 and the portal redesign move with it.

The counterargument is in the table, and it deserves to be said plainly: **some debt is worth carrying**, and saying so is honest. Item 1 costs about €3,000 a month of cost-based time and two invoicing incidents a quarter, and the work to remove it would take the two specialists the company can least spare. Larkspur’s investor expects to hold its share for about four more years, and item 1 may well be carried to that **exit**, the point at which the investor sells its share. What makes that a decision rather than neglect is that the cost is stated, the reason is written and a review date is set. The evidence that would change it is the same evidence that put it on the register: a rise in the workaround hours, a third incident in a quarter, or a pricing change the catalogue cannot absorb. A leader who says “we are choosing to carry this, at this cost, until this date” has answered the investor’s question; a leader who says “it is fine” has not.

## What to Say When the Investor Asks

**“How much technical debt do we have?”** Five items on a register with three columns each. Carried, they cost about €10,000 a month, of which €5,500 is cash and the rest is about 11 engineer-days of our own people’s time; the invoicing module causes two incidents a quarter, the scheduling engine caused our last two incidents this year and takes 45 minutes to fail over, and it stops us serving customers above 400 technicians, about €12,000 a month of revenue we are not winning. Two of the five have external dates: the reporting stack goes first because its date is close and its work small, and the retiring model’s successor is tested in October, before we sign the vendor commitment, so that we sign only on a pass and decide any replacement by the end of November, four months before its date. One we are choosing to carry, at a stated cost, to a stated date.

**“Why not just rewrite it?”** A rewrite of the engine is nine frozen months, one switch-over where every risk arrives together, and about €340,000, €300,000 of it our engineers’ time and €40,000 cash, committed before anything is in hand. The tranche plan puts failover and rightsizing in hand by March for about €58,000, funds the large-customer work only against that result, and keeps the core replacement as a decision we can still take later, priced when we take it. The one benefit-at-the-end project we are doing is the reporting stack, because it has a close date and no half-way state.

**“Why fund work customers will not see?”** Because the register shows what they do see: two invoicing incidents a quarter, a prospect we cannot pass a security review for, two prospects we cannot serve. The after column is the evidence: incidents, failover time, lead time and the hosting line, measured on the same basis before and after each tranche.

With the register kept and reviewed with the bill, the technology the company runs has a stated cost, a stated risk and a stated speed, and a plan for each. The next three chapters take one column each: [[prove-you-can-restore]] the risk a saving can hide, [[cheaper-cloud-bill]] the cost of a metered service per useful unit, and [[ai-worth-its-cost]] the return on the newest such bill. A register like this is also what a buyer’s investigation looks for in the chapter [[diligence-corrects-the-plan]], and the debt the company chose to carry is among the obligations the chapter [[handover-of-obligations]] passes to the next decision-maker.

## Questions to Consider

1. *List the five largest items of technical debt you carry. For each, can you state its cost per month, the risk it carries and what it slows, from figures you already have?*
2. *Which of your items has an external date, a support end, a retirement, a customer’s review, and is it sequenced by that date or by its size?*
3. *For the project most often proposed as a rewrite, what would the first tranche be, what would its gate measure, and what would still be a decision after it?*
4. *Which debt are you choosing to carry, at what stated cost, until what date, and who has been told?*

- **[The WyCash Portfolio Management System](https://c2.com/doc/oopsla92.html)** — Ward Cunningham, experience report at the OOPSLA software conference, 1992.<br>*The two-paragraph origin of the debt metaphor, where Cunningham already says a little debt speeds development so long as it is paid back promptly, which is the dated shortcut of item 4.*

- **[Managing Technical Debt: Reducing Friction in Software Development](https://www.sei.cmu.edu/library/managing-technical-debt-reducing-friction-in-software-development/)** — Philippe Kruchten, Robert Nord and Ipek Ozkaya, Addison-Wesley, 2019.<br>*The book-length treatment of the same authors' method for identifying debt items, costing them and paying them off over time, useful when the full register grows well beyond a board's shortlist.*
- **[Technical Debt Quadrant](https://martinfowler.com/bliki/TechnicalDebtQuadrant.html)** — Martin Fowler, 2009.<br>*Sorts debt into deliberate or inadvertent and prudent or reckless, a finer cut than this chapter's four origins that helps when the board asks how the company got here.*
- **[Strangler Fig](https://martinfowler.com/bliki/StranglerFigApplication.html)** — Martin Fowler, 2004, revised 2024.<br>*The pattern behind the tranche plan: replacing a system piece by piece around the live one, so each step returns value and no single switch-over carries every risk at once.*
- **[Online Migrations at Scale](https://stripe.com/blog/online-migrations)** — Jacqueline Xu, Stripe engineering blog, 2017.<br>*One company's own account of moving hundreds of millions of records in four gated phases (writing to both the old and new stores; checking that the new store returns the same results and switching reads to it; sending new writes only to the new store; then removing the obsolete data and code) with no time during which the service was unavailable.*
