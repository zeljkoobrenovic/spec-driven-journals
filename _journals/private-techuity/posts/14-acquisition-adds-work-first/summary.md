An **acquisition** purchases a business; **integration** makes chosen activities work together. A **carve-out** separates a business from a larger owner. Both can legally complete before the operating dependencies have been resolved. The transaction plan can assume savings or independence before the operating teams have validated the work, so bring those dependencies into the discussion while scope, price and timing can still change. Who proposes and approves varies; in the fictional Larkspur scenarios a fund’s deal team proposes, its investment committee approves the terms, the company board approves the plan and budget, and the seller and a regulator affect timing.

Combining and separating are **different processes under one principle**: a transaction draws a new boundary, and customers on both sides expect the product, contracts and support to continue. Define which capabilities must work across the boundary, who is accountable for each, and what transition makes that possible. Each process has a completion test. Combining is complete when a customer can buy and use the combined offering, one team supports it and the duplicated cost has actually been retired. Separating is complete when the business has run for a defined period with no service from the parent.

**Combining businesses.** Write the thesis as what customers or the company will do differently, and separate **organic growth** from purchased revenue and valuation-multiple effects. Choose the depth of integration for the intended benefit: separate products, shared services, connected workflows or consolidation are design options, not maturity levels, and a group standard needs a stated problem, a cost of compliance, an exceptions process and a named funder. Then decide what waits. In the fictional add-on purchase, the two engineers who understand Larkspur’s scheduling engine are needed for sixteen weeks of integration, ten weeks of a promised customer portal and three weeks of diligence on the next target, against twenty-six available. The board puts the priced integration first, moves the portal one quarter with customers told, and delays the next diligence six weeks; the synergy date stays at month nine, and the choice is revisited if the identity work overruns.

**Separating a business.** A **transition services agreement** provides temporary parent services; it buys time and does not perform the separation. A €400,000 allocated technology cost becomes €700,000 of annual standalone spending plus €300,000 of separation work, and the model must carry both. In the separate carve-out scenario, the TSA ends on 30 June 2027 with one costly extension; identity, finance and network milestones land between October 2026 and April 2027 under Alex and Sam; and a May 2027 month with every parent service disconnected, including a month-end close, a customer onboarding, a release and a restore, is the independence test before the TSA is allowed to expire.

Confirm rights to code, data, licenses and contracts before moving data or consolidating platforms; respect pre-closing limits on planning. Track the specific mechanism in the scorecard and count a shared saving once. Ownership changes on the transaction date; the operating transition is complete only when the completion tests pass.
