**Toys R Us**, the toy and baby-products retailer bought by a group of investment firms in 2005, shows how borrowing, competition and the time a transition needs can interact. The documents describe technology gaps and proposed investment beside heavy debt and bills falling due; **liquidity** means the ability to meet payments when due. They show why a transition needed funding, not that the proposed work would have restored competitiveness or that one cause explains the failure. The evidence comes from the last two years of that ownership.

The fiscal 2016 release (the accounting year ended January 28, 2017) shows why “profitable” misleads. First, **operating earnings**, the accounting profit from running the business before interest and tax, were $460 million. Second, once $457 million of interest on borrowing, $2 million of interest earned and $34 million of tax were counted ($460 million − $457 million + $2 million − $34 million), the year ended in a **net loss** of $29 million for the whole group, or $36 million after removing the $7 million of earnings belonging to outside owners of some subsidiaries, businesses the company controlled but did not fully own. [S35: Toys R Us fiscal 2016 results](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1005414/000100541417000010/truq4-16earningsreleaseexh.htm)

Third, **operating cash flow**, cash actually received minus cash actually paid to run the business, interest included, was roughly zero. Earnings and cash differ because of timing: a sale is recorded when it is made, but the cash can move earlier or later, and stock may be paid for before or after it sells, depending on supplier terms. It also spent $252 million of **capital expenditure**, spending on durable assets such as equipment and software. Interest already inside operating cash flow must not be subtracted again.

Technology work was underway: online sales grew 11% while total sales fell 2.2%. The chief executive's September 2017 court declaration describes a missing subscription-delivery capability and a proposed $90.4 million technology program for 2018–2021, beside roughly $400 million a year of **debt service**, the cash needed for interest and scheduled repayments of borrowing. These are management's explanations to the court, not court findings. [S50: Brandon declaration](https://profhayesuwla.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/09/toys-r-us-first-day-dec-chairman.pdf)

It also reports a chain: concern about survival led nearly 40% of suppliers to demand earlier payment; the same stock then needed cash before it was sold; the cash need rose, potentially by more than $1 billion on management's estimate; and less room remained to fund the transition. A product improvement that pays off over several years cannot pay for next week's stock.

In September 2017 the company entered **Chapter 11**, the US court-supervised process that lets a business keep trading while it reorganizes its debts. The court authorized, but did not require, a combined package of new borrowing of up to $3,125 million for the bankruptcy period, spread across several loan arrangements for different group companies, including Canada. Part of it repaid existing loans; the rest was a ceiling subject to lenders' conditions, not fresh free cash. The court also allowed payment of some earlier bills such as wages, and the stores kept trading. [S36: Toys R Us bankruptcy note](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1005414/000100541417000047/R9.htm) [S118: Toys R Us bankruptcy financing note](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1005414/000100541417000047/R10.htm)

Six months later, after no buyer or agreed restructuring emerged, Reuters reported plans for US store **liquidation**, selling the assets and winding down those operations, with roughly 33,000 jobs exposed. [S37: Reuters US liquidation report](https://www.business-standard.com/article/reuters/toys-r-us-to-close-doors-leaving-void-for-toy-lovers-118031500236_1.html) International operations and later brand use are separate stories.

Hasbro's 2018 report separately records $60.4 million of costs associated with the bankruptcy, one supplier's documented consequence. [S51: Hasbro 2018 annual report](https://www.annualreports.com/HostedData/AnnualReportArchive/h/NYSE_HAS_2018.pdf)

**Transfer the funding question carefully.** A young company awaiting its next investment or a product funded by a parent company has different facts. Each leader still needs to know whether obligations and the transition can be funded until benefits arrive: operating cash flow, whether interest and changes in working capital (stock plus money customers owe, less unpaid supplier bills) are already inside it, then capital expenditure and repayment dates, tested against a change in supplier terms. Distinguish a necessary improvement from a sufficient rescue, and keep the consequences for employees and suppliers visible in the decision record.
