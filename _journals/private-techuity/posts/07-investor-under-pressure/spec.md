# Spec: Judge an Investor by Their Behavior Under Pressure

## Intent

Assess the investor relationship a leader can influence, including one already inherited. Resolve the two-term-sheet opening with a compact comparison (evidence obtained, remaining uncertainty, effect on Larkspur's next decisions, negotiated commitment) that ends in a recorded choice, show one changed dependency for an investor already in place, and give a missing-evidence outcome. Engagement design belongs to Part IV and is only linked.

## Audience

Product and engineering leaders inside companies working under investors, including leaders who inherit an ownership arrangement. Assume no specialist finance background; explain necessary terms before use. Investor-side readers are secondary.

- Keep the opening WHY INVESTORS CARE callout to the investor’s one trade-off: writing conditions down gives up the freedom to refuse later, while payment still needs the conditions met and a valid request. The institutional definitions belong to the introduction that follows it, and the Investor A/B details to the comparison.
- Introduce the basic arrangement in plain words before the first claim that depends on it: an investment firm manages a fund (a pool of its own investors’ money), a committee approves investments, a follow-on is more money after the first investment. Explain onboarding, the pilot and every finance term at first meaningful use in each format, including labels drawn inside images.
- State the written follow-on as one negotiated arrangement in this fictional example, not as what investors generally accept. What it avoids is a fresh funding negotiation in which the committee is free to refuse; Larkspur must still submit a valid request. Keep three states apart everywhere: agreed in a term sheet, owed under a signed agreement after a valid request, and cash received. Scope the term-sheet statement to the investment obligation. Signing, closing and receipt of the first €4.5 million are separate events: the agreement-status label changes at signing, and the first money is tracked as cash only once received.
- State the retest rule for the follow-on condition. The pilot plan, agreed with B and attached to the investment agreement, allows one second group: at least eight new customers, taken in the order they signed so that nobody picks them, measured the same way. The second group’s own average decides and the groups are not combined; waiting time, board confirmation and the other conditions apply unchanged; a third group needs B’s agreement and is not planned on. Give the second result a planning date (about month eight) that falls before the month-eleven decision and the fifteen-month expiry.
- Present the follow-on terms so a reader can find each one separately (payer, amount, condition, other conditions, expiry, payment period, signing state), and record the decision as choice, rejected alternatives, accepted risks and reopening evidence.
- Give the follow-on a fictional cash-timing assumption: what the first €4.5 million pays for, when the pilot result and the request are expected, the payment period after a valid request, and what happens to the second country if the condition or the payment fails. The fifteen-month expiry limits when Larkspur may ask; it is never a promised cash date.
- Do not derive a fund’s remaining money or patience from its age. State Investor B’s remaining capacity and intended holding period as explicit fictional answers B gave.
- Keep the funding condition (a group average of 65 hours or less per implementation, inclusive, across the agreed group of at least eight customers; an average, not a test of every customer) apart from the board’s pilot rule in the linked chapters (below 60 hours supports requesting the next stage and bringing the expansion decision forward; exactly 60 is the middle band; both are group averages). The funding condition tests an agreed level of observed improvement. It is not proof that the automation caused the reduction, and it does not by itself justify expansion.
- Figures distinguish a review, an approval and a payment; labels inside images are reader questions or plain terms, not descriptions of drawn props. Where a figure has a time axis, work that depends on a payment sits after that payment: pilot, review, approval, payment received, then wider use.
- The TL;DR says on its own who decides for the investor (its investment committee) and who decides for Larkspur (its board), and explains references and a quarter. It counts the fifteen-month request window from closing, explains closing, and keeps the thirty-day payment period separate.
