Assess an investor through its **behavior under pressure** and its ability to support this company’s next decisions. Promises of patience, expertise and freedom to decide need evidence before they become planning assumptions. Most product and engineering leaders never sign the term sheet, but their plan’s feasibility depends on how the investor behaves after a missed quarter.

Begin with the company’s next difficult decisions. For fictional Larkspur: an onboarding pilot, a second country and a retiring founder, each needing something specific: an experienced contributor, follow-on capital on known conditions, and time. Public operating-team descriptions do not prove suitability. [S22: KKR Capstone description](https://www.kkr.com/approach/capstone)

Seek references that include difficulty: a failed plan, a disagreement with an operating expert, a request for further investment, support that was discontinued. A reference has its own perspective; look for repeated mechanisms and check alternative explanations. **Make the support offer reviewable**: who is available, for how many days, who pays, who has authority to decide, what would stop it. Examine the financing alongside the support; a fund’s ten-year term is not a sale date.

**The evidence changes the choice**, in a fictional scenario of its own. Investor A offers €5 million at the higher valuation and a broad platform; an independent reference reports a four-month, unwritten committee process for a follow-on after a miss. Investor B offers €4.5 million, one operator with two onboarding automations behind him, and funded a reference’s bridge within six weeks while replacing its CFO; its fund is in year seven of ten, with less money left. Either could be right. Ines chooses B because its next funding decision can be written down: not a reserve inside B’s fund, which B’s committee controls, but an investment-agreement clause obliging the same fund to subscribe €1.5 million once the pilot shows 65 hours or less per implementation with waiting time no worse, lapsing fifteen months after closing; plus ten named operator days a quarter. Until the agreement is signed, the term sheet binds nobody. No term addresses B’s management-change practice or exit pressure; the board accepts both risks, subject to further references, and a second former executive with the same CFO story would send Ines back to negotiate the approval route for management changes before signing.

**For an investor already in place**, change one dependency. In a separate scenario, Alex moves a June hire that assumed an uncommitted co-investor to a dated July board decision, funds the onboarding pilot from operating cash, and labels the investor’s AI help unconfirmed until a named person and days exist. When references or fund information are unavailable, label the support unconfirmed, make no dependent commitment, and set a review date.

Avoided downside scenarios, shifting definitions or instructions that bypass executives warrant investigation, not an allegation; uncomfortable challenge can be useful discipline. Reassess fit periodically; when conflict emerges, take a concrete decision, its evidence and options to the forum authorized to decide ([[decide-who-decides]]).
