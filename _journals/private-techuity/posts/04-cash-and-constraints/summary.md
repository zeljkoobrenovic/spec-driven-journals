The board sees growing operating earnings. Engineering is told there is no money for an essential migration. Both are true at once, and the gap between them is where most technology budget arguments are lost.

**Follow Larkspur's cash bridge.** EBITDA is €10 million. Cash interest takes €4 million, cash tax €1 million, capital expenditure €2 million, and growth ties up another €1 million in working capital — leaving €2 million. Required debt repayment takes €1.5 million of that. **What remains is €0.5 million**, against an engineering leader asking for €1 million. Neither party is being unreasonable; the money genuinely is not there in the year it is wanted.

The working-capital line is the one engineers most often miss. Larkspur grew, so more customers owed it money at year end than at the start — real cash, earned and unavailable, and **growth made it larger rather than smaller**, because you pay to serve customers before they pay you.

**Debt changes the consequences of being wrong.** A maturity date can force refinancing even when every interest payment has been made. Covenants — conditions attached to borrowing, such as a cap on net debt at a multiple of EBITDA — hand the lender new rights the moment they are breached: fees, restrictions, blocked spending, or a demand for repayment. That is why one bad quarter can freeze a migration budget approved in the last one. And rates move: €60 million of floating-rate debt costs €3.6 million a year at 6% and €5.4 million at 9%, a €1.8 million swing that can exceed an entire technology initiative.

**Accounting changes the picture, not the work.** The same €1 million of Larkspur development spending shows EBITDA of €9 million if expensed and €10 million if capitalized — while exactly €1 million leaves the bank either way. Capitalization is not suspicious, but an EBITDA improvement deserves the question: did the work get better, did the policy change, or both? Adjusted earnings need the same discipline, especially when the excluded costs recur as part of the strategy.

Efficiency and resilience pull against each other. Turning off unused environments reduces waste; removing the people who understand a fragile billing platform creates a deferred cost that can exceed the payroll saved. Distinguish reversible savings from capability losses, and committed owner support from hopeful support.

So bring choices rather than a single number: a minimum continuity option, a staged improvement, and a fuller investment — each with cash timing, dependencies, expected outcome, and what would trigger a stop. The question is which plan gives the company the strongest feasible ability to serve customers and meet its obligations. Learn the bridge and you can argue about the right thing.
