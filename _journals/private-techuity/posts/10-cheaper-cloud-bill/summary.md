**Cloud services** are rented computing, storage and related services. A lower bill can reflect efficiency, reduced demand, poorer service or postponed obligations; the bill alone cannot say which. In the fictional Larkspur scenario, the board-approved plan assumes flat cloud spending while volume grows by half, and the investor’s technology adviser has asked why last quarter’s bill rose 20%. Two questions sit inside that one: did the service unit improve, and was the plan met?

Choose a **meaningful unit of delivered value** and read it, on one basis, against both the prior period and the plan. Last year Larkspur spent €100,000 a month for one million successful transactions: €0.10 each. The plan held spending flat at 1.5 million transactions: about €0.067. Actual: €120,000 for 1.5 million, €0.08. **Unit cost improved 20%** against last year, yet the flat-spend plan was **missed by €20,000 a month** and unit cost is 20% above plan. By contrast, €80,000 for 500,000 transactions would raise unit cost to €0.16.

**Put every figure on the same basis.** A figure can be cash paid, cost assigned to the period (upfront payments spread over the months covered), a credit-reduced invoice or commitment usage; agree which first. The chapter uses the second.

**Separate usage, price, and architecture.** Removing unused resources changes consumption; negotiating rates changes price; redesign changes resource needs and adds transition costs; they are not simply additive. **FinOps**, cross-team management of technology value, applies unit economics this way. [S16: FinOps unit economics](https://www.finops.org/framework/capabilities/unit-economics/)

A purchasing commitment can lower the nominal rate while increasing **exposure to unused capacity**. On the example’s assumed terms, a €35,000 commitment replacing a €50,000 flexible bill saves €15,000 a month at unchanged demand and costs €15,000 more at €20,000 of demand. Larkspur states a demand range (€30,000 to €60,000 next year) and a funding condition (no new financing for twelve months), then chooses a one-year commitment covering €30,000 of demand: €9,000 a month saved across the range, €1,000 lost in the stress case, nothing beyond the funded horizon. The CTO proposes, the CFO confirms cash timing and terms, the CEO approves within her authority; a usage drop or a transaction reopens it. Stable demand with multi-year funding might justify the larger commitment.

Credits, minimum spend, renewal and ownership-change terms change the invoice, not the resources consumed. In a separate fictional example, credits cut a €30,000 monthly bill to €5,000 for six months; the plan must still fund the full cost afterwards. Check which entity signs, any minimum spend and whether a commitment transfers on a carve-out. Keep **service quality** as the final test: successful work, latency, errors and recovery.

Track the **opportunity** modelled, the **change implemented** and the **observed net effect** separately on the agreed basis; a run-rate projection is a labelled estimate, not an observation. Larkspur’s answer has two parts: unit cost improved, and the plan was missed. The commitment closes €9,000 of the €20,000 gap; the remaining €11,000 needs further changes or a revised envelope: a contractual choice beside an honest plan comparison, not a savings announcement.
