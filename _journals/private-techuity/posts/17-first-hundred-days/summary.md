The first 100 days should establish the conditions for useful execution: shared understanding, clear ownership, clear starting measurements for comparison, feasible priorities, and funded decisions. The number is a planning convention, not evidence that every business changes at the same speed.

Start by checking what has changed since **due diligence**, the investigation before the investment and what company leaders already know. Some commitments are urgent; some findings were based on incomplete access; some initiatives already work. Separate immediate continuity and material risk obligations from the improvement portfolio, while keeping both visible to the people responsible.

**Choose a small set of connected priorities that fits actual capacity.** The chapter uses three priorities for fictional Larkspur: improve new-customer setup, share knowledge held by too few people, and test whether service can be restored after a failure. The example is a method demonstration. It is not a claim that three priorities, or any fixed count, is empirically optimal.

Each initiative needs a company owner, a business mechanism, a starting measurement, resources, work that must happen first, and a review condition. State what must stop or wait to make room. An outcome target without an implementation path is an ambition; an activity list without an outcome is a work program of uncertain value.

Sequence dependencies deliberately. A growth target may require simpler implementation before more sales demand. A data initiative may require clear ownership and lawful access before a new tool. A recovery exercise may reveal a need for supplier changes. The portfolio should express these connections rather than assuming every initiative can proceed independently.

Agree how investor support will help the company’s priorities. A short engagement agreement should name the adviser’s contribution, company time, information use and review. Its value depends on whether it improves the work after the effort required from both sides is included.

At the end of the period, review decisions and evidence rather than celebrating completion of a presentation. Which assumptions survived? Which outcomes moved? What remains uncertain? Which initiatives should stop, expand, or change? Company leaders should make this learning usable in decisions with the investor, drawing on advisers where they help. The result should be a company-owned plan that can adapt through the rest of ownership without losing sight of its economic purpose or operational limits.
