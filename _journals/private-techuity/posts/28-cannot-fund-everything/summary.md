A **company investment** commits resources now for an expected future benefit. Choosing investments requires a view of both cash and **team capacity**, the time and capability available to do the work. Several attractive projects can fit one limit and exceed the other. Investor ownership changes the conditions of that choice: establish the actual funding, authority and deadline before committing, and name the result the funding was agreed on. The method is proposed; the figures are fictional.

In a fictional Larkspur exercise, the board has authorized up to €500,000 of additional cash and 24 engineer-weeks for a set of improvements, and the CEO decides how to use them. Routine operating work is already budgeted; this change budget must also fund the improvement needed to meet the agreed recovery requirement. One engineer-week represents one person’s working time for a week. Four proposals compete: service restoration (€80,000, four weeks), a simpler customer-setup step (€180,000, twelve weeks), a full customer portal (€220,000, twelve weeks) and customer research into the portal need (€20,000, two weeks). Together they total €500,000 and 30 engineer-weeks. **The cash fits; the engineering time does not.**

Sort the rows by what they rest on before comparing them. Restoration meets an agreed obligation, though the company still chooses how to meet it. The setup change rests on customer evidence and on the growth plan’s assumption that onboarding can grow without a proportional increase in the implementation team, the one investor condition that matters here. The portal rests on an assumption; the research row exists to test it. **Opportunity cost** is the benefit of the best alternative a choice gives up; a shared specialist can make that trade-off binding even when both projects have funding.

One defensible selection funds restoration, the setup change and the research: €280,000 and 18 engineer-weeks, leaving €220,000 and six weeks uncommitted. The full portal is rejected for now; the spare money does not make its twelve weeks fit into six, and its benefit is untested. The record names who is accountable for each piece and what evidence would change the choice. The spare resources are room for uncertainty, not savings and not permission to start another project.

Use funding stages when an early result can inform a later commitment.

Then review the combination, not each project. In the exercise, the day-45 restoration test failed: the service took eleven hours against an agreed four because of a missing credential and an unavailable database version. The €80,000 spent is **sunk cost**; the obligation remains. Correcting the faults and retesting takes about €20,000 and two weeks from the reserve. The revised commitment is €300,000 and 20 engineer-weeks, with €200,000 and four weeks left, the retest by day 85 as the next review point, and the portal build still deferred. The CEO takes the revision to the board because it changes what the envelope funds.

The toolkit’s record for comparing investment choices and capacity holds these fields; update it after the review rather than starting a new one.
