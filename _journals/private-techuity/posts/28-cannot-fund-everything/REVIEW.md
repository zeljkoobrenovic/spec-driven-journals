# Editorial review: You Cannot Fund Every Good Project at Once

Reviewed 14 September 2026. Read the full article, summary and reader-facing comic text.

## Overall assessment

This is an effective opening to the operating chapters. It argues that leaders must choose a feasible combination of work against both cash and capacity, including obligations and the option to learn before committing further. The fictional €500,000/24-engineer-week exercise actually reaches a decision, making it more useful than chapters that end with questions alone.

The main improvement is to reach that example sooner. Several preliminary frameworks and the four-ownership-scenario table delay the most distinctive teaching. A small ambiguity also remains about which existing obligations are already funded and which the decision still needs to fund.

## Detailed feedback

- **Introduction and voice:** Larkspur's competing recovery, onboarding and portal requests create immediate stakes. The warning against turning an unfunded target into pressure to work harder is excellent. “A leader who chooses by team preference … will find the plan overruled or unfunded” is unnecessarily absolute and shifts the emphasis from good judgment to avoiding investor rejection.
- **Organization and flow:** Results, minimum obligations, ownership scenarios, option questions and only then the numerical case creates a long runway. “The Same Project Can Need a Different Commitment” repeats a framework already established in Part I. The chapter's own contribution is the combination decision, so make that its spine.
- **Clarity and terminology:** Cash and engineer-weeks are clearly separated; the caveat that people are not interchangeable is particularly useful. Opportunity cost and sunk cost are explained economically. The sentence “after covering existing commitments” needs clarification because restoration subsequently appears as an unfunded project needed to meet an agreed recovery requirement. This is a confirmed ambiguity in the planning boundary, not proof the figures double-count anything.
- **Examples and accuracy:** The four projects total €500,000 and 30 engineer-weeks. The chosen three total €280,000 and 18 weeks, leaving €220,000 and six weeks. The arithmetic works. The explanation that research precedes a possible portal build prevents a simplistic parallel-scheduling reading. Preserve it.
- **Pacing and conclusion:** The sections on safe intermediate states and reviewing the whole combination add real value. The conclusion hands off naturally to the benefit chain. The final questions mostly repeat the comparison table and could be shortened.
- **Evidence:** The method is explicitly proposed and the numbers fictional. It does not need to pretend a prioritization formula has empirical authority. The claims in further-reading annotations about real options and decision reversibility should not imply that every staged project has a cheaply exercisable option; the body already handles this nuance well.
- **Other formats:** The summary and comic accurately retain the two constraints and the actual selection. “The money fits. The time does not” is a strong example of accessible compression worth retaining across the collection.

## Recommended changes

- **High impact — Bring the resource table forward.** Let the opening requests lead directly to the two limits, then explain obligations, evidence and dependencies as the team chooses among the rows. This gives the preliminary concepts a purpose readers can see.
- **Medium impact — Clarify the funding boundary.** Suggested wording: “Routine operating work is already budgeted. The following change budget must also fund the improvement needed to meet the agreed recovery requirement.” Alternatively, remove restoration from the discretionary pool and show the budget remaining after it. Choose one model and keep it across formats.
- **Medium impact — Show one review that changes the selection.** For example, customer research invalidates the portal need or a failed restoration test consumes the spare capacity. State the revised commitment. That would demonstrate the final method instead of merely describing it.
- **Medium impact — Compress the investor-scenario table.** Keep the one condition relevant to this exercise and link back to financing comparisons. This chapter can demonstrate the shared decision method without repeating every investor category.
- **Low impact — End with a usable record.** Link directly to the toolkit's initiative/commitment record and retain three reflection questions. Replace the “overruled” prediction with an explanation of the missing constraint.

## Proposed structure

Three requests → cash and capacity table → distinguish obligations and assumptions → select the feasible combination → test whether stages can stand alone → revise the selection after new evidence. Keep this chapter separate from the roadmap chapter: choosing the portfolio and substantiating one project's benefit are different reader tasks.
