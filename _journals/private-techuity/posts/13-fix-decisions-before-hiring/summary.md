An **operating model** describes how teams, responsibilities and decisions are arranged. A chart and a headcount target describe it incompletely. A fictional company buys a tool, changes its architecture and hires in a cheaper location; six months later it still waits on the same executive and two experts. The plan funds new resources, but it has not funded the management time and decision changes needed to use them.

![Customer work exposes queues, knowledge dependencies and decision handoffs, supported by skills, authority, capacity and a funded transition.](assets/images/13-fix-decisions-before-hiring/summary-at-a-glance.jpeg)
**Figure 1:** *Design teams around the work, knowledge and decisions customers depend on.*

**Follow one piece of work.** At fictional Larkspur, a pricing change takes sixteen working days from request to production: three waiting for a commercial decision, seven in a two-person review queue, two building, three waiting for the CEO’s invoice sign-off, one to release. The trace separates a decision constraint (authority sits with one person), a knowledge constraint (only two people can change the module) and the staffing constraint left afterwards.

**Change the decisions first.** Delegating a commercial catalogue and the invoice sign-off should remove about six waiting days without hiring, a forecast the next traces will test. The specialists’ queue stays; it is a knowledge problem. The remaining gap is a third person who can change the invoicing module and protected time for the two who can, not the five-person second team the board asked for.

**Let the diagnosis decide the hire.** Larkspur hires one billing engineer now, about €90,000 a year from the operating budget, protects the specialists’ knowledge-transfer time and defers the second team until second-country demand is evidenced **and** the next round is committed and approved. Rejected: the full team, which lengthens the queue without touching it, and no hiring. A lead time still above two weeks after sixty days calls for a new trace, not a hire; faster second-country sales satisfy the demand condition only; an engineer who cannot release independently by month six means a failed transfer.

**Assess leaders in their system.** A CTO’s choices matter; so do the authority, information and commercial commitments around the role. A leadership change needs a **hypothesis**: what must new leadership enable, and what evidence shows that capability is missing? It cuts both ways: if the conditions are changed and the leader still holds every release personally, support that leaves that authority in place is not enough; added leadership with release authority, or replacement, is the evidenced response.

**Offshoring** locates work abroad; **nearshoring** does so closer in geography or time zone; cultural similarity must be checked, not assumed. The fictional comparison starts from €1 million of external development spending: a €650,000 team plus €150,000 of coordination costs €800,000 a year, saving €200,000, and the €250,000 transition makes the first year €50,000 dearer than before. Whether the work transfers coherently matters more than the wage rate.

Knowledge transfer ends when the receiving team performs the work under realistic conditions, not when a document is delivered. Organizational health is evidence of the plan’s feasibility. The assessment’s system constraint, country rules coupled into the invoicing module, remains; the next chapter compares a supplier’s configuration, an owned boundary and a core replacement, and chooses one.
