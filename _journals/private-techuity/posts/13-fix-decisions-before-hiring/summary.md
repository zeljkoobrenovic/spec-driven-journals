An **operating model** describes how teams, responsibilities and decisions are arranged. An organization chart and a headcount target are incomplete descriptions of it. A fictional company buys a tool, changes its architecture and hires in a cheaper location; six months later it still waits on the same executive and the same two experts. The plan funds new resources, but it has not funded the management time and decision changes needed to use them.

![Customer work exposes queues, knowledge dependencies and decision handoffs, supported by skills, authority, capacity and a funded transition.](assets/images/13-fix-decisions-before-hiring/summary-at-a-glance.jpeg)
**Figure 1:** *Design teams around the work, knowledge and decisions customers depend on.*

**Follow an actual piece of work.** At fictional Larkspur, a pricing change takes sixteen working days from request to production: three waiting for a commercial decision, seven in a two-person review queue, two building, three waiting for the CEO’s invoice sign-off, one to release. The trace separates a decision constraint (authority sits with one person), a knowledge constraint (only two people can change the module) and, once those are addressed, the staffing constraint actually left.

**Change the decisions first.** Delegating a commercial catalogue and the invoice sign-off removes about six waiting days without hiring. The specialists’ queue stays; it is a knowledge problem. The remaining gap is a third person able to change the invoicing module and protected time for the two who can, not the second product team of five the board asked for.

**Let the diagnosis decide the hire.** Larkspur hires one billing engineer now, about €90,000 a year from the operating budget, protects the specialists’ knowledge-transfer time and defers the second team until second-country demand is evidenced and the next round is committed. Rejected: the full team, which does not touch the queue and lengthens it, and no hiring. Evidence that would change the decision: a lead time still above two weeks after sixty days, faster-than-expected second-country sales, or a billing engineer who cannot release independently by month six.

**Assess leaders in their system.** A CTO’s choices matter, but so do the authority, information and commercial commitments around the role. A leadership change needs a **hypothesis**: what must new leadership enable, and what evidence shows that capability is missing? The hypothesis preserves accountability. If the conditions are changed and the leader still approves every release personally, replacement or added leadership is the justified response.

**Offshoring** locates work abroad; **nearshoring** does so in a country closer in geography or time zone, and cultural similarity has to be checked rather than assumed. The fictional comparison starts from €1 million of external development spending: a €650,000 team plus €150,000 of coordination costs €800,000 a year, saving €200,000, and the €250,000 transition makes the first year €50,000 more expensive than before. Whether the work transfers coherently matters more than the wage rate.

Knowledge transfer ends when the receiving team performs the work under realistic conditions, not when a document is delivered. Organizational health is diagnostic evidence about whether the plan is feasible. The system constraint from the assessment, country rules coupled into the invoicing module, remains; the next chapter compares a supplier’s configuration, a company-owned boundary and a core replacement, and chooses one.
