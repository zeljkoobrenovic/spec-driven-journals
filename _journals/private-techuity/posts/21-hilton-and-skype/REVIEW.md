# Editorial review: Hilton and Skype: A Successful Exit Still Needs Explaining

Reviewed 14 September 2026. Read the complete article, summary and reader-facing comic text.

## Overall assessment

This paired case argues that a successful investor exit is an outcome to explain, not proof that a particular technology intervention created the return. Its audience is product and engineering leaders asked to justify work through future sale value. Hilton supplies a long financing and operating story; Skype supplies a shorter story involving essential technology rights and a strategic buyer. The contrast earns the two-company structure.

The best material separates company value, sponsor-reported returns, investor cash flows and subsequent product history. The main weakness is cumulative qualification: evidence limits recur so often that the comparison's affirmative lessons become less prominent than the warnings. The reader also needs a clearer distinction between a technology capability that existed during ownership and an improvement attributable to that ownership period.

## Detailed feedback

- **Introduction and organization:** The central question is clear, and the parallel histories are intelligible. Preview the contrast in one sentence before the first chronology: Hilton requires a long financing explanation; Skype requires an explanation of rights and buyer-specific value. This gives readers a reason to retain the many dates and figures.
- **Narrative flow and pacing:** The Hilton subsections build from chronology to returns to competing explanations. Skype adds product changes, exit and later retirement. That longer sequence is justified, but “Read an Ownership Transition From the Company’s Side” and “What the Comparison Teaches” partly repeat the same transfer cautions. Combine their practical conclusions.
- **Examples and useful depth:** The 2013 IPO versus 2018 final realization distinction, the owned-and-leased properties' uneven recovery, Skype's intellectual-property settlement, and the warning against dividing $8.5bn by $2.75bn are excellent. Keep these specifics. They make source criticism concrete rather than merely procedural.
- **Terminology and evidence:** Sponsor-reported results are correctly distinguished from reconstructed net LP returns. The treatment of Microsoft's later retirement of Skype is appropriately restrained. Do not turn that later event into a verdict on the earlier investment or infer that an announced strategic rationale was achieved.
- **Concern needing verification:** In “Was It the Technology, the Timing, or the Terms?”, establish which commercial systems predated Blackstone, what changed during its ownership, and what evidence dates that change. The article does not explicitly claim Blackstone invented OnQ; the placement can nevertheless invite readers to attribute inherited capabilities to the new owner. A dated baseline would prevent that inference.
- **Source annotation needing precision:** The eBay 2007 further-reading note calls €630m “the write-down.” The release distinguishes an additional €630m impairment from the earn-out settlement also included in the total charge. Identify the component and avoid implying it is the complete charge. The currency itself is supported; this is not a confirmed euro/dollar error. See the [eBay SEC release](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1065088/000089161807000579/f34172exv99w1.htm).
- **Tone and ending:** The skepticism is useful and fair. Repeating that the cases do not establish other ownership outcomes in the transition section, comparison and ending is less useful. End with two case-specific operating questions and one shared evidence boundary. Place the final explanatory figure before the prose handoff so the chapter has one clear finish.
- **Other formats:** The summary and comic preserve the central distinctions well. The comic's document-reading frame avoids fabricated historical dialogue. Its last panel could name the two different decisions more explicitly rather than returning to the general question of transferability.

## Recommended changes

- **High impact — Make the technology chronology explicit.** Add a compact “inherited capability / documented change / remaining uncertainty” row for each company. If the sources cannot establish a change, say so. This protects the chapter from implying the attribution it warns readers against.
- **Medium impact — Move a short comparison near the beginning.** Contrast holding period, financing issue, technology issue and type of exit; leave detailed values in the case sections. Readers can then compare mechanisms instead of remembering isolated facts.
- **Medium impact — Consolidate repeated limitations.** Keep qualifications next to sensitive figures, but put the common absence of complete investor cash flows and stakeholder evidence in one closing paragraph.
- **Medium impact — Finish with a usable decision.** Suggested rewrite: “Hilton asks whether the company has financing and time for the operating plan. Skype asks whether it controls the rights the product depends on and what a particular buyer values. Neither answer can be read from the exit price alone.”
- **Low impact — Clarify the eBay charge annotation and move the final figure.** These are localized repairs, not reasons to replace the paired case.

## Proposed structure

Keep both companies together: comparative question → short comparison → Hilton chronology and competing explanations → Skype chronology, rights and strategic exit → two operating questions → common evidence limits. Retain Skype's later history as a short bounded subsection. Splitting the article would lose its most useful contrast and duplicate the return-measure cautions.
