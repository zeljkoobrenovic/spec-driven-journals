An **investor** has put money into a company expecting a financial gain, and an **investment firm** manages such investments for others. An **investor’s technology adviser** is employed by that firm to judge a company’s technology and often help improve it — here Morgan, the **Technology Principal** in the fictional Larkspur scenarios. Alex, Larkspur’s **chief technology officer (CTO)**, leads its technology. What distinguishes the adviser is not access but the reporting relationship: Morgan reports to the firm.

**Figure 1:** *Agree the adviser’s assignment without obscuring company responsibility. The agreement settles three things: its **purpose**, the work it is for; the **authority** it does and does not carry; and the **capacity** — the people and time — behind it.*

The role comes from one supplied private brief, not a universal definition. [P01: supplied role brief](bibliography.html) Ask what the assignment is and what it authorizes. Read the investment agreements first — the contracts with the **shareholders** who own part of the company, which often already grant **information rights**: a named investor may inspect records and discuss the business with senior executives, within stated limits.

Establish **which job the adviser is doing**. **Due diligence** investigates a business before an investment decision. **Coaching** develops a person’s judgment. **Operating support** improves company work. **Formal oversight** reviews results and risks for the board or investor, producing findings rather than instructions unless a decision is delegated. Employees should know which they are hearing: an assessment finding, a recommendation or an authorized instruction.

**Separate influence from authority.** Proximity to the investor gives a suggestion weight; authority comes from an executive role, the **board** of directors that oversees company leadership, agreed shareholder rights or an explicit assignment — and the adviser has only what those grant. Engineers taking priorities from adviser, CTO and investment team at once face an unauthorized second chain of command.

The hardest case is a change of role. In a fictional scene, Morgan has coached Alex for three months when the firm asks for a written assessment of his leadership. Their coaching agreement is a written rule: nothing Alex says in coaching is reported to the firm, and a concern about the company goes to Alex first — and to Ines, Larkspur’s **chief executive officer** and company leader, only if it remains unresolved.

**Who approves the work?** Morgan may form an **investor opinion** from information the existing arrangements already lawfully provide, without new company approval. A **company-commissioned assessment**, or extra access such as interviews with Alex’s managers, needs Ines’s approval — or the board’s where it will decide the matter. Temporarily directing delivery work needs authority the board has granted.

**Who releases the coaching?** Only Alex can let his coaching statements be used in the assessment, because the agreement protects what he said. Approving the work does not lift that protection, although the agreement cannot override a disclosure the law requires. If Alex declines, Morgan works from independently obtained evidence and says so, or asks the firm to appoint someone else.

Build a **shared account**: the same facts for investor and engineers, each side naming the evidence that would change its view. The planning meeting then opens on agreed terms: Morgan recommends; Alex decides the technical plan within the approved budget.
