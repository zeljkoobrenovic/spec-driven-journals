An investor's explanation of value is not yet an instruction to a technology team. "We are valued on growth" leaves open growth in which customers, products and markets, with what retention, contribution and future investment. "We are valued on EBITDA" leaves open which year's earnings, under which adjustments, and how the company will sustain them. The translation work in between is where technology decisions are actually made.

**Business priority suggests architectural emphasis, not a specific design.** A company valued mainly on future expansion tends to need isolated changes, configurable workflows and reliable measurement — the capabilities that make learning cheap. A company valued on established, repeatable earnings tends to need lower cost to serve, simpler operations and predictable investment. Neither priority cancels the other: a growth plan with deteriorating unit economics may need cost work urgently, and an earnings-focused company whose product is becoming obsolete may need experimentation urgently. The useful question is which uncertainty currently matters most.

The translation has limits worth stating. Modularity is not a mandate for microservices; a modular application with one deployment is often cheaper for its team to operate. Consolidation is not a mandate for the lowest invoice; a managed service can cost more per month while reducing the total work of running the product. Architectural flexibility is a choice about which changes to make easier, at what cost. Efficient architecture is a choice about the total resources needed for an acceptable outcome. Both require a view of the company's future work, not a preference about technology.

**One proposal looks different through each lens.** Larkspur's €200,000 onboarding change, expected to avoid €100,000 of annual external setup cost after a one-year implementation, supports four legitimate readings. An earnings discussion examines the recurring cost reduction. A cash discussion includes the initial payment and the year's wait — cumulative savings recover the outlay about three years after the investment. A growth discussion asks whether easier onboarding removes a constraint on selling and serving more customers. An architecture discussion asks which boundaries deliver the improvement without a larger rewrite.

A multiple-based figure is tempting and easily misused: at an unchanged 10× EBITDA multiple, €100,000 of additional annual EBITDA corresponds to €1 million of enterprise value. That is a sensitivity calculation, not an independently established project value, and adding it to the present value of the same savings double-counts the benefit.

Before choosing a design, five things should be agreed among the CEO, CFO, product leader, CTO and Technology Principal: the value assumption, the operating requirement, the technical options, the funded transition, and the review evidence that would justify expansion, revision or stopping. A design should remain defensible if growth is slower, the multiple falls, or ownership lasts longer than planned.
