**Ask for the help your gap needs, not the help that happens to be on offer.** An investor can reach useful people fast, but its help earns its cost only when it leaves the company able to do the work itself.

Investors offer help for mixed reasons: a stronger company is worth more, but a shared team of advisers also needs to stay busy. The offer can be genuine and still not the best route.

**Describe the gap through the work that fails.** Rotaline, the fictional software company this book follows, must set up each new customer before they can use its product. The investor’s pre-deal investigation sampled five of these setups. In three, the configuration needed the same specialist’s manual attention, and across all five the whole team spent about 80 hours per customer. “We need help with onboarding” is hard to act on; “one person is a bottleneck and setup takes 80 hours” is not. Then say what kind of help you want: understanding the problem, making a decision, people to do the work, or building your own skills. Rotaline wants the last two.

Next, **compare credible routes for the same need**. The board has committed €180,000 to a pilot of a better setup. Priya, who leads product and is accountable for the pilot, measures eight pilot customers at day 90, and the board reviews the result at day 100. With Alex, who must find the engineering time, she weighs four routes. A peer conversation with a leader at another company the investor owns costs an hour and half a day’s preparation. The investor’s specialist offers ten days over six weeks from day 21, capped at €15,000. An independent specialist would take seven to nine weeks to find and contract, finishing too late for day 100. A permanent hire would take three to four months to start and cost about €150,000 a year.

They take the peer conversation first and choose the investor’s specialist, provided the references hold up and the start does not slip past day 21. The independent route is a fallback, started only if that choice falls through, and hiring waits for evidence from the pilot. The money stays inside the pilot’s budget, so Priya can approve it under authority delegated by Ines, the chief executive; Alex releases six engineer-days from research.

**Borrowed experience needs its context.** A peer’s practice arrives with their customers, scale and systems behind it, and a tool built for a bigger business may need administration you cannot support. Keeping a specialist service long-term is fine, as long as its cost and the dependence are explicit rather than discovered later.

The search ends with a request short enough to agree in one conversation: “Help our customer team make one setup path repeatable, using a specialist who can work alongside our engineer for six weeks; Priya will assess customer outcomes.” The next chapter turns that sentence into terms: [[set-terms-of-help]].
