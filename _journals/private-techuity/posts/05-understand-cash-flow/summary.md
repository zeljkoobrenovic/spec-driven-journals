**Profit on paper is not money you can spend.** Before committing to a new initiative, work out how much cash the company can actually use, and when.

**Figure 1:** *From earnings to the cash Rotaline can commit, the option that fits, and the evidence that decides what comes next.*

Take Rotaline, a fictional software company. Its **EBITDA**, earnings before interest, tax and the accounting charges that spread an asset’s cost over its useful years, is €10 million. That sounds like plenty of room. But interest on the company’s loans takes €4 million, tax €1 million and planned investment in equipment and software €2 million. Customers owe €1 million more at year end than they did at the start: money earned but not yet received. Then €1.5 million of the loan is repaid. What remains is **€0.5 million**.

Add €0.3 million of cash held above the reserve the board keeps for running the business, and Rotaline has about **€0.8 million it can commit** this year. This is a planning calculation, not a ranking of who gets paid first. Loan payments change only under the loan’s terms, tax is set by law, and investment is a choice.

Now Alex, who leads engineering, asks for €1 million to automate how new customers are set up, which today takes about 80 staff hours each. Instead of arguing yes or no, the team prices three options: keep a contract specialist through two busy quarters for €75,000; run a €210,000 pilot that automates one reusable setup step; or fund the full €1 million program. The full program does not fit. **Ines, the chief executive, approves the pilot**, which sits within the spending limit the board has delegated to her, and about €0.6 million stays untouched.

The pilot comes with a test. Priya, the product leader, measures the first eight customers at day 90, and at day 100 the board decides what the evidence justifies. More money follows only if the next eight customers average 50 hours or less, and only with its own priced approval. If the pilot disappoints, or customers pay late, the fallback is modest: keep the specialist one more quarter, provided Sam, who leads finance, forecasts that cash stays above the reserve.

Cash also runs out over time. In a separate scenario, a debt-free Rotaline holds €3 million and spends €250,000 a month more than it collects: **twelve months of runway**. Hires adding €100,000 a month cut that to about 8.6 months, before funding expected in month nine.

Debt raises the cost of being wrong. Loan conditions, called **covenants**, can give lenders rights after one weak quarter, and a floating interest rate can rise just as sales weaken. Accounting choices can move a cost between years, but the cash still leaves when it leaves.

Knowing what the company can afford is half the question. The other half is who may approve the spending, which Part II takes up in [[clarify-authority]].
