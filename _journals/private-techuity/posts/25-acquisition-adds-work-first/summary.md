An **acquisition** purchases a business, or a share of its ownership; **integration** makes chosen activities work together; a **carve-out** separates a business from its **parent**, the larger business that owns it. The plan behind a transaction can assume savings or independence before anyone has checked the work fits the time allowed — so raise those dependencies while scope, price and timing can still change.

They are **different processes under one principle**: a new boundary, a continuing customer promise. Combining is complete when the chosen integration works for customers, the benefit named in the **purchase rationale** — the buyer's explanation of how the purchase will create value — is evidenced, and duplicated cost counted as a saving has ended. Separating is complete when the business has run a defined period with no temporary parent service. A service it keeps buying on purpose, under a contract it can end, is a supplier relationship, not a failed separation.

**Choose depth for a named benefit**, not as a stage of maturity: a sales agreement between separate products, shared services beneath them, connected workflows, or one combined product. Selling an acquired product to existing customers does not by itself require joining the two.

**The work competes with the product plan.** In the fictional purchase closing 1 April 2026, the two engineers who understand Larkspur’s scheduling software have twenty-six **engineer-weeks** of project time to September — one engineer-week is one engineer for one week. Three jobs need twenty-nine. Their part of the integration takes April to June, and the wider team then prepares a launch on 1 January 2027. The customer booking website moves to 31 December; the next purchase candidate is investigated from July. Something waits; the decision is which, and who approves it.

**Fund independence before the parent’s services stop.** A second, separate fictional example, with its own dates and figures: Larkspur is instead a division being separated from a parent. The share of parent costs charged to it — €400,000 a year — is an accounting split, not a quotation. The same services bought independently cost €700,000 a year, plus €300,000 one-time. Each replacement first runs two months beside the parent’s service before the old one is switched off. That cuts the parent’s charges, but the cut is **charges avoided, not a saving**: while both run, the company pays for both.

**Reserve the fallback before it is needed.** A single three-month extension of the parent’s services must be **reserved in writing by 31 March 2027**, the last date it can be requested — while the test that might fail runs in May. Reserve it whatever March shows: it costs nothing until 31 May, after which the whole €157,500 is owed. Hold money for reconnection and repair beside it. A spare month is not a recovery month: only a failure in the planned May test leaves room to repair and retest. Past the contractual end the **company directors** can only ask the parent for terms it may refuse, or fall back on an outside supplier lined up months earlier — arranged late, that is no fallback, and the interruption is real.

Confirm rights to code, data, licences and contracts before moving or combining anything. Measure the benefit the purchase rationale named; count a shared saving once. Ownership changes on the transaction date; the operating transition is complete only when the tests pass.
