# Spec: Turn “We Expect Growth” Into a Design Decision

## Intent

Translate an investor’s growth assumption, which reaches the team as a request for flexibility, into one completed design decision. Start from the assessed constraint handed over by [[can-the-team-deliver]] (country rules coupled into the invoicing module) after [[fix-decisions-before-hiring]] has relieved the queue around it; compare configuring a supplier, a company-owned boundary and a core replacement against the same customer need, delivery date, ongoing responsibility and cash limit; choose one under explicit assumptions and name the evidence that would reverse it. Keep technical evidence, valuation inference and the funding of future options separate. Cover structure, build and buy, so the chapter is usable by companies whose technology is largely bought and integrated.

## Audience

Product and engineering leaders inside companies working under investors, including leaders who inherit an ownership arrangement. Assume no specialist finance background; explain necessary terms before use. Investor-side readers are secondary.

## Success criteria

- Add one whole-post overview visual to the TL;DR after its opening paragraph, generated with the Nano Banana article illustrator. Provide alt text and a concise numbered caption. Keep the summary prose bold-emphasised and cited, and as short as it can be while still standing alone: the 300–500-word target is superseded where the standalone-comprehension criterion requires a definition, since a summary a lay reader cannot follow fails at any length. The current summary runs to about 800 words for that reason.
- Provide a distinctive article header logo and a simple navigation icon with unique asset paths. Keep both consistent with the journal’s visual style.
- Include two explanatory article figures with accurate short labels, alt text and numbered captions. Use restrained bold emphasis for key claims; preserve the words, citations and historical data. The comic modality is a sequence of comic pages: each page is one image of three stacked strips that carries its dialogue, labels and amounts in the artwork, with consistent fictional characters and a short caption under each page. Every string drawn in a page comes from the page script, which traces to the article, and generated artwork is inspected against the script (spelling, cast identity, which speaker each bubble points to, counts and sizes), not assumed.
- Make the company leader’s decision, authority and funding assumptions explicit. Distinguish the stated ownership arrangement from a claim about every investor.
- Keep historical findings within their source scope and label new comparative scenarios as fictional.
- Introduce necessary terms in ordinary language before using them in explanations, examples, tables, or diagrams; keep opening key points readable without prior study. This binds the financial vocabulary as strictly as the technical: expanding an abbreviation is not explaining it. EBITDA, enterprise value, net debt, present value, margin, holding period, buyout and funding round each need a plain-English gloss at first meaningful use, and the €1 million sensitivity is walked through a step at a time.
- Name the fictional company and the cast on first appearance, with each person's role in ordinary words rather than by abbreviation, so a reader can tell who approves spending from who delivers the work.
- Move from a concrete question through explanation and example to a practical conclusion; connect the conclusion to the next chapter in the configured reading order.
- Make summaries and comic storyboards understandable on their own; preserve source limits and label fictional scenarios, including changes in example assumptions. Standing alone means carrying its own definitions: a format that names EBITDA, a multiple, enterprise value, a replay or a specialist-week either explains it there or uses the simpler wording instead. Every date, amount and trigger in a short format matches the article's, including the distinction between switching supplier, deferring a launch and reassessing an assumption. A short format may not silently swap one financial measure for a looser one: if the €1 million sensitivity appears, it names the same earnings measure the article names and the assumptions the multiplication rests on, or the passage keeps only the payback and double-counting lesson.
- Give the short formats scannable structure where readers must compare things. The summary presents the three options and the reversal triggers as separate bullets or rows rather than one long paragraph, names each person's role where it names them, and explains reconciliation in its own words. The article's reassessment separates its three decision points — before spending, after €80,000, after launch.
- Describe artwork as it actually is. Alt text and captions transcribe what a page draws, kept separate from what it means; substantive labels drawn in a page are explained in visible caption text, not in alt text alone; and a diagram's direction must match the chronology its caption claims.
- The comic must carry the chapter's worked decision, not only its questions: at least one page comparing the three options with their prices and dates, and one showing the actual record — the €140,000 choice inside the €300,000 limit, the delivery target and the conditional supplier fallback. Alt text and transcripts reproduce the meaning of labels drawn in the artwork, and the artwork avoids background jargon it does not explain. A comic-only reader must also be able to say what the second country actually requires (paying customers within twelve months, a third country expected within two years), what B changes (the country rules are separated out, so later countries are settings), why B was chosen over the cheaper-at-first A, and that the fallback needs available specialists and follows unexplained replay differences after the two-week investigation window. Every short format carries **both** switch triggers the article states — unresolved replay differences, or the loss of the protected specialist time B requires — and says that A still needs its own two specialist-weeks and a supplier schedule reaching payment inside twelve months, or the launch is deferred.
- Keep the two article figures conceptually honest: modularity and separate deployment are distinct decisions, and a business need is drawn as a question to examine rather than as a prescribed technical answer.
- Show why "we are valued on growth" and "we are valued on EBITDA" are incomplete as instructions, and what questions complete them.
- Map growth and earnings priorities to technical capabilities and their trade-offs as a short overview plus two smaller build-side and buy-side comparisons, without prescribing microservices for growth or cost-cutting for earnings; treat supplier or single-person dependence by comparing impact and likelihood with the cost and effectiveness of alternatives, including consequences the company cannot accept (linked to [[prove-you-can-restore]]), never by rarity alone.
- Complete the Larkspur second-country design decision: three options in one table, the chosen option, alternatives rejected, funding (€140,000 of a €300,000 envelope, stated as a separate, later scenario from the hundred-day plan), costs compared on one basis (first-year cash, then recurring supplier fees; the fallback priced at €60,000 setup plus €40,000 a year, its stop state defined), scarce capacity (six specialist-weeks, defined as effort not elapsed time), who is authorized, and the reversing evidence.
- State each reversal trigger as the action it actually justifies, judged on money and time still ahead rather than money already spent. Losing the third-country assumption is a **reassessment** trigger, not an automatic switch: say what is compared at that review and how the answer differs before any spending and after €80,000 is spent.
- Make every A-versus-B preference conditional on a stated number of years of use. A is cheaper in the first year, level with B at two subscription years and dearer from the third; no format may call either option simply "better" without naming the horizon, the delivery risk and the staff capacity that carry the judgment. Keep the two reasons separate wherever either is given: expected use beyond two subscription years already favours B on its own, and the expected third country is a second, independent reason that strengthens the case and brings the advantage forward. No format may present the third country as the thing that makes B cheaper within the holding period.
- Price B as *no further supplier subscription*, never as costing "nothing". Its maintenance consumes the billing engineer's already funded time, which is an existing budget line rather than an eliminated cost; cumulative comparisons stay explicitly on the additional-cash basis.
- Distinguish a planning condition from an arithmetic deadline. Where the fallback's latest start is asserted, show the calculation once — decision date + supplier wait + setup + payment delay — and state the margin it actually leaves. The latest workable wait depends on the setup length: a five-month setup allows one and a half months, a four-month setup two and a half. No sentence may declare the date lost at a wait the arithmetic still permits under the other setup length.
- Fund *and staff* the fallback. Where the article shows the supplier route being invoked, name who supplies its eight engineer-weeks, how its two specialist-weeks are protected when the trigger is itself a loss of specialist capacity, and what happens to the date if they are not available.
- Hold one milestone throughout: first **paying** customers within twelve months. Where a date is an invoice date or a go-live date, label it as such and state the payment terms that carry it to cash receipt, so the required event and not merely the event before it falls inside the window.
- Keep the €200,000 / €100,000 payback and the 10× sensitivity as a bounded, separately labelled illustration, including the double-counting trap.
- Fill the five-answer decision record with the chosen design and stress it against slower growth, a lower sale valuation and a longer ownership period.
- Open with the specific condition (an assumption arriving without its reasoning), not a contrast between stable and investor ownership; use “a common arrangement” / “one common way” rather than prevalence claims.
- Make the boundary with [[can-the-team-deliver]] explicit and do not repeat its assessment; hand off to [[cheaper-cloud-bill]] by naming the operating cost line the design creates.

## Non-goals

A valuation method (that is the financial valuation primer’s job), current market multiples, or an algorithm deriving architecture from a financial ratio.

## Modalities

Article, TL;DR, illustrated comic of eight pages (three strips each) — matching the rest of the manuscript.
