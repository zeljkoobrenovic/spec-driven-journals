# Spec: Find the Cash Behind Your Technology Budget

## Intent

Show that positive EBITDA does not establish the cash available for a new initiative: reconcile it with investment, tax, financing payments, working capital and cash on hand, then check dates and restrictions. Work through the cash bridge, separate the lines that are obligations under an agreement from those that are management choices, price three options for one initiative with decision dates and deciders, and teach runway and financing downside in a separate loss-funded scenario. No universal payment hierarchy and no contrast with companies that have no investors.

## Audience

Product and engineering leaders inside companies working under investors, including leaders who inherit an ownership arrangement. Assume no specialist finance background; explain necessary terms before use. Investor-side readers are secondary.

Each specialist payment is assigned once: operating-plan engagement, extension, or neither; the €180,000 contains no specialist days. The funded pilot cost reproduces without another chapter.
- Present the pilot so a newcomer can find its purpose, additional cash, staffing, approver, evidence date and fallback without reconstructing them: short labelled paragraphs (what the pilot buys; who does the work; the specialist after March; what it leaves out; the calendar; the maintenance charge; who approves, with dates and authority in separate paragraphs) and one compact two-column at-a-glance table; the opening carries no second recap of the chapter’s plan; the day-100 check is a short table followed by its qualifications and its conclusion in separate paragraphs; the comic gives the immediate choice and the later review a page each (three priced options; evidence, then money); the numbered sequence remains the main chronology; comparisons with other chapters sit in one labelled scenario note; in the fallback, the cash conclusion and the covenant explanation are separate paragraphs. The specialist passage separates the option’s terms, the dates on each path (including option 1) and the mid-year case; the mid-year check separates the payments already made, the remaining commitments, the scenario exclusion, the continuing collection shortfall, and the 30 June balance arithmetic into their own short paragraphs; the covenant section separates the definition and types, the leverage example, and the breach consequences. The day-100 table uses the template’s compact-table treatment (few short columns, cells wrap) so a 390-pixel phone shows both descriptions and amounts without sideways scrolling; wide tables keep the scrolling treatment.
- Anchor the pilot chronology to the financial year: day 0 is the first day of the financial year (approval), the step enters service about day 35 (early February), day 90 is 1 April, day 100 about 10 April, the mid-year review 30 June, and the residual is counted at each quarter end. Maintenance is charged at €2,500 a month from the month the step enters service, so €27,500 falls in this financial year and the last €2,500 of the first maintenance year in January (so the payments still due after day 100 are at most €150,000 through December and €152,500 including January); state that this deliberately differs from [[raise-what-you-need]] and the day-100 ledger, which place the first year of maintenance in the following financial year, and that Ines reserves the full €30,000 at approval.
- Show the fallback’s affordability with dated checks rather than the original annual total: for a poor day-90 cohort (decided at day 100) and for weak first-half collections (decided at mid-year), state cash above the protected reserve with maintenance already paid deducted, remaining non-cancellable build payments, remaining maintenance only and the €37,500 specialist cost. For the mid-year case give a short dated schedule (30 June to 30 September) with the payment dates, state whether the collection shortfall persists (assume it continues at the same rate in the second half) and show the first date cash would cross the protected reserve; make the fallback conditional on the revised forecast covering those dates, naming the alternatives (spending reduction, board approval, funding) when it does not. State the day-100 assurance narrowly: the roughly €330,000 above the reserve covers the listed additional pilot payments through year end on the assumption that ordinary payroll, tax, interest and other operating obligations stay funded from the operating plan and that later quarters add no further surplus; it is not a claim that the business could survive with no customer receipts, and the quarter-end residual is a simplified timing convention, not proof against a cash shortage inside a quarter. If collections are already behind plan at day 100, the forecast is rerun then, with actual receipts, the payments already made and every remaining commitment including the specialist once the option is exercised; an April shortfall is never deferred to the June check. Check two is a separate mid-year illustration whose balances assume the day-100 option was not exercised; once it has been, the specialist cost is already committed and partly paid, and those balances are not reused.
- In the downside case, say why the covenant is rechecked: lower countable cash raises net debt, defined there beside its first consequential use (borrowing less the cash the loan agreement lets the company count against it); whether EBITDA also falls depends on whether the shortfall is late payment of recorded invoices (cash down, earnings unchanged) or weaker trading (both down). No unsupported “usually” claim.
- Label the accounting comparison table with its currency and scale (illustrative amounts, € millions) and name its first column; state that the crisis study concerns almost 500 UK PE-backed companies during the 2008 crisis compared with matched non-PE peers.
- The comic’s first page must distinguish money customers owe Larkspur (not yet arrived) from Larkspur’s own payments, in artwork, caption and alt text: interest, tax and planned investment go into trays on Sam’s desk, while the bar for unpaid invoices is still in a customer’s hands, so the picture shows money owed to the company. Its caption describes the adjustment as the increase in unpaid customer invoices during the period, not their whole balance, with the €2 million to €3 million contrast, so the standalone comic leads to a €1 million adjustment. The three-priced-options page must show the decision: the sum that gives the €0.8 million capacity, the three priced options against it, and Ines approving the €210,000 pilot. Its caption reads on its own: it opens with short complete sentences that state, as an explicit fictional assumption, that after interest, tax, loan repayment, planned investment and the operating reserve the board protects, the forecast leaves about €800,000 across the year to commit, subject to checking payment dates and separate from the runway page’s €3 million scenario; it says the pilot automates part of the manual setup of new customers and carries no backward reference to the article. The final page shows the day-100 board review and the later conditional gate as the undecided part. Every amount, label and line of dialogue drawn in a page comes from the page script, which traces to the article. Generated artwork is inspected against the script (spelling of every string, cast identity, which speaker each bubble points to, counts and sizes), not assumed.
- The summary reads on its own: it explains capital expenditure, a floating interest rate and Sam’s finance role at first use, says that rising floating rates can raise borrowing costs at the same time as sales weaken (not that they must), states the month-six gate’s conditions as the article does (a further eight customers whose average is no more than 50 staff hours per setup, so one 60-hour setup among seven 48-hour setups still passes; waiting time down; satisfactory cash timing; a separate priced approval, at the board’s discretion), and splits the decision into a short choice paragraph and a short review-and-fallback paragraph. The comic explains interest, asset and expense at first use and says “unpaid customer invoices” where the article says working capital; the article explains inventory, research versus development and GAAP in plain words. The summary also explains inventory (goods held for sale or use), asset (equipment or software that benefits the company for years), cohort (another group of customers), the month-six gate (a review that must be passed before more spending can be approved) and parent company (the company that owns this one) locally, stays within 300–500 words of prose counted as published (cross-link titles expanded; image line and caption excluded), and ends with an explicit transition naming who may approve spending and funding commitments. The article describes Larkspur’s transaction as a buyout that was, in this case, financed largely with borrowing (a leveraged buyout), not as if every buyout were borrowing-financed.
