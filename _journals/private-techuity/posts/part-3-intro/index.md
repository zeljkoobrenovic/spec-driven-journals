> **Part III in one line:** this is the part about your actual job — and about the claims you should refuse to make on its behalf.

<br>
Parts I and II were about the arrangement around the company. This part is about the work inside it, and it is the longest in the book for a reason: it is where most of a technology leader's decisions live.

One argument runs through all eight chapters. **Technology work becomes business value only by changing what someone does** — a customer, an operator, a buyer — and that chain has more places to break than most business cases admit. Every chapter here is a specific case of that translation.

## What These Eight Chapters Do

- [[pt-product-value]] sets out the chain itself, from roadmap item to revenue, and is the parent argument for everything after it.
- [[pt-engineering-and-architecture]] and [[pt-valuation-and-design]] cover the systems underneath: what engineering work the business case requires, and how an owner's valuation assumption reaches an architecture decision.
- [[pt-cloud-economics]], [[pt-security-and-resilience]] and [[pt-data-and-ai]] take three areas where claims are easy to make and hard to verify.
- [[pt-people-and-operating-models]] covers the constraint that defeats the others when ignored: the people, and how decisions move among them.
- [[pt-acquisitions-and-carveouts]] closes the part with acquisitions and separations, which stress all of the above at once, against a date someone else set.

## What You Should Be Able to Do

By the end of Part III you should be able to state, for any initiative you are proposing, which link in the chain you are relying on and what evidence would show it holding. You should also be quicker to notice when someone else — including you — has skipped a link.

The recurring discipline is modesty about attribution. Several chapters argue against claims your own side would like to make, because a claim that cannot survive scrutiny damages the next one you need to make.

Part IV then changes seats entirely, and looks at the same work from the investor's side of the table.
