> **IN THIS SECTION, YOU WILL:** Get an introduction to Part III, Turning Investor Expectations Into Commitments, and an overview of its nine chapters in three moves: choose and justify the work, assess and change the delivery system, then apply the reasoning to major operating choices.

<br>
An investor’s **thesis** explains why it expects an investment to succeed. For a company leader, that expectation becomes a series of choices: which customers to serve, what to build, which systems to change and which people to hire. Translating the thesis into those choices is your responsibility. The thesis itself is a prediction rather than a fixed instruction, and evidence from the work may show it needs to change.

The same choice looks different under different planning assumptions. Suppose better onboarding needs engineering work. If further funding is uncertain, a small test may have to establish demand before anyone is hired permanently. Under an expansion plan, the question may instead be whether the change repeats across markets. Where the company has borrowed, the timing of payments can become decisive. A corporate owner may need an interface that fits group distribution. These are alternative assumptions to plan against, not rules attached to investor labels; the chapters keep this comparison short and refer back here.

This is the center of the book, and its nine chapters follow three moves. Throughout, make the **expected benefit, funding horizon and work displaced** visible together. When an investor’s target cannot be met within those constraints, show **which assumption or decision must change**.

## The Learning Path

**First, choose and justify the work.**

- [[cannot-fund-everything]] chooses a combination of work that fits both the cash and the team time available, then revises it when a test fails.
- [[roadmap-to-revenue]] follows one chosen change through customer behavior to a business result, and finishes the pilot decision.

**Then assess and change the delivery system.**

- [[can-the-team-deliver]] assesses what the software and the team can support, producing a finding the next two chapters use.
- [[fix-decisions-before-hiring]] changes decision-making and responsibilities before adding people, and decides whether hiring still proceeds.
- [[growth-into-design]] chooses one implementation from the assessed constraint, with its funded transition.

**Finally, apply the same reasoning to major operating choices.**

- [[cheaper-cloud-bill]] tests whether a lower bill reflects a better service unit and completes a commitment decision.
- [[prove-you-can-restore]] carries a failed restore test through funded correction and retest.
- [[ai-strategy-three-questions]] separates product opportunity, internal workflow and competitive substitution into three funded decisions.
- [[acquisition-adds-work-first]] applies the reasoning when businesses are combined or separated.

By the end, you should be able to explain why one combination of work deserves resources, how its benefits would arise and what evidence would change the plan. Value here means customer outcomes and continued capability as well as financial results.

Begin with [[cannot-fund-everything]]. Part IV then considers how the investor’s people and connections can help close the gaps the plan exposes.
