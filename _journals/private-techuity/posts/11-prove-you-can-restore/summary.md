At six in the morning a dispatcher at a Larkspur customer opens the scheduling service to assign the day’s jobs, and it doesn’t load. **Security** protects systems and information from harm; **resilience** is the ability to continue or restore essential work through disruption; **recovery** is the work of restoring service afterwards. The dispatcher doesn’t care which one failed. The company’s question is what evidence it has that dispatch would resume, how soon, and what that evidence costs. The scenario is fictional.

Start with the **business function that must keep working** and write its recovery objective in plain words. Larkspur’s, agreed by product, the customer team and the CTO: dispatch must resume within four hours of a failure, with no more than fifteen minutes of lost schedule updates. A fictional objective, not a legal standard; every restore test is judged against it. The US **NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0** supplies consistent questions across six functions without prescribing one implementation. [S17: NIST CSF 2.0](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/CSWP/NIST.CSWP.29.pdf)

**Evidence of a backup is not evidence of an operating service.** Diligence found backups had run without error for three years and nobody had restored from them. The day-45 test took eleven hours, stopped twice (a missing credential, an unavailable database version) and restored data a night old: it failed the objective on both time and lost updates. A restore test must show consistent data, working access, running dependencies and a dispatcher assigning a job within the objective.

A failed test is a finding with a price. The board-approved plan already carried “improve and test service restoration” at **€80,000 and four engineer-weeks**, chosen ahead of the customer portal because recovery was a customer commitment. It buys a restore environment at the production database version, managed credentials with an emergency procedure, fifteen-minute backups, and a rehearsal the operations lead can run. Deferring until the next financing round was rejected because it names nobody who accepted the exposure; a two-region redesign was rejected as outside the envelope and unnecessary for four hours. The CTO proposed, the CFO confirmed the envelope, the CEO approved under the board’s plan.

**Risk arithmetic supports the decision but is not the reason for it.** A 5% annual chance of a €4m outage implies €200,000 expected annual loss; cutting the probability to an assumed 2% leaves €80,000, a €120,000 difference. The probabilities are illustrative, a small chance of not surviving is not made acceptable by an affordable average, and the €120,000 is not a booked operating gain.

The corrected restore at day 85 met the objective. The record lists what it did not cover: a regional cloud failure, a rehearsal outside the dispatch window, and dependence on two people. The CEO accepts that **residual risk** on the board’s behalf with a quarterly retest. Documented acceptance records a decision; it cannot discharge an unmet mandatory obligation, which specialist interpretation governs. Shared investor platforms and data requests need agreed purpose, access and incident responsibility (Part IV); the passed test and its written limits are what a future owner should inherit.
