At six in the morning a dispatcher at a Larkspur customer opens the scheduling service to assign the day’s jobs, and it doesn’t load. **Security** protects systems and information from harm; **resilience** keeps essential work going through disruption; **recovery** restores service afterwards. The dispatcher doesn’t care which one failed. The company’s question is what evidence it has that dispatch would resume, how soon, and at what cost. The scenario is fictional.

Start with the **business function that must keep working** and write its recovery objective in plain words. Larkspur’s, agreed by product, the customer team and the CTO: dispatch must resume within four hours, with no more than fifteen minutes of lost schedule updates. A fictional objective, not a legal standard; every restore test is judged against it. The US **NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0** supplies consistent questions without prescribing one implementation. [S17: NIST CSF 2.0](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/CSWP/NIST.CSWP.29.pdf)

**Evidence of a backup is not evidence of an operating service.** Diligence found three years of error-free backups and no restore. The day-45 test took eleven hours, stopped twice (a missing credential, an unavailable database version) and restored data a night old: it failed on both counts. A restore test must show consistent data, working access, running dependencies and a dispatcher assigning a job within the objective.

A failed test is a finding with a price, and the planned price was already spent. The board’s €500,000 / 24-engineer-week hundred-day envelope carried REC-1 at **€80,000 and four engineer-weeks**, ahead of the customer portal because recovery was a customer commitment; by day 45 that had bought the restore environment, backup-store access, fifteen-minute backups and the test itself. The correction (a version-matched environment, managed credentials with a two-person procedure, a written rehearsal) costs **€20,000 and two engineer-weeks from the reserve**. Deferral was rejected because it names nobody who accepted the exposure; a two-region redesign as outside the envelope and unnecessary for four hours. The CTO proposed; the CFO confirmed only the reserve could pay; because a reserve draw changes what the envelope funds, the CEO took it to the board, which approved a retest by day 85.

**Risk arithmetic supports the decision but is not the reason for it.** A 5% annual chance of a €4m outage implies €200,000 expected annual loss; cutting the probability to an assumed 2% leaves €80,000, a €120,000 difference. The probabilities are illustrative, a small chance of not surviving is not made acceptable by an affordable average, and the €120,000 is not a booked operating gain.

The day-85 retest met the objective for loss of the application environment in this region, not for a regional outage. The record lists what it did not cover: that regional failure, a rehearsal outside the dispatch window, and dependence on two people. The CEO accepts that **residual risk** on the board’s behalf with a quarterly retest; the region question returns at the next quarterly review. Documented acceptance records a decision; it cannot discharge an unmet mandatory obligation, which specialist interpretation governs. Shared investor platforms and data requests need agreed purpose, access and incident responsibility (Part IV); a passed test with written limits is what a future owner should inherit.
