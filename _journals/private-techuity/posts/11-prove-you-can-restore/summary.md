**Security** protects systems and information from harm. **Resilience** is the ability to continue or restore essential work through disruption. Together they protect the company's ability to operate, keep commitments, and recover. Their value cannot be judged solely from whether an incident happened during the reporting period. Under investors, a security investment competes with growth work for limited cash and appears as cost without revenue, while a breach during the ownership period can reduce what the company is worth and diligence will test the controls.

Start with **critical business services** and credible failure scenarios. Identify what customers would lose, how long the company could tolerate disruption, and which dependencies matter. Then connect controls, response, and recovery to those scenarios. A compliance checklist can support the work but does not replace evidence that the service can withstand or recover from failure.

The US National Institute of Standards and Technology’s **NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0** includes governance alongside identification, protection, detection, response, and recovery. [S17: NIST CSF 2.0](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/CSWP/NIST.CSWP.29.pdf) The implication for ownership is practical: executives and boards must understand the risk decisions, responsibilities, and resources. Purchasing a security tool does not transfer that accountability.

**Risk reduction is different from booked profit.** The fictional example in the full article changes an assumed annual loss probability and calculates a reduction in expected loss. Those probabilities are uncertain, a low-probability event could still threaten the company’s survival, and the result is not an EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) saving. Use scenarios and sensitivity to support judgment rather than presenting false precision.

**Recovery needs operational evidence**. A backup exists; a restore test demonstrates something about usability. Even a successful technical restore may not establish that customer service, communications, data reconciliation, and supplier dependencies can recover within the required time. Test the business process as well as the component.

Product and engineering leaders should put material risk into the operating plan, request focused expertise where needed and ensure findings receive an owner and a decision. Company management retains its responsibilities. Information sharing must respect the engagement's confidentiality, purpose, and authority.

Uncertain funding does not identify **who may accept risk**. If a recovery improvement is delayed, name the service exposure, feasible minimum work and authorized decision-maker. A minority investor’s request for information or a corporate owner’s shared security offer also needs a defined purpose, access boundary and operating responsibility. Budget for the local work required to adopt shared services, and test whether the company can still recover if the provider or ownership changes.

A useful scorecard therefore combines exposure, remediation progress, tested recovery, and consequential exceptions. Avoid rewarding teams for suppressing incident reports or closing findings without verifying the result. The objective is a company that understands and manages its exposure, including the uncomfortable decisions that require money, time, or an explicit acceptance of the risk remaining after the chosen protections.
