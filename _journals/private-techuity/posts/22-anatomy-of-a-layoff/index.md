> **IN THIS SECTION, YOU WILL:** Learn to turn a request to cut staff into an operating plan: find where the pressure actually comes from, compare the alternatives by how much cash each leaves month by month, choose roles by the work that stops, and plan for the people who leave and the people who remain.

> **WHY INVESTORS CARE:** An investor lending a company money to carry it until a delayed investment arrives is putting new money at risk, and it wants that money to last. A reduction that costs more than it saves in the months that matter, or that removes the work the new investors expect to pay for, protects nobody’s investment.

> **WHY YOU SHOULD CARE:** “Cut 20% of engineering” is a number, not a plan. The roles you keep, the work you stop and the dates the savings arrive decide whether the company keeps enough cash until new money comes, and what it can still deliver afterwards.


> **KEY POINTS:**
>
> * **Find the real source of the pressure.** A condition attached to a loan, a director’s vote and an adviser’s comparison with other companies are three different things. Only the first two carry authority, and only the first sets a date. Answer each with the response it requires.
> * **Reconcile the reduction by date.** People who leave are paid through their notice period and receive a leaving payment before any saving appears. At Larkspur’s terms and dates the reduction cannot replace the late investment; it only makes the bridging loan last longer. Elsewhere the answer depends on the costs, the savings and the funding dates, so show cash month by month under each plan before anyone is told.
> * **Choose roles by the work that stops, and plan for both sides of the door.** The people who leave need notice, a leaving payment and a record of what they knew. The people who remain need a published list of what is no longer expected of them, a plan for who answers problems out of hours, and the evidence that would restore the plan.

<br>
Outside investment **cuts both ways for a team**. It can let a company hire much faster than customer revenue alone would allow: a round of new investment can pay for roles that revenue would not cover for years, and a headcount plan can grow by a third in a quarter.

The same money arrives with obligations and expectations attached: a minimum cash balance to keep, loans with conditions, a growth or profit target, a date by which the plan must show results. When the money behind the plan moves, **the pressure to cut costs can arrive as quickly as the money to add them did**, and that is how layoffs happen in companies that were hiring a year earlier. Layoffs are unpopular, and done badly they are damaging: they remove capacity the investment was meant to build, they cost the trust of the people who remain, and the savings arrive later than the board expects. This chapter is about doing one as well as it can be done, which begins with knowing where the pressure comes from.

It is 1 October at fictional Larkspur, a company that sells scheduling software to businesses whose staff work in the field, such as maintenance firms. A scheduled board review is about to begin; the **board** is the group of directors who oversee the company and approve its plan. The scenario is a delayed investment, and three terms define it:

- The **round** is the €4 million of new investment Larkspur has been negotiating since June, in which investors would receive shares, stakes in the company’s ownership. It is still an **expression of interest**, a stated intention with nothing signed. Its **closing**, the day the money is paid in, keeps moving.
- The **bridge** is a smaller loan from the existing investors, €600,000, meant to carry the company until the round closes.
- The **reserve** is the minimum cash the board has agreed never to fall below, €400,000. Falling below it breaks the board’s own safety limit; it does not mean the company has run out of money.

The existing investors signed their commitment to the bridge on 20 September, three weeks after the date the board had set, and it comes with a condition. They will pay the bridge only if, by 31 October, the board has adopted an **operating plan**, the agreed plan for what the company will do, whom it employs and what it spends, that keeps Larkspur’s cash above the €400,000 reserve until at least 30 June, counting no money from the delayed round.

Four people carry the decision. Ines, the chief executive officer (**CEO**), leads the company. Sam, the chief financial officer (**CFO**), leads finance and keeps the cash forecast. Alex, the chief technology officer (**CTO**), leads engineering, and Priya leads product. In August, when the round first slipped, the board chose the bridge as its fallback, decided a first reduction, **R1**, that ends the company’s contract engineers on 31 October, and asked Alex and Priya to cost a deeper reduction for this review. Now the board has to decide it.

How the round slipped and how the board chose that fallback is the subject of the chapter [[the-financing-slipped]] in Part IV; this chapter can also be read on its own. It stays deliberately separate from the shared Larkspur ledger that runs through the chapters [[first-hundred-days]] and [[handover-of-obligations]]: its cash, its monthly spending, its people and its dates are its own, and every figure is fictional. What the funding decision never needed to state is who works at Larkspur and what they cost. **A reduction cannot be decided without that baseline**, so it comes first.

The table reproduces the funding decision’s figures: a €200,000 burn; €150,000 from 1 November, once **R1**, the first reduction, already decided, ends the agreements of the contract engineers who work on internal tooling, the software Larkspur’s own staff use; and €500,000 of cash on 31 December without the bridge. **Collected revenue** is the money customers have actually paid, not what has been invoiced or forecast, and **net monthly burn** is the cash the company pays out each month beyond what customers pay in.

The payroll row is pay plus employer charges and benefits: the part of a person’s cost that stops when their employment ends. The fuller cost of a role also includes the laptop and software the person uses. Here those sit in the €76,000 row, because they do not simply stop when someone leaves: a laptop already bought is not refunded, and a software licence may run to the end of its term.

The engineers are organized around five pieces of work:

- **Four on the onboarding pilot**, a trial of a faster way to set up new customers. The investors in the round expect to see its results.
- **Two on the recovery work**, which makes sure the service that sends field staff to their jobs can be restored quickly after a failure. It ends in December.
- **Five on the scheduling core**, the main product: fixing **incidents**, failures in the live service, making changes promised to customers, and maintenance.
- **Three on the pricing and tax rules for a second country**, where two customers signed in the summer with **go-live**, the date a customer starts using the product, promised for the first quarter, January to March.
- **Two on a mobile app for field workers**, which has three beta customers, customers trying an early version, no revenue yet and a launch planned for the second quarter.

One product manager and one designer work with the onboarding and core teams; the other product manager and designer belong to the mobile app.

## Three Pressures That Sound Alike

“The investors want a reduction” describes three different things at Larkspur, and a company leader has to answer each with a different response. The chapter [[decide-who-decides]] gives the general rule; here it is applied.

The first is a **funding condition**. The bridge commitment says the money arrives only if the plan holds the reserve to 30 June. That is a condition attached to cash, with a date, and the company either meets it or does without the money. **It says nothing about headcount**: a plan that met the date by other means would satisfy it just as well. Sam’s first job is to translate the condition into a number, and the arithmetic is short. With R1 and the bridge, cash on 31 December is €1.1 million and the burn is €150,000, so the reserve is reached about 20 May. To hold it to 30 June, spending between February and June must fall by at least €40,000 a month, more if the reduction itself costs money first.

The second is a **formal approval right**. Larkspur’s fictional **shareholders’ agreement**, the contract among its owners that sets their rights, makes adopting or materially changing the annual operating plan a **reserved matter**: a decision the agreement takes out of management’s hands and gives to a named party. Here that party is the **investor director**, the board member appointed by the lead investor, the investor that led Larkspur’s earlier funding; and the operating plan is the board’s to approve in any case. A reduction changes what the plan funds, so it is the board’s decision, and the investor director’s consent is part of it. That is authority, exercised through a seat and a clause, and the response is to bring the board a decision it can take: the options, the cash by date and the consequences.

The third is **influence**. At the same meeting the investor director says that in the other companies the investor’s fund has backed, a delayed round has meant a cut of about a fifth of engineering; a **fund** pools money from many investors and invests it in companies. Morgan, an adviser working for the investor, has seen the same. A fifth of Larkspur’s engineering payroll is about €28,000 a month. It would not meet the condition, and it would select people by a ratio rather than by the work the company is going to stop. A comparison with other companies is **information about those companies; it is not an instruction**, and treating it as one would produce a smaller saving than the condition needs and a worse team than the plan can afford. Alex records it as one option to cost beside the others.

## The Alternatives, Costed on the Same Basis

Before a reduction, the board asks what else could close the gap. Several measures are already in force or easy to state, and the table puts them on one basis: the recurring monthly saving once it has fully arrived, the one-off cash it costs first, the first month in which the full saving appears, and what the company gives up. Every figure is fictional.

Two rows need explanation. R3 is the number the board asked for in August, the cost level at which the company could continue with no new money at all: total costs of €250,000 against €250,000 of collected revenue, which means removing €150,000 a month beyond R1. It is an alternative to R2, not a further step after it. **It is worth knowing, and it is not a plan.** At that level Larkspur would release the two second-country customers, cut the implementation and support team that the collected revenue depends on, and take the engineers off the onboarding pilot whose results the round’s investors are waiting to see. It also would not save January, for the reason the next section shows.

R2 is the reduction the rest of this chapter follows: **it removes whole pieces of work rather than a slice of every team**, it meets the condition with a margin, and it keeps the protected obligations and the pilot intact.

## Reconcile the Reduction by Date

A reduction is often presented as a monthly saving. **Cash does not arrive that way.** Under Larkspur’s fictional employment terms:

- **Notice periods**, the time between a person being told their job ends and their last day, run from one to three months by length of service and are paid whether or not the person works them.
- **Severance**, a payment made on leaving, is one month’s pay for every two full years of service, with a minimum of one month, paid with the final salary.
- **Accrued leave**, holiday earned but not yet taken, is paid out.

R2 gives notice to eight people on 31 October: two engineers, a designer and a product manager from the mobile app, two of the three second-country engineers, one sales role that existed for the second-country expansion and one administrative role. Their combined pay, employer charges and benefits are €63,000 a month. A further €9,000 a month of non-payroll spending stops with the app: its **cloud environment**, the rented computing it runs on, its rented test devices, and the paid services used to test and distribute it. Those contracts end on 31 January, so that part of the saving starts in February.

Three of the eight leave the payroll on 30 November and three on 31 December. One of the two second-country engineers, who are on three months’ notice, leaves on 31 January. The other is retained on an agreed **fixed term**, a contract with a set end date, to 31 March, at the same monthly cost of about €8,500, for the reason given under customer commitments below. The saving therefore builds up gradually: about €20,000 in December (the three who left in November), about €45,000 in January (six people), €63,500 in February and March (seven people and the app’s contracts), and the full €72,000 from April.

The one-off costs run the other way: about €95,000 of severance, €15,000 of job-search support and legal cost and about €10,000 of accrued leave, €120,000 in all. They are paid with the final salaries: €30,000 in November, €45,000 in December, €32,000 in January and €13,000 for the retained specialist in March.

Sam’s cash forecast rests on stated assumptions:

- Cash on 1 October is €1.0 million. Customers keep paying €250,000 a month; no new sales are assumed.
- Nothing is counted from the two second-country customers at any point in the table, even after they go live. Their fees sit in Sam’s sales forecast instead, so the cash table does not depend on either customer going live or staying.
- The bridge’s €600,000 is **drawn**, meaning actually transferred to Larkspur’s account, on 15 November. Under its terms it is a convertible loan, one that turns into shares when the round closes. It carries no fee, its **interest**, the charge for borrowing the money, is added to the amount owed rather than paid, and nothing is payable in cash until twelve months after the money arrives. So no loan payment falls inside the table.
- Nothing from the round is counted, and neither is the executive pay deferral.
- Each month-end figure is the previous month’s cash, plus any funding received, minus the burn before R2, plus R2’s saving, minus R2’s one-off costs.
- The day on which cash crosses the reserve within a month is approximate, because it assumes spending is spread evenly across the month.

Under the plan, cash on 30 June is €488,000: about €88,000 above the reserve, a little more than one month of R2’s €78,000 burn. That meets the condition. The plan holds one contingency, one further month of the retained specialist at €8,500, described under knowledge transfer below. Used, it moves the specialist’s €13,000 final leaving payment from March to April, leaves €479,500 on 30 June and moves the reserve date to about 1 August, still inside the condition. Without R2, cash on 30 June is €200,000 and the reserve is breached about 20 May, which is why the investors attached the condition.

The last two rows are the comparison a board rarely asks for. Without the bridge, R2 or no R2, Larkspur is below the reserve in January: the one-off costs come first, and the first full month of saving is April. R2 with no bridge even reaches the reserve about ten days earlier than R1 alone, because its leaving costs arrive before its savings.

At Larkspur’s notice periods, severance terms and dates, **the reduction makes the bridge last longer and cannot stand in for it**. **That is a result of these figures, not a rule**: shorter notice, lower severance, an earlier decision or a later reserve date would change the answer, which is why transition costs, savings and funding have to be compared by date rather than assumed either way. Sam puts the dated comparison at the top of the board paper, the document the board reads before deciding. On these figures it is also why R3 is not the fallback if the bridge fails.

## The Decision, Recorded

The board meets on 15 October rather than waiting for its November date, because the condition’s date is 31 October and the notice periods have to start then for the savings to land by February. The record reads:

- **Chosen:** R2. Notices on 31 October; one second-country specialist retained to 31 March, under an agreement signed before any notice is served, to keep the second customer’s first-quarter promise; the plan adopted meets the bridge condition with cash of €488,000 at 30 June.
- **Rejected:** a fifth of engineering by ratio, because it saves €28,000 against a need of at least €40,000 and chooses people by proportion; R3, because it removes the revenue and the pilot the company exists to deliver and still does not hold January without the bridge; reduced hours, because they take capacity from the protected onboarding and recovery work as much as from anything else; and waiting for the round, because the round is an expression of interest and the condition’s date is 31 October.
- **Funding:** €120,000 of one-off cost from operating cash inside the adopted plan; the retained specialist’s fixed term, €17,000, inside the plan; the savings as tabled; one contingency of €8,500 for a further month of the specialist, spent only if the transfer test fails; the agreement’s May and June options, €17,000, held outside the plan and usable only with the board’s approval; the bridge drawn on 15 November once the plan is adopted.
- **Scarce capacity:** the one engineer who will remain on the second-country rules and the specialist retained until 31 March; Priya’s time for two customer negotiations; Alex’s time for eight individual conversations and the transfer test below.
- **Authorized:** the Larkspur board, with the investor director’s consent under the reserved matter, on 15 October. Ines gives the individual notices within the adopted plan and the applicable employment law; Alex and Priya select the roles by the work that stops, not by a ranking of people.

A record like this is **what distinguishes a decision from a number**. It shows the condition being met, the alternatives that were not chosen and why, and the evidence that would change the plan, which a later board, or a later owner, can check.

## The Team After the Plan

The reduction is chosen by work, so the organization that results can be described by work. In the base case, once the retained specialist leaves on 31 March, Larkspur has twelve engineers besides Alex from 1 April: four on the onboarding pilot, and eight on a core team. The core team is the original five, the two engineers redeployed from the recovery work when it ends in December, and the one remaining second-country engineer, who brings the second-country rules with them. Until the retained second-country specialist leaves, on 31 March or later if the transfer test described below fails, the specialist works alongside that team on the rules and the second customer’s configuration, so any extra months have thirteen engineers besides Alex. Product and design are Priya, one product manager and one designer, both with the onboarding and core teams. Implementation and support are unchanged, because the collected revenue depends on them.

Three things had to be decided for that team to be real rather than drawn.

**Work stopped.** The mobile app stops on 15 December. Its code is frozen and archived with a written record of what works, what does not and which defects are open, so that a later decision to restart it **starts from evidence rather than memory**. The app is withdrawn from the app stores. The three beta customers are told on 3 November and moved back to the web product, the version used in a browser, with their data exported by the closing date. The two sales prospects whose interest depended on the app come out of the forecast. Further countries stop too: the remaining second-country engineer serves the two signed customers and no others, and Sam removes expansion from every forecast until the round closes and a new decision is taken.

**Customer commitments revised, or kept.** The two second-country contracts promise go-live in the first quarter, by 31 March, and give the customer a right to terminate if go-live slips past 30 June. Those are two different dates. The first is the promise; the second is the customer’s remedy if the promise is badly missed, and **a right to terminate after June is not an agreement to be delivered in June**.

With only one remaining engineer, the promise could be kept for neither customer. So Priya settles the question with both before the notices are served and before the roles are fixed, so that no customer learns of the change from a departing engineer. The first customer’s own rollout, the introduction of the software across its business, is not ready before the autumn. It signs an amendment moving its go-live and its termination deadline to 30 September, with three months’ subscription fees waived: about €9,000 that was never in the collected revenue, because the customer is not yet live. The second customer declines any change, and the plan **keeps its original promise rather than reinterpreting it**: one of the two second-country engineers who would have left on 31 January is retained on a fixed term to 31 March.

Whether that is enough is an arithmetic question. An **engineer-month** is one engineer working full time for one month. Alex’s estimate, fictional like the rest and made for the 15 October board, counts the work left from 1 November. The country’s tax and pricing rules and the second customer’s **standard configuration**, the settings that fit Larkspur to that customer, need about eleven engineer-months. The first customer’s **bespoke configuration**, settings built for its own way of working, needs about five more.

One engineer-month of that goes to writing down the tax-rule mapping and reviewing it with the remaining engineer in November and December. That leaves twelve for eleven engineer-months of work: one spare, about two weeks of calendar time for the two engineers working in March. The 31 March date holds, but with little room, which is why the second customer’s progress is read at the December and February reviews. From April the remaining engineer works alone: six engineer-months to 30 September, about five for the first customer’s configuration and about one, roughly a day a week, for supporting the second customer once it is live.

The retention is part of the plan, not a contingency, and it is in the cash table above. What remains uncertain is recorded as such: the two-week margin, the second customer’s termination right if 30 June is missed, and the timing of both customers’ fees, which Sam’s sales forecast tracks and the cash table leaves out throughout.

**Knowledge transferred on real work, with a bounded fallback.** The two second-country engineers hold the mapping between the country’s tax rules and Larkspur’s configuration. One leaves on 31 January at the end of a three-month notice period, having written the mapping down and reviewed it with the remaining engineer. The other, by an agreement recorded before the notices are served, works a fixed term from 1 February to 31 March at the same monthly cost, about €8,500, €17,000 in all, alongside the remaining engineer; this specialist’s severance and accrued leave are paid with the final salary. The same agreement lets Larkspur keep the specialist month by month to 30 June at most, at €8,500 a month, and the specialist accepts those options when signing. Only the first further month, to 30 April, is in the adopted plan, as its contingency; May and June would change the plan and need the board’s approval. **Signing that agreement is a precondition of serving the notices.** Had the specialist refused, the board would have had to redesign the second-country part of R2 before 31 October, rather than discover the gap in April.

The transfer test from the chapter [[fix-decisions-before-hiring]] therefore **runs on the actual work rather than on an exercise**. In the first week of March the remaining engineer **releases**, meaning puts into use for the customer, real changes alone, without the specialist reviewing them. The test has two parts, read separately because they carry different risks:

- **Routine support:** the second customer’s configuration changes and its support requests. A mistake here is an inconvenience the customer reports.
- **A legally required rule change:** one change in the country’s tax rules, translated into Larkspur’s configuration and checked against the written mapping. A mistake here means the customer’s prices or tax are calculated wrongly under the law.

Passing both is the evidence that the remaining engineer can support the second customer after go-live and build the first customer’s configuration alone. Passing only routine support shows that part is safe, but the specialist still cannot leave, because nobody else has shown they can make a rule change. The fallback runs on dates fixed in advance:

| Date | What happens | Who can support the live second customer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| First week of March | Transfer test, both parts, on real releases | The specialist and the remaining engineer |
| 15 March | Pass both: the specialist leaves on 31 March. Fail either: the further month to 30 April starts, the plan’s only contingency, and the part that failed is repeated | The specialist, to 31 March or 30 April |
| First week of April | Retest, if the first test failed | The specialist |
| 15 April | The retest is read. Pass: the specialist leaves on 30 April. Fail: Priya opens talks with both second-country customers on an agreed early end, as the fallback | The specialist, to 30 April |
| By 22 April, if the retest failed | The board decides whether to use the May and June options, before the April month ends | The specialist, to 30 June at most |
| First week of June, read on 15 June | Final test. Pass: the specialist leaves on 30 June | The specialist, to 30 June |
| By 22 June, if the final test fails | The early end, or the unresolved branch below | See below |

If the retest fails, three steps follow, each saying who supports the live customer and what it costs:

1. **Keep the specialist under the agreed options, to 30 June at most**, at €8,500 a month. Support continues with no gap, and the specialist checks every rule change before release. It changes the adopted plan, so the board approves it with the investor director’s consent. May and June leave €462,500 on 30 June, still above the reserve, and bring the reserve date forward to about 25 July. The options bind both sides, although the specialist, like any employee, could still resign; that is one more reason the customer talks begin on 15 April.
2. **If the final test fails, agreed early ends with both second-country customers**, signed before 30 June. The failure affects both. Passing both parts of the test was the evidence that the remaining engineer could support the second customer and also build the first customer’s configuration alone. The six engineer-months from April give the remaining engineer time for that configuration, not proven ability with the tax rules. Ending either contract leaves the cash table unchanged, because it never counted either customer’s fees. Any settlements, such as refunding fees the second customer has already paid, come out of the €62,500 by which cash on 30 June exceeds the reserve, for both customers together. Each €10,000 brings the reserve date forward by about four days, and more than €62,500 paid by 30 June would break the bridge condition, so the board approves the amounts before Priya offers them.
3. **If the final test fails and either customer refuses an early end, the plan has no answer for that customer, and the record says so.** For the second customer, Larkspur would have a live customer, legally required rule changes to make and nobody who has shown they can make them alone. For the first, its 30 September go-live and the rule changes after it would rest on the same unproven ability. Until the board resolves this, the specialist’s departure is not part of an approved plan. By 22 June the board either agrees new terms with the specialist beyond 30 June, long enough to cover each customer that stays (for the first customer, through its go-live and until someone else has passed the rule-change test), costed and added to the forecast before they are offered, or takes legal advice on Larkspur’s obligations under each contract. Every further month at €8,500 brings the reserve date forward again, by about three days. The remaining engineer carries on with routine support only if they passed that part of the test. A hire can be reopened, but it adds nobody for months, because recruitment and the transfer test come first.

A contractor is not assumed, because **no contractor holds the mapping**. The mobile app needs no transfer, because nobody is going to change it; it needs the state-of-work record.

## The People Who Leave

A reduction chosen by work still ends in eight individual conversations, and the record of what the company owes each person is part of the plan, not an afterthought. At Larkspur the terms are the fictional ones above: notice paid in full, whether worked or not; severance on the stated formula; accrued leave paid out; a written reference; and job-search support at the company’s cost, often called **outplacement**.

Each person also holds share **options**, rights to buy shares at a fixed price that become usable, or **vest**, over time. The chapter [[different-bets]] explained why the **leaver terms**, the plan’s rules for what happens to options when someone leaves, decide what those are worth. Larkspur’s board treats the eight as **good leavers**, the category the plan treats more generously than someone dismissed for serious misconduct. So options that have vested can be **exercised**, used to buy the shares, for twelve months after leaving, rather than **lapsing**, expiring, after ninety days; unvested options lapse. None of this compensates for a job lost in the middle of a winter. It is what the company can do, **it is written down, and it is the same for all eight**.

The order of the day matters. On 31 October each of the eight is told individually in the morning, by the leader they work for, with Ines present or available, before anyone else in the company hears. **Nobody learns their fate from a calendar invitation or a changed access badge.** Where a person is asked to work part of the notice for the transfer test, that is agreed with them, not announced for them.

**The employment process belongs to a jurisdiction, and the thresholds decide the calendar.** Two statutes show how differently the thresholds can be drawn. Neither is asserted to govern Larkspur, the table below is a reference rather than advice, and the bibliography entries carry the article-level detail.

The European Union directive on collective redundancies covers dismissals for reasons not related to the individual workers concerned. An employer within it has two separate duties. It consults the workers’ representatives in good time, with a view to reaching an agreement, on ways of avoiding or reducing the redundancies and mitigating their consequences. And it notifies the competent public authority in writing. The redundancies then take effect no earlier than thirty days after that notification, without affecting individual notice rights. National law may let the authority shorten that period, or extend it to sixty days or longer. The directive is a floor: member states may be more favourable to workers, and the rule in any particular country is the national one. [S77: Council Directive 98/59/EC](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eudr/1998/59)

The United States **Worker Adjustment and Retraining Notification (WARN) Act** is a federal statute that requires advance written notice of a plant closing or a mass layoff, as it defines them, from employers above its size threshold. State law can add notice rules of its own. [S78: US WARN Act, 29 U.S.C. §§ 2101–2102](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/29/2101)

| | EU directive (S77) | US WARN Act (S78) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who is covered | Employers in member states, under each state’s national law | Employers with 100 or more employees excluding part-time employees, or 100 or more employees, part-time included, who together work at least 4,000 hours a week, not counting overtime |
| What counts | Dismissals for reasons not related to the individual worker; other terminations the employer initiates for such reasons count once there are at least five dismissals | An employment loss: a termination other than discharge for cause, voluntary departure or retirement; a layoff longer than six months; or hours cut by more than half in each month of a six-month period |
| Part-time employees | Not separately defined | Someone employed on average for fewer than 20 hours a week, or for fewer than 6 of the 12 months before notice is due. Left out of the 100-employee test and of the plant-closing and mass-layoff numbers, but counted in the alternative 4,000-hours test |
| Thresholds | Over 30 days: at least 10 dismissals where more than 20 and fewer than 100 are normally employed; at least 10% where 100 to 299; at least 30 where 300 or more. Or, as each state chooses, at least 20 over 90 days whatever the size | Plant closing: a shutdown of a site, or of facilities or operating units within it, causing 50 or more employment losses there in 30 days. Mass layoff: at one site in 30 days, at least 33% and at least 50 employees, or at least 500. Smaller groups within 90 days are added together unless they have separate and distinct causes |
| Process | Consult workers’ representatives in good time, aiming at agreement; notify the public authority in writing | Written notice to the employees’ representatives (or, if none, each affected employee), the state’s rapid-response body for laid-off workers and the local government |
| Waiting period | No earlier than 30 days after notification; the authority may be allowed to shorten it, or to extend it to 60 days or more | 60 days. Shorter only in narrow exceptions, with as much notice as practicable and a brief statement of why: business circumstances that were not reasonably foreseeable; a natural disaster; and, for the shutdown of a single site only, an employer actively seeking capital or business that would have avoided or postponed it, which reasonably and in good faith believed that giving notice would have prevented it obtaining them ([§ 2102(b)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/29/2102)). Seeking investment does not by itself shorten notice |

Eight dismissals in an establishment of forty sit below the directive’s thirty-day band for that size, which starts at ten, and below its ninety-day alternative of twenty; forty employees sit below the federal employer threshold. But a national law may set lower thresholds, individual dismissal law applies in any case, works-council rules apply wherever a **works council**, an elected body that represents employees in dealings with the employer, exists or the law requires one, and this book does not say which law governs Larkspur. Larkspur’s notice periods, severance formula and fixed-term retention above are its fictional contract terms, not a statutory minimum anywhere.

What the chapter does say is that the calendar in the cash table assumes individual notice with no collective procedure, and that Larkspur’s legal advisers confirm that assumption before the date is fixed. If the assumption is wrong, the timetable has to be rebuilt under the rules that apply: which steps must come first, and whether individual notice can run at the same time as consultation and any waiting period, or only after them. A reduction planned without that check has **a saving date nobody can rely on**.

## The People Who Remain

Thirty-two people remain at Larkspur once the retained specialist leaves, on 31 March in the base case, and the plan has to say what it expects of them. The failure to avoid is the silent one: **the work of eight people distributed over the rest by omission**, so that the reduction saves €72,000 on paper and costs the company its next four resignations.

So **the stopped list is published, not implied**. The remaining engineers are told, in writing, what is no longer expected: no mobile app, no support for its beta after 15 December, no country beyond the two signed customers, and on the core team only customer-promised changes and the maintenance the incidents demand.

The **on-call rota**, the schedule of engineers on standby for problems in the live service outside working hours, drew on ten engineers and now draws on eight. Each person carries one week in eight instead of one in ten, and every review from December onward looks at the incident count before anything else, because the evidence that would change the plan includes it. The executives’ pay deferral is announced with the plan, as a deferral, not as a sacrifice that others are asked to match. And the team is told what would restore the plan: cash received from the round, or a commitment the board authorizes counting under the restart rule, and what that would and would not reopen.

The people who remain draw conclusions of their own, and some leave; the reading list at the end includes the research on that. **A company cannot promise them stability it does not have.** It can tell them which plan is running, why, and what the next review will decide, and it can make sure that **the same dated cash table sits behind the board paper, the investor update and the team briefing**. A team that hears “the round is nearly done” in October and watches colleagues start leaving in November has learned something about its leaders that no later update repairs.

## Two Variations on the Same Decision

**A profitable company pursuing a higher margin.** In a **buyout**, where an investor has bought control of a company, the pressure may come from a profit-margin target, profit as a share of revenue, in the **value-creation plan**, the investor’s plan for making the company worth more. There is no cash date, and the reduction is proposed when nothing is late. The authority is the same board and the same reserved matters. What is missing is the latest useful date that cash supplies, so the discipline has to come from the comparison instead: the recurring saving against the revenue it puts at risk, the one-off cost against the first year’s savings, and the capacity removed against the work that will actually stop. The support-location consolidation in the chapter [[different-bets]] is that comparison, worked through to a staged decision; a margin target with no stopped list attached is the “cut engineering by 20%” of the chapter [[decide-who-decides]], **a number that is not yet a plan**.

**A consolidation after an acquisition.** When one company buys another and the two are combined, the price may have assumed savings from roles that exist in both. The reduction then has a date the deal set, and the danger is releasing the people who hold the acquired product’s knowledge before the receiving team has passed the transfer test in the chapter [[fix-decisions-before-hiring]]. The chapter [[acquisition-adds-work-first]] gives the completion test: **a duplicated cost is saved when it has actually ended**, and it ends when the merged system runs without the people who used to run the old one, not on the **synergy date**, the date the deal’s plan set for those savings. Rules on employees transferring with a business, and on consultation, vary by jurisdiction and can constrain which roles may change and when; take that advice before the synergy date is fixed, not after.

In every version, **continued ownership**, the same owners carrying on without the new money, is the scenario to plan for. At Larkspur, **R2 buys time; it does not end the problem**. Even after R2 the company spends about €78,000 a month more than customers pay: cash is €410,000 at the end of July and reaches the reserve in the first week of August. The bridge also remains a debt. If the round never closes there is nothing for it to convert into, so the €600,000 plus added interest falls due in November of the following year, twelve months after the money arrived, unless the investors extend it or agree other terms. The board therefore has to decide how Larkspur is financed after July, or what other outcome it pursues, such as a sale, well before August; the 1 April review puts that decision on the agenda.

The record above, including that cash horizon and the loan’s terms, is what Larkspur’s next board, its next investor or its buyer inherits. The chapter [[handover-of-obligations]] follows that record through whatever event comes next: who receives cash, who keeps an ownership stake, and how the obligations that survived the reduction, the two customer contracts, the frozen app, the deferred hires and the bridge loan, are handed over with the company rather than discovered afterwards.

## Questions to Consider

1. *Where does the reduction pressure on your company actually come from: a condition attached to money, a right someone holds under the agreements, or a benchmark somebody quoted? What response does each one require?*
2. *If you gave notice on the last day of this month, in which month would the full saving first appear, and what would the reserve look like in the months before it?*
3. *Which pieces of work would stop entirely, and has that list been written down for the people who remain, or will they discover it when they are asked to do it anyway?*
