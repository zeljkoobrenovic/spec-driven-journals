**A technology operating partner brings the investor's technical and operating judgment to your company.** Build the partnership so you get experience, contacts and a second opinion, and judge it by better decisions and capability your company keeps, not by activity.

Your investor's plan increasingly rests on software, data and **artificial intelligence (AI)**: serve twice as many customers, absorb an acquired business, launch an AI product. Someone must test what your systems and people can actually support before the investor commits, and follow the findings afterward, because checks made before investing go stale. That is the partner's job, and you are their main source of evidence. The sources, mostly executive-search firms, describe a widening remit, not a measured hiring rate.

The partner can help across the whole investment: checks before buying, a funded plan of work, product and engineering improvement, hiring, acquisitions, learning from other portfolio companies and preparation for a sale. Treat each piece of help as an agreement: who decides, how much of the partner's time you get, who pays and what evidence will show it worked.

**You and your fellow executives keep the continuing responsibilities.** The chief product officer owns product direction, the chief technology officer and engineering leaders own delivery, and the chief information officer owns the business systems. The partner adds assessment, challenge, support and only the authority an assignment grants. If the partner steps in as interim technology chief, insist that the reporting line, budget and end date are written down.

Hiring follows the same logic. **Define the capability gap with the partner before opening a search**: what your company must do but cannot yet do well. Developing existing people, coaching or temporary help may close it. When a hire is right, the partner can shape the role, introduce candidates and help assess them, while you settle who approves the appointment.

In a fictional example, an investment firm wants three of its software companies to adopt one AI support tool, which drafts replies to customers from past records for staff to check. The first company has reliable records, so it designs a pilot, a small, time-limited trial, with a budget and review date. The second first confirms which records it is allowed to use: being able to open a record is not permission to feed it to the tool. The third gets most of its complaints because its core service keeps failing, so it fixes reliability and defers the tool. Each decides through its own approval route, while the partner shares evaluation methods across all three.

At review, the first company and the partner look together at solved problems, waiting time, review effort and running cost. **Released hours are capacity, not cash.** Money is saved only when spending falls against an agreed comparison, after the tool's own costs. Even a successful pilot cannot isolate the partner's contribution from your team's work, so agree in advance not to claim more than the evidence supports.
