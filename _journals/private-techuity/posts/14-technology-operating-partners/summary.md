**A technology operating partner brings technical and operating judgment to an investment firm and the companies it owns.** Judge the partner by better decisions, funded improvements and capability your company keeps, not by activity.

Investment plans increasingly rest on software, data and **artificial intelligence (AI)**: serve twice as many customers, absorb a business the company has bought, launch an AI product. Someone must test what the company’s systems and people can actually support before the investor commits, and follow the findings afterward, because the checks made before investing go stale as people and products change. The sources, mostly executive-search firms, describe a widening remit, not a measured hiring rate or higher investment returns.

The work spans the whole investment: checks before buying, a funded plan of work, product and engineering improvement, hiring, acquisitions, learning across companies and preparation for a sale. Each contribution should leave something the company can see, such as a funded fix, a named leader or evidence of a result.

**Company leaders keep their continuing responsibilities.** The chief product officer owns product direction, the chief technology officer and engineering leaders own delivery, and the chief information officer owns the business systems. The partner adds assessment, challenge, support and only the authority an assignment explicitly grants. If the partner steps in as interim technology chief, record the reporting line, budget and end date, so advice does not quietly become a second management hierarchy.

Hiring follows the same logic. **Define the capability gap before opening a search**: what the company must do but cannot yet do well. Developing existing people, coaching or temporary help may close it. When a hire is right, the partner can shape the role, introduce candidates and help assess them, while the company decides who approves the appointment.

In a fictional example, an investment firm wants three of its software companies to adopt one AI support tool, which drafts replies to customers from past records for staff to check. The first company has reliable records, so it designs a pilot, a small, time-limited trial, with a budget and review date. The second first confirms which records it is allowed to use: being able to open a record is not permission to feed it to the tool. The third gets most of its complaints because its core service keeps failing, so it fixes reliability and defers the tool. Each decides through its own approval route, while the partner shares evaluation methods across all three.

At review, the first company looks at solved problems, waiting time, review effort and total running cost. **Released hours are capacity, not cash.** Money is saved only when spending falls against an agreed comparison, after the tool’s own costs: staff answering more questions on unchanged pay save nothing, while cutting paid overtime may. Even a successful pilot cannot isolate the partner’s contribution from the company team’s work.
