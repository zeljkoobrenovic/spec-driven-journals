**Judge an investor by what it did when a plan went wrong, not by what it promises.** Most product and engineering leaders never sign the terms, yet their plans depend on how the investor behaves after a missed quarter.

**Figure 1:** *Start from the company’s next decisions, compare investors on how they behaved under pressure, and spend only the money that has arrived.*

Start with the company: write down its next difficult decisions and what each needs. Larkspur, the book’s fictional software company, has three: a pilot to automate how new customers are set up, which needs someone who has done it before; a second country, which needs more money in about a year; and a founder’s retirement, which needs time. A brochure of support services shows what a firm says it offers, not whether that help will be available.

Then ask for **the difficult references**: people who worked with the investor when a plan failed, a disagreement flared or more money was needed. What did the investor do, and what happened next? Look for repeated patterns, not one hard story, and make any support offer concrete: who is available, for how many days, who pays, who decides.

In a fictional scenario, Ines, Larkspur’s chief executive, compares two offers this way. Investor A offers €5 million and a broad support platform. But a former finance chief reached independently says more money took four months after her company missed its plan, and A will not write down its criteria for further funding. Investor B offers €4.5 million and one operator who has run two onboarding automations. When one of its companies slipped a year, B provided short-term funding within six weeks, and in the same quarter replaced that company’s finance chief. B’s fund is in year seven of ten, so it will probably want to sell within about five years.

**The evidence did not rank the firms; it changed which uncertainty Larkspur would carry.** Ines chooses B, which agrees to write its next funding decision into the investment agreement: its fund must pay €1.5 million more within thirty days of a valid request, once the pilot brings setup down to an average of 65 hours or less without longer customer waits, and other agreed conditions hold. Even a signed right **is not cash**, so no second-country hire, lease or customer promise is made until the money arrives. B’s readiness to replace managers and its likely sale timing remain risks the board accepts.

The same test works **when the investor is already in place**. In a separate scenario, Alex, who leads technology, had a June hire resting on a co-investor who had not committed; he turns it into a decision at the July board meeting, once the co-investor commits or declines. Where evidence is missing, label the support unconfirmed, make no commitment that depends on it and set a review date.
