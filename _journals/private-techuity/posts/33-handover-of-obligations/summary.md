The morning after a company takes new investment or changes owners, its customer contracts, promised recovery time, half-finished system move and postponed hire are still there. The deal changed who holds the **shares**, units of ownership, not the work. **Hand over those obligations, not only the shares.**

**Keep the evidence during the work.** At each review, record the baseline (the starting measurement), the result and its uncertainty, the open work, and three money figures: **committed** (approved for named work), **incurred** (work received) and **paid** (cash sent). A project list assembled before a sale cannot replace that record.

**The worked example.** Larkspur, a fictional software company, spent about 80 staff hours setting up each new customer. Its board approved €180,000 to trial one reusable setup step. By day 100 of the plan, about €90,000 of work had been received; €90,000 was still to come. Finance checks the unrecorded payments before signing. Setup fell to 62 hours, not the 50 hoped for; poor customer data caused about 40% of the rest, so the board approved, but has not spent, €40,000 for a data-quality step. Upkeep costs €30,000 a year.

**Sort the open obligations into four kinds.** Binding obligations, contracts and legal duties, stay with the company whoever owns it; some are work owed, such as setting up signed customers, others money owed. Funded work is approved but not built. Accepted risks are known dangers someone authorized agreed to carry: Larkspur’s recovery test passed, but an outage of every rented data centre in one area is not covered and only two trained people can restore the service. Options awaiting evidence (a second country, two hires) go to a review around day 190 and wait if the next measured customer group is unfinished; the date approves nothing by itself.

**Follow who receives cash and who keeps ownership.** In a funding round the company sells new shares and is paid. When a shareholder sells existing shares, the seller is paid. Many companies belong to a fund, a pool of investors’ money run by a manager; when one fund investor sells its share of the pool to another, the fund remains the company’s shareholder. When the manager moves the company into a new fund it also runs, selling and buying at once, price and conflicts of interest need attention. [S02: Institutional Limited Partners Association principles](https://ilpa.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/06/ILPA-Principles-3.0_2019.pdf) An initial public offering, a first sale of shares to the public, can leave control with the founders. A deal announcement does not show investors’ final gains.

**Get the handover accepted.** At day 130, after new investment lowers her spending limit, Ines, the chief executive, signs for the record with Priya, who stays accountable for customer setup; the new investor’s director receives it for information. The €30,000 upkeep, not yet in an adopted budget, is the recorded exception. Later results are evidence to learn from, not a verdict on the previous owners.
