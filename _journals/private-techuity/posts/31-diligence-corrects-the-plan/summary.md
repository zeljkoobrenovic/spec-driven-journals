**Technical due diligence** is an investor’s investigation of a company’s product, systems, people and risks before investing. Its findings shape the price, the deal’s conditions and the company’s plan. Here it tests the **investment thesis**, why the investment should succeed: that fictional Larkspur, a scheduling-software company, can double **onboarding** (setting new customers up to use the software) without more implementation staff.

**When you have access sets your levers.** At **signing**, the parties commit to the deal. At **closing**, the money is paid and the **shares**, units of company ownership, change hands. Buying an owner’s existing shares pays that owner, not the company. Before signing, attach conditions to every commitment. After closing, your levers are the plan and its funding; any price change depends on the signed terms.

**The evidence.** Morgan, the investor’s technology adviser, recorded finding **D-3**: in three of five sampled implementations (of Larkspur’s last twelve, one country, six months), one specialist entered the customer’s settings by hand. Effort averaged about 80 staff hours across all five. Alex, the chief technology officer, reports that roughly 40% of the effort goes into correcting customer data; Morgan infers the dependency on one person is built into the work. Both can be true; their sizes are unknown: the time records hold only totals, and five samples may not represent other customers.

**The decision.** The investment proceeds, with a changed plan:

- **Growth:** the year-one volume assumption falls from 2× to 1.5× (half as many again), a forecast resting on pilot evidence. Expansion to a second country waits for that evidence.
- **Pilot:** a limited trial of one reusable setup step, costing €180,000 and 12 engineer-weeks (one engineer’s week of work each) to build, then €30,000 a year to maintain.
- **People:** two permanent hires (€300,000 a year) are deferred. A contract specialist serves customers for twelve months (about €150,000, from the running budget) while Larkspur’s specialist helps build the step.
- **Who decides:** the investment firm’s investment committee approves the deal terms; Larkspur’s board of directors approves the plan and its money. Priya, who leads product, is accountable; Alex’s disagreement is recorded.

**The review.** Day 0 is the board meeting after closing. By day 20, a **baseline**, the starting figure, covers all twelve implementations: totals from the time records, the activity split estimated from notes and staff checks. The pilot records it directly. Priya measures the **cohort**, the customers set up with the new step, at day 90; the board decides at day 100. Under 60 hours permits a request for the next stage, which needs approval and does not prove 1.5×; 60 to 70 hours funds the next fix with an estimated cost; above 70 reopens hiring or the target.

**Beyond D-3.** A seven-area coverage check shows what went unexamined; it is not a score, and missing evidence stays missing. Receiving a report is not acceptance: each accountable leader records agreement, disagreement or evidence still needed. D-3 then becomes a funded priority in [[first-hundred-days]].
