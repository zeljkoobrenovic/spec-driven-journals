**Visma**, a group of business-software companies, has kept the same investment firm, **Hg**, involved since 2006 while the funds and shareholders behind that name changed. A **fund** is money pooled from investors and used to buy **shares**, units of ownership in a company. The **investment manager** is the firm that runs the fund; one firm can run several funds with different investors, rights and holding periods; the fund, not the manager, owns the shares. A change of fund, even under the same manager, can change the money and timeline behind long-running work on systems shared by several products.

Hg’s 2017 announcement names two different Hg funds for its 2006 and 2014 investments, records KKR’s announced complete exit and Cinven’s sale of 40% of its own holding, both subject to regulatory approval, and Hg’s planned 41% of the shares after that buyout (a purchase of a controlling share). [S62: Hg 2017 Visma announcement](https://hgcapital.com/insights/hg-leads-usd5-3bn-buyout-of-visma) Visma’s December 2023 announcement describes a **secondary sale**: existing shareholders sold shares to other investors, so the money went to sellers, not the company. The deal values the business at €19 billion, with over €1 billion from about 20 new investors and about €3 billion from existing ones, and keeps Hg as **majority** holder (more than half the shares). [S30: Visma 2023 transaction](https://www.visma.com/newsroom/visma-attracts-new-investors-for-further-international-expansion-in-a-transaction-valuing-the-company-at-eur-19-billion-59703d9f) Neither document says who sold in 2023, which funds now hold Hg’s shares, or how much money, if any, reached Visma.

Visma describes local decision-making, shared infrastructure (technical foundations such as hosting and security), product investment and 33 acquisitions in 2024, nearly three a month on average. [S32: Visma annual-report announcement](https://www.visma.com/newsroom/visma-releases-annual-and-sustainability-reports-for-2024-7f5c9f37) The sources document no intervention with its cost and customer outcome; the model is a question, not a proven mechanism.

**The numbers need the same check.** **EBITDA** is profit before interest (borrowing costs), taxes, and depreciation and amortization (charges spreading asset costs over years of use). Visma’s 2024 annual report shows EBITDA of €892.646 million; its 2025 report adds back €11.665 million of “M&A expenses” (mergers and acquisitions: fees for carrying out deals, not the companies’ purchase prices), to reach **adjusted EBITDA** of €904.311 million for the same year (rounded: €893 million and €904 million). [S43: Visma 2024 annual report](https://cdn.prod.website-files.com/69787181d8720ea08c1f22fe/69787181d8720ea08c1f460b_Visma-Annual-Report-2024.pdf) [S44: Visma 2025 annual report, p. 96](https://cdn.prod.website-files.com/69787181d8720ea08c1f22fe/69bb9fc407e856cb8d6f7726_Visma%20Annual%20Report%202025.pdf) An **add-back** removes a recorded expense from the comparison on paper; no cash comes back; the later figure is a changed measure, not extra performance.

The adjustment cannot settle the budget. **Costs can be exceptional for one transaction while recurring across a continuing program.** A group that keeps acquiring must plan cash and engineer-weeks (one engineer for one week) for buying the next companies and integrating the last ones, know which earnings measure its lenders apply, and name who approves the next purchase.

After such a transaction, recheck: which funds or holding companies hold the shares and what they expect, without assuming one fund holds the majority; whether the shared-service budget and integration capacity (people and time to connect acquired systems and move their customers) are still funded, and by whom; and which earnings definition the board, lenders and operating plan each use. Independent customer, employee and acquired-company evidence is missing.
