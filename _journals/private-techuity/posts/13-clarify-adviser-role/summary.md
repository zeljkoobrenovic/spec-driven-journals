**An investor’s technology adviser can help you, assess you or, if nobody says otherwise, seem to decide for you.** Before you share anything sensitive, agree which job the adviser is doing, who they report to and what authority, if any, they hold.

Picture a planning meeting at Larkspur, a fictional software company. Morgan, the investment firm’s technology adviser, offers useful ideas. Are they suggestions, an assessment or instructions? The answer starts with the reporting line: Morgan works for the firm, so what Morgan learns can inform the investor’s decisions. The book’s example role, Technology Principal, comes from one supplied brief, not a position every investor has. [P01: supplied role brief](bibliography.html)

Four questions settle most of the ambiguity: who the adviser works for, what the assignment is, how much time they can give and which decisions, if any, they may make. Read the investment agreements too: they often already let the investor and its representatives inspect records and talk to senior executives.

Then name the job. Due diligence investigates before an investment decision, coaching develops a leader’s own judgment, operating support improves the company’s work, and formal oversight reviews results for the board or the investor. Employees should know what they are hearing: **an assessment finding, a recommendation or an authorized instruction**.

**Influence is not authority.** Closeness to the investor gives Morgan weight, but the right to decide comes only from an executive role, the board, rights agreed with the shareholders or an explicit assignment, and an adviser usually holds none of these. When engineers take priorities from Morgan, Alex, the chief technology officer, and the investment team at once, they face a second chain of command nobody authorized.

The hardest case is a change of role that nobody announces. For three months Morgan has coached Alex, who has been candid. Then the firm asks Morgan to judge in writing whether Larkspur’s technology leadership can carry an expansion into a second country. Their coaching agreement has one written rule: nothing Alex says in coaching is reported to the firm, and a concern about the company goes to Alex first, then to Ines, the chief executive, only if it remains unresolved.

So **two permissions stay separate**. Morgan may form a view from information the existing agreements already provide. A new assessment commissioned by the company, with interviews of Alex’s managers, needs Ines’s approval, or the board’s where it will decide. But only Alex can release his coaching conversations, because the rule protects him, although it cannot override a disclosure the law requires. If he declines, Morgan works from independent evidence and says so, or the firm appoints someone else. Ines then tells the managers in writing what Morgan is now doing.

Back in the meeting, the terms are clear: **Morgan recommends; Alex decides** the technical plan within the approved budget. The next chapter looks at the wider group at the firm whose job is practical help: [[technology-operating-partners]].
