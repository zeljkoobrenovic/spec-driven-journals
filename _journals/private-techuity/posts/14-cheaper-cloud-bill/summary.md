**Cloud services** are computing and storage rented from a supplier. A lower bill can mean efficiency, lost demand, worse service, or an obligation pushed into later years; the bill alone cannot say which. In the fictional Larkspur scenario, the 2026 plan approved by the **board** — the directors overseeing the company — assumes cloud spending stays flat against 2025 while customer transactions grow by half. The investor’s technology adviser asks why last quarter’s bill rose 20%. Two questions sit inside that one: did each completed transaction get cheaper, and was the plan met?

Pick a unit of **useful work** — for Larkspur, one appointment successfully scheduled — and read its cost against both the prior year and the plan. 2025: €100,000 a month for one million transactions, €0.10 each. 2026 plan: €100,000 for 1.5 million, €0.067. Actual, last quarter of 2026: €120,000 for 1.5 million, €0.08. **Cost per transaction improved 20%**, yet the plan was **missed by €20,000 a month**. An €80,000 bill for 500,000 transactions would mean €0.16 each: a cheaper bill hiding lost demand.

**Put every figure on the same basis.** Cash that left the account, cost assigned to the period (advance payments spread over the months covered), a bill cut by **credits** (temporary supplier discounts that cut the bill, not the computing used), and how much of a purchase promise was used are four different numbers. This summary uses the second.

**Separate usage, price and software design** — how much computing is used, what each unit costs, and how the software is built to do the work. The three savings do not simply add up. **FinOps** — Finance plus DevOps, the practice of building and running software as one team — manages technology spending jointly across engineering, finance and business, using **unit economics**: money spent per unit of useful work. [FinOps Foundation: unit economics](https://www.finops.org/framework/capabilities/unit-economics/)

A **commitment** — promising to pay a fixed amount for a fixed term — lowers the quoted price but can leave the company paying for capacity it no longer needs. Demand here means what usage would cost at **flexible prices**, paying only for what is used. All the contract figures below are assumptions for the example, not a supplier’s published prices.

**The purchase, and its downside.** For its scheduling service, Larkspur works to a board-approved 2027 demand range of €30,000–€60,000 a month. It picks a one-year commitment covering the first €30,000 of demand at **€21,000 a month, €252,000 in total**, with usage above €30,000 billed flexibly. That saves €9,000 a month (€30,000 − €21,000) across the range and loses €1,000 (€21,000 − €20,000) in the €20,000 **stress case** — deliberately harsher than the plan, used to test the decision rather than predict it.

**Payment timing, and what funds it.** The €21,000 is paid in advance on the first of each month. Usage above the €30,000 it covers is billed on the first working day of the following month and paid 30 days later — January’s extra usage is billed in February, paid in March. Cash held plus cautiously expected receipts cover both streams for 2027, plus a reserve for the last bill: expiry ends future minimum payments but not charges already incurred, so December 2027’s extra usage is billed in January 2028 and paid in February 2028. No *new* 2028 spending is approved; settling those 2027 charges is.

**Who approves it.** The contract cannot be cancelled early or transferred without the provider’s consent. The technology leader proposes, the finance leader checks timing and terms, the chief executive approves under standing permission for contracts of a year or less. The board has approved the 2027 demand forecast and the cash to meet it — but not yet a 2027 spending target, which comes in the autumn planning round.

Credits, minimum payments and change-of-owner terms change the bill, not the computing used. Check who legally signs, and whether the commitment survives a **carve-out** — selling or separating part of the company. Keep service quality as the final test: work completed, **latency** (how long a user waits), failed requests and recovery after failure.

Track the **opportunity** modelled, the **change implemented** and the **observed net effect** separately. Stretching €9,000 a month into €108,000 a year is a labelled estimate, not an observation. Larkspur’s answer has two time frames.

**What already happened (2026).** Unit cost improved, the plan was missed. The money is spent; nothing signed now changes it.

**What is expected next (2027).** From 1 January 2027, €9,000 a month less than buying the same usage flexibly, at every point in the range — a projection, not an observation. The first bill covering a full month under the new arrangement arrives in February 2027. That saving is constant; total spending is not. Holding the other €70,000 steady, Larkspur spends about €91,000, €111,000 or €121,000 a month at €30,000, €50,000 and €60,000 of scheduling demand. At €50,000 the gap against the €100,000 the 2026 plan allowed is about €11,000 a month — illustrative on those assumptions, and measured against the 2026 benchmark only because no 2027 target has been set yet.
