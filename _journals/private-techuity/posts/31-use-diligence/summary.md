**Diligence is your last good chance to correct the plan before you are held to it.** Treat it as a test of the deal’s assumptions, not an exam to pass.

Before an investor commits money, technical due diligence examines the company’s product, technology and team. Its findings shape the price, the funding, the conditions of the deal and the plan you will later deliver. How much you can influence depends on when you have access. Before signing, when the parties commit to the terms, a finding can still move the price, the terms or the plan. After closing, when the money is paid and the shares change hands, your levers are the plan and its funding.

Start with the **investment thesis**: why the investment should succeed. For Rotaline, a fictional scheduling-software company, the thesis says it can set up twice as many new customers a year without adding implementation staff. A demonstration cannot prove that; samples of real work can.

Morgan, the investor’s technology adviser, sampled five of Rotaline’s last twelve customer setups. In three of them, one specialist entered the customer’s settings by hand, and the five took about 80 staff hours each on average. Alex, the chief technology officer, says roughly 40% of that effort goes into correcting poor customer data. Morgan says the dependency on one person is built into the work. Both can be true; nobody yet knows how big each share is.

The team recorded this as **finding D-3**, keeping observation, claim, inference and the unknown apart, rather than writing “technical debt: high”. Then they priced the options: hire two specialists for about €300,000 a year, fund a €180,000 pilot of one reusable setup step, or narrow the target. The investment went ahead with a changed plan. The first-year growth assumption fell from 2× to 1.5×, expansion to a second country waited for evidence, the pilot became a funded condition and the permanent hires were deferred.

The investment committee approved the deal terms; Rotaline’s board approved the plan and its money. Priya, who leads product, is accountable, and Alex’s disagreement stays on the record. What comes next is fixed in advance. Priya measures the first customers set up with the new step at day 90, and the board decides at day 100: below 60 hours per setup, a request for the next stage may follow; between 60 and 70, the next priced fix is funded; above 70, hiring or the target is reopened.

One caution: a report received is not a report accepted. Each accountable leader records agreement, disagreement or evidence still needed, and **missing evidence stays missing** rather than being scored as neutral.

Once the deal closes, D-3 becomes a funded priority competing for scarce engineering time, and [[first-hundred-days]] takes it from there.
