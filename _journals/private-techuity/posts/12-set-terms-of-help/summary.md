**Agree the work before the helper’s first day.** An offer of help usually names a person but not the work; a short written charter turns it into an engagement with a result, resources, authority and an ending everyone can check.

Rotaline, the fictional software company this book follows, depends on one employee, its implementation specialist, to set up each new customer by hand: about 80 hours per customer, counting data cleaning, configuration, customer sessions and rework. Its investor offers an outside specialist from the firm’s own team for a few weeks. Accepted with nothing agreed, the visitor could spend two weeks interviewing the same engineers three times while nobody knew what would change or who was paying.

So Rotaline writes the charter first. **The result is one repeatable setup path** that a Rotaline engineer and the customer team can run without the implementation specialist. Priya, who leads product, directs the work and answers for it. A partner at the investment firm sponsors it and secures the specialist’s days. Alex, the technology chief, approves changes to the software; a change of scope needs Priya and the sponsor, and a change of budget goes to the board.

**External help consumes internal time**, and the charter says whose. Ten specialist days over six weeks, capped at €1,500 a day or €15,000 in total, come from the €180,000 already committed to the pilot. They need six days of a Rotaline engineer, two customer-team sessions and two hours a week of Priya’s time. The engineer’s days come from customer-portal research, which starts later rather than being quietly double-booked. The specialist starts on day 21.

By week three, live setups show that configuration takes less than half the hours; much of the rest goes on cleaning the customer’s records first, like a staff list in which forty people have no site recorded. **The charter already says who may redirect the work**: Priya proposes, the sponsor agrees and Alex confirms the effort. The specialist’s remaining five days move from reusable settings on the path to a check the customer team runs on a customer’s records before setup. No money moves, so the board is not asked.

At week six comes the handover test: the engineer and a customer-team member complete two setups with the specialist only watching. Hours per setup are falling, though customer waiting time has not yet changed, and freed hours are not cash saved. The engagement ends on schedule within its cap, and the data-quality finding goes to the day-100 board review.

There are three good endings: a capability the company can run, a better decision, or a continuing service kept on purpose. What no engagement should leave is a dependency nobody planned to fund.

A charter settles the work. The relationship with the person giving help, especially one who also reports to the investor, needs the same care: [[clarify-adviser-role]].
