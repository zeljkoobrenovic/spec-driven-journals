An investment announcement is not a budget. When a company is said to have received €100 million, some of that may have bought the founder's existing shares, some may have repaid old borrowing, and some may have paid the lawyers and bankers. Possibly none of it reached the company's bank account. **An ownership transaction can change what a company owes far more than what it can spend.**

**"The investor" is usually four entities, not one.** The investment firm has the name on the door and wants fees, a track record and a next fund. A fund is a specific pot of money with its own investors and an end date. A holding vehicle is a legal container that owns the company and often carries the debt. The portfolio company sells to customers and pays wages. Two consequences follow. "The firm has capital" says nothing about your company — a firm can raise a new fund while the fund that owns you is fully invested and near the end of its life. And the borrowing frequently sits in the holding vehicle above you, so your balance sheet can look calm while the structure above it is tightly financed.

Money reaches a fund through **commitments** and **capital calls**. A limited partner — a pension fund, insurer or endowment — promises capital and pays it when asked, not up front. Follow one LP with a €10 million commitment: it commits and no money moves; a first call requests €2 million; in year three the fund marks its companies up and the LP receives nothing at all; in year seven a sale finally distributes cash. **A valuation increase pays nobody.** That distinction runs through the whole book.

The **distribution waterfall** sets the order money is paid out. On €160 of proceeds from €100 contributed, the LPs get their €100 back first, then the €60 profit splits — 20% carry to the general partner, the rest to the LPs, who receive €148 in total. The investors' original money returns before anyone earns a share of the profit. Real agreements add a preferred return, a catch-up and a clawback. [S01: SEC investor guide](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/investment-products/private-investment-funds/private-equity)

Owning a company and running it are separate acts. A fund's investment committee deciding to buy you is not your board approving an operating plan; lenders hold rights neither can override. When someone says "the investor wants X," ask which body, exercising which right, and whether they can actually require it.

Three questions make this practical: which entity pays the invoice and who can commit its money; by what mechanism promised support would actually arrive; and who is receiving company data, under what authority. Common ownership answers none of them.
