**A lower cloud bill is not proof of improvement, and a higher one is not proof of waste.** Measure the cost of a unit of useful work, compare it with both last year and the plan, and size any commitment to the demand the company can actually fund.

Cloud services are computing and storage rented from a supplier. A falling bill may mean unused resources were removed, customers left, or the company promised to buy years in advance. The bill cannot say which.

Take Rotaline, the fictional company this book follows. Its board’s 2026 plan kept cloud spending flat at €100,000 a month while customer transactions grew by half. The investor’s technology adviser now asks why last quarter’s bill rose 20%. Two questions hide in that one: **did each unit of useful work get cheaper, and was the plan met?**

Rotaline’s unit is one appointment successfully scheduled. In 2025 it spent €100,000 a month for a million of them, €0.10 each. The plan allowed €100,000 for 1.5 million, about €0.067. Last quarter the volume arrived, but spending ran at €120,000: €0.08 each. Against 2025, **unit cost fell 20%**; against the plan, spending was €20,000 a month too high. Reporting only the first would dress a missed plan as success. A falling bill can mislead just as easily: €80,000 for 500,000 transactions would mean €0.16 each, a cheaper bill hiding lost demand.

Compare like with like: cash paid, cost assigned to the month, a bill cut by temporary supplier credits and use of a prepaid promise are four different numbers. Then separate the three reasons a bill moves: how much computing is used, the price per unit, and how the software is designed. Their savings do not simply add up.

The biggest lever is a **commitment**: promising to pay a fixed amount for a fixed term in return for a lower price. At flexible prices, Rotaline’s scheduling service would cost €30,000 to €60,000 a month in 2027, the range the board has forecast and funded; nothing is approved for 2028. A three-year commitment would save €15,000 a month at forecast demand, but cost more at the low end and bind two years nobody has funded. Rotaline instead signs a one-year commitment covering the first €30,000 of demand for €21,000 a month. It saves €9,000 a month across the range and costs €1,000 more only in a deliberately harsh €20,000 case. Ines, the chief executive, approves it under her permission to sign contracts of a year or less. It cannot be cancelled or transferred, which is why it runs one year only.

The €9,000 is a projection until the first full-month bill arrives in February 2027, and stretching it to €108,000 a year is a labelled estimate, not an observation. Service quality stays the final test: work completed, waiting time, failed requests and recovery after failure.
