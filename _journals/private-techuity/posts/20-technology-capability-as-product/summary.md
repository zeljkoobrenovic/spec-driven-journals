This optional appendix addresses readers designing technology-support services. **Productscapes** is the author’s proposal for designing reusable investment support. Its **Technology Value Creation Platform** means a service delivered through people, methods, decision records, specialist access and learning. Building software to administer it is a later decision that needs its own evidence.

Keep three levels distinct: the investment partnership, the portfolio-support offering, and the operating companies' products. The Principal can help develop the middle level while company leaders remain responsible for their businesses. Shared methods do not make every company a deployment of one centrally designed product.

The Productscapes material proposes six connected areas: Invest, Align, Accelerate, Learn, Scale, and Exit. [P02: Productscapes hypothesis](pt-bibliography.html) They are a proposed map of work, not proof that firms organize this way. Learning and exit evidence should run through ownership rather than becoming isolated late stages.

An **accelerator** is a reusable support package, such as a guided review of cloud costs. It should help someone reach a decision and a useful outcome. It needs a diagnostic, evidence of the current condition, options, actions, an owner, and a review. A benchmark belongs only where definitions and comparison groups justify it. A slide deck or tool list is not sufficient on its own.

Track use of the service, the company result and any supported claim about its financial contribution separately. Also track delivery cost and burden on company teams. Participation can be affected by owner influence; outcomes can be affected by market conditions and other initiatives; financial contribution requires a defensible bridge and finance review.

The chapter proposes starting with a small number of recurring decisions, testing the service with willing company teams, and defining conditions to expand, revise, or stop. This is an experiment design, not a validated optimum. Examples include onboarding constraints, cloud economics, and leadership assessment, selected only where there is a real need and access to usable evidence.

“Standardize the questions, not the answers” is the author's useful starting principle. It still needs boundaries: some controls and interfaces may require consistency, while product architecture and operating choices remain contextual. A better support product makes high-quality judgment easier. It does not manufacture uniformity or claim that the existence of a platform establishes value creation.
