# Editorial review: An AI Strategy Hides Three Investment Questions

Reviewed 14 September 2026. Read the full article, summary and reader-facing comic text; spot-checked the cited productivity research.

## Overall assessment

The chapter's strongest idea is to separate customer-product opportunity, internal productivity and competitive substitution before making an AI commitment. It serves leaders asked for a strategy or staffing claim before anyone has examined the workflow. Its treatment of review costs, data permissions, demonstrations and dated experimental evidence is careful and useful.

The article now contains a second substantial argument: AI needs an organization-wide operating model with functional goals and champions. That argument arrives late, is asserted more strongly than the evidence supports and competes with the three-question structure. The shortened formats no longer carry the same scope consistently.

## Detailed feedback

- **Introduction and tone:** The product/finance/CEO scene clearly explains three distinct concerns. “A valuation question dressed as a technology question” is memorable but becomes reductive when presented as the explanation for every investor request. “Almost every investor now” and “most companies make their first mistake” are unsupported prevalence claims. The cited company-adoption data cannot establish what nearly all investors think.
- **Organization and pacing:** The chapter promises three questions, but its headings move through customer work, data, three studies, a demonstration, experiments, saved time and only then competition. Use the three questions as the visible structure. The detailed coding-study comparison presently outweighs the competitive-threat analysis and the nonengineering workflows the ending says matter most.
- **Clarity and technical depth:** Measuring the complete task and separating saved time from expenditure are clear, though the latter repeats the roadmap chapter. The data-rights paragraph carefully separates technical access from permission. Defining AI “here” bounds the chosen scope appropriately; it need not become a general history of AI.
- **Evidence check:** The [Copilot paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.06590) supports the bounded JavaScript-task result, and [METR's original study](https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/) reports 19% longer completion times in its setting. [METR's February 2026 update](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/) supports the selection and concurrent-work measurement concerns. Preserve the dates and limits. The follow-up's statement that the missing participants/tasks likely benefit more is an inference, not a measured property of those excluded cases; attribute that judgment to the researchers.
- **Examples and missing payoff:** The demonstration-versus-launch distinction is strong. None of the three investment questions gets a completed local decision with costs, quality threshold and resulting action. The competitive section identifies useful questions but does not show how substitution evidence would change the roadmap or commercial model.
- **Conclusion and operating-model claim:** A named champion and a seat for every function is one proposed coordination model, not an established definition of AI strategy. The body acknowledges that a small company should start with two or three functions; the final “every function” test and the summary lose that proportionality. Shared platform, data, risk and commercial responsibilities also need clearer decision rights than “one function among several.”
- **Companion consistency:** The summary includes the new company-wide model, while all six comic panels remain focused on use cases, studies, data and demonstrations. The comic omits a major concluding recommendation. The summary has also grown substantially; check its visible prose against the promised 300–500-word format.

## Recommended changes

- **High impact — Make the three investment questions the headings.** Give each a decision, evidence required and one local example. Move the research comparison into the internal-work section as a compact evidence box.
- **High impact — Recast the operating model as a proposal and integrate it earlier.** Explain who coordinates the three questions, which functions participate in this company and which decisions are shared. Retain champions as an option with time and authority, not a requirement for every function.
- **High impact — Remove unsupported prevalence claims and align all formats.** Replace “almost every investor” with the specific investor expectation in the opening scenario. Restore the small-company qualification in the summary and add the coordination decision to the comic if it remains central.
- **Medium impact — Develop the competitive-threat example.** Show one customer task a substitute could perform, the switching constraint and the evidence that would justify defending, partnering, repricing or stopping a feature investment.
- **Low impact — Shorten repeated capacity-to-cash instruction.** Refer back to the roadmap chapter and keep the AI-specific review, error and task-selection complications.

## Proposed structure

The investor's three requests → product opportunity → internal workflow, with dated evidence → competitive substitution → proportionate company-wide coordination → three funded decisions and review triggers. If the operating-model material expands into implementation guidance, split it into a companion post linked to the organization chapter; do not append another full thesis to this one.
