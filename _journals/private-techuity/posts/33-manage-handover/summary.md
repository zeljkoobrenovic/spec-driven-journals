**A deal changes who owns the company, not the work.** The customer contracts, the promised recovery time and the postponed hire are still there the morning after, so hand over those obligations along with the shares.

**Figure 1:** *Keep the record during the work, sort what is still open, know who receives the cash, and get the handover signed.*

A handover is only as good as the record behind it. Keep the evidence while the work happens, so the next owner, investor or board can see what really changed. A project list assembled in a hurry before a sale is a story, and careful buyers trust stories less.

At Larkspur, the fictional company this book follows, setting up each new customer took about 80 staff hours, and the board committed €180,000 to a pilot of one reusable setup step. By day 100, about €90,000 of that work had been received and €90,000 was still to come; before anyone signs, Sam, who leads finance, checks how much has actually been paid. Effort fell to 62 hours, not the 50 the plan assumed, and about 40% of the remaining hours traced to poor customer data. So the board approved, but has not yet spent, €40,000 for a data-quality step. Upkeep will cost €30,000 a year. “Onboarding automation delivered” would be true and useless; this record shows what partly worked, and why.

Open items are not all alike, so **sort them into four kinds**. Binding obligations, such as the setup promised in signed customer contracts, stay with the company whoever owns it. Funded work, like the data-quality step, is approved but not yet built; the receiving side can stop it, but only as a recorded decision. Accepted risks are dangers someone authorized chose to carry: Larkspur’s restore test passed, but an outage of a whole cloud region is not covered. Options awaiting evidence, such as a second country and two hires, owe nothing yet; what travels is the decision date, around day 190.

It also pays to **follow who receives the cash**. In a funding round the company sells new shares and gets the money. When a shareholder sells existing shares, the seller gets it. When one investor in a fund sells its share of the fund to another, the fund stays the company’s shareholder and the company receives nothing. A deal announcement never shows what investors finally made.

Finally, get the handover accepted by a named person, not a distribution list. In the chapter’s illustration, a follow-on round closes at day 130 and lowers the spending limit of Ines, the chief executive. **Ines signs for the record with Priya**, who stays accountable for onboarding, while the new investor’s director receives it for information. The €30,000 upkeep is the one recorded exception, because the new board has not yet adopted the budget it belongs to.
