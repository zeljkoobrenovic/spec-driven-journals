> **IN THIS SECTION, YOU WILL:** Learn to lead when a company’s money or owners change: check the business before investing, fund the first plan, revise commitments when expected money is late, cut work and jobs only if the delay forces it, and hand over the evidence and unfinished promises.

<br>
Larkspur is the book’s fictional software company. Setting up a new customer is called **onboarding**. In a sample of five customer setups, three depended on one specialist entering the customer’s settings by hand. Part IV showed Larkspur asking its investor for help with that work. This part follows the finding through changes in funding and ownership, seen from the company’s side.

**Investors** put money into a company hoping for a gain, usually in return for part of its ownership, and the arrangement keeps changing. An investor may invest again in a new **funding round**, an occasion when the company raises new investment. It may sell part of its **holding**, the share of the company it owns. Or it may stay longer than planned. The company may **refinance**, replacing an old loan with a new one, or be bought by a larger company.

These events follow no fixed order, and how much each changes the leadership work depends on the arrangement. A round that leaves the investor with under half the company may leave management in place. A **change of control**, when someone new gains the power to direct major decisions, may reset who approves spending, the financing and the review dates at once. A **buyout**, a purchase giving the buyer control, and a **corporate acquisition**, one company buying another, are both changes of control.

The onboarding finding runs through the first, second and fifth chapters, as it did through the earlier chapters of Parts III and IV, where Larkspur ran a limited trial of a new setup step and then asked its investor for help. The third and fourth use a separate Larkspur scenario with its own cash, people and dates.

- [[diligence-corrects-the-plan]] explains **due diligence**, checking a business before an investment or purchase, and turns the finding into an agreed response.
- [[first-hundred-days]] funds the response with a named person responsible, approved money and team time. The **board**, the directors who approve major decisions, adopts the plan shortly after the investment completes, and a review follows a hundred days after that adoption.
- [[the-financing-slipped]] compares when money is expected, and how firm that expectation is, with when bills must be paid once a funding round, a refinancing or a budget from the company’s owner is late, and has the board choose a fallback in time.
- [[anatomy-of-a-layoff]] continues that scenario into the cut the delay forces: less spending, work stopped and jobs eliminated, comparing what each option costs or saves, and when.
- [[handover-of-obligations]] carries the record into a further round, a sale, a merger into a buyer’s business or continued ownership: who receives cash, who keeps a share, who can approve decisions and what unfinished work goes with the company.

Begin with the chapter [[diligence-corrects-the-plan]]. Part VI then tests these ways of thinking against historical company situations.
