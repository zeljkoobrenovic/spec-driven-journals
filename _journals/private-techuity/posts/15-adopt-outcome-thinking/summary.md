Delivering a setup feature does not by itself get a new customer working sooner. Before choosing work, Larkspur writes an **outcome record**: the change it wants for customers, the business result it should feed, who answers for each measure, and when each is reviewed. An **outcome** is a measurable change in what customers or the company can do; an **output** is something delivered. The **key performance indicators (KPIs)** in the title are the numbers chosen to track these outcomes, for customers and the business.

Larkspur is a fictional scheduling-software company; every figure is fictional. Ines is its chief executive; Priya leads **product**, the team that decides what the software should do; Alex leads technology, Sam finance. Morgan advises Larkspur’s **investor**, an investment fund that put money in expecting a return, and asks for a north star, the one measure a company steers by.

**One customer outcome.** After asking six customers, Priya writes it in their words: a new customer is scheduling real work within a few weeks of signing, without Larkspur’s specialist setting the account up by hand. Its metric is the **median** wait from contract to first real schedule, about ten weeks. The median is the middle wait when customers are lined up from fastest to slowest; with an even number of customers, it is the average of the two middle waits. The driver is staff hours per setup, about 80 in total, of which the specialist’s hand configuration is only part. Both figures were provisional when the investment completed. Within days, the **board**, the group that oversees the company, met and adopted the plan; that meeting is day 0, and project days count from it. On day 20, time records confirmed both figures.

**One business outcome.** Ines gets her executives and the investor’s director on the board to agree on **retention and expansion** of paying customers: keeping the customers Larkspur has, and their payments, and selling them cover for more technicians or locations. That a fast start makes them stay and grow is a hypothesis, tested at their first renewals.

**Two pyramids, joined.** One measure sits in both pyramids: the share of customers with a first schedule within eight weeks, read from the same dates as the median wait. It confirms the customer outcome and is an input to renewal. Staff hours per setup are the suspected cause of the wait, tested by the **pilot**, a small trial of the changed setup with a few customers. The hours are also a cost of serving customers, outside the retention pyramid.

**Who answers for what.** Product answers for the setup measures. Data readiness (records complete enough to load) is shared with the customer and the **customer success** team, which helps existing customers get value. Early use is counted from activation, the day the customer’s account is ready to use, which can be weeks after signing: real work scheduled in the first week after activation, and setup-related requests for help in the first month after it. Product shares early use with customer success and support. Product only influences renewal and expansion: customer success and the people who set prices answer for renewal, sales and customer success for expansion.

**The investor bridge, on an honest clock.** **Net revenue retention (NRR)** compares what last year’s customers pay now in repeat subscription payments with what they paid then. If they paid 100, then bought 20 more, cut 10 and left with 5, they now pay 105: an NRR of 105%. New customers and lower costs do not change it.

Each measure has a reading window: setup hours when each setup finishes, first-schedule weeks within three months of each signing, and the pilot customers’ renewals and NRR at their first anniversaries, a year after signing.

The director, whose fund plans to own the company about four years, cannot wait a year to know. The first **cohort**, eight customers followed together, reports setup hours and first-schedule weeks at day 90, ninety days after that board meeting. If both are still unchanged, the leadership reconsiders the two outcomes it chose, not just the plan.

**Balance is an allocation.** Customer value comes before what the company charges for it; overcharging shows as customers leaving, at Larkspur mostly at yearly renewals. Before ranking, the plan is split into new capabilities, improvements to the existing product, and running and maintaining it; an item enters if it moves a measure, protects an outcome or obligation, or tests an assumption.
