# Spec: Adopt Outcome Thinking: Balance Customer and Business KPIs

## Intent

Open Part IV with the practice that keeps a company building things that create real value for customers while connecting that value, explicitly and on an honest clock, to the business results and investor metrics the plan is held to. The chapter delivers an artifact rather than a culture: an **outcome record** with one key customer outcome in the customer's language, one key business outcome the leadership agrees on, the causal chain between them (drivers, leading and input metrics, lagging results), the investor metric each business outcome feeds and the horizon on which it moves, and the split between what product influences and what sales and customer success own. Every later chapter of Part IV uses that record: priorities are set against it, the revenue chain is tested through it, AI questions are sorted by it, capability is sized to it. The reader leaves able to answer an investor's "what is your north star", "why is product not judged on this quarter's revenue" and "which of our metrics does product actually move", and to say no to a loud request with the record rather than with a fight.

The chapter is a translation for leaders under investors of the author's product-management operating model ([[outcomes]], [[goals-and-questions]], [[lanes]], [[balanced-roadmap]], [[business-collaboration]]) and of the customer-and-business KPI pyramids of the Productscape domain model, written in the book's third-person, evidence-first voice rather than the journal's first person.

## Context

Larkspur, the book's fictional scheduling-software company, at the start of the shared chain that the chapters [[diligence-corrects-the-plan]], [[first-hundred-days]], [[cannot-fund-everything]], [[roadmap-to-revenue]] and [[handover-of-obligations]] follow (canonical facts in `_research/shared-scenario-record.md`, which the article must not contradict): setting up a new customer, called onboarding, takes about 80 hours of Larkspur staff time in total (the specialist's manual configuration is only part of it, but it holds the queue); in the week after closing that figure and the median wait of about ten weeks from signed contract to first real schedule are provisional (Morgan's diligence sample of five implementations, three of which needed the specialist's manual configuration), and the day-20 baseline reconstructed from the twelve implementations of the previous two quarters confirms both; the diligence finding D-3 says setup depends on one specialist; the initiative ONB-1 (€180,000, twelve engineer-weeks, €30,000 a year maintenance) targets the setup work; the day-90 cohort of eight came in at 62 hours; gate 2 at month six needs ≤ 50 hours and a median wait of eight weeks or less; capacity at €75 an hour is capacity, never cash, until a dated conversion; the investor's original ambition was a second country within twelve months and a third within two years, but diligence moved the second country out of year one and made it conditional on the pilot's evidence, so the plan approved at closing is 50% more customers in the first year with the same team and a second country only if the evidence supports it; the chapter must not carry the original country dates into the record as current commitments. Same people (Alex technology, Priya product, Ines the company, Sam finance, Morgan the investor's adviser). The chapter writes the outcome record those chapters later execute and measure; it introduces no new shared figures.

## Audience

Product and engineering leaders inside companies working under investors, including leaders who inherit an ownership arrangement. Assume no specialist finance or product-management background; explain necessary terms before use. Investor-side readers are secondary.

## Success criteria

- **Outcomes, not outputs, defined so a newcomer can apply it.** Define outcome (a measurable change in the customer's or the company's reality), output (what was shipped), key performance indicator (KPI), leading and lagging indicator, and vanity metric in plain words at first use. Give the test for a strong customer metric: it would still matter if the product changed completely, and customers confirm they would pay, renew or expand to get it. Show the contrast table "what this says / what it does not say" (shipping still matters; usage counts only when it connects to value; product is judged over quarters and years but is still accountable).
- **One customer outcome, one business outcome, agreed.** For Larkspur, name the key customer outcome in the customer's language (a new customer scheduling real work within weeks of signing, without Larkspur's specialist doing the setup by hand) with its metric (median weeks from contract to first real schedule; setup hours per customer as the driver) and the key business outcome the leadership agrees on (retention and expansion of paying customers, feeding the investor's second-country thesis), and show Ines getting the executive team and the investor director to agree on the one business result product optimizes for, so the roadmap is not a treaty negotiation.
- **Two pyramids and the chain between them.** Build the customer-outcome pyramid and the business-outcome pyramid with one explicit structure used in every format: the outcome at the top, the named contributing factors beneath it, and their measures beneath those, parallel branches at the same level. The business pyramid holds only what enters net revenue retention (renewal and expansion of the customers the company already has); cost to serve is shown feeding gross margin and new customers signed feeding annual recurring revenue, outside that pyramid. Connect the two pyramids at the one shared measure: the share of customers reaching a first real schedule within eight weeks, read from the same signing and first-schedule dates as the customer outcome's median and shown in the customer outcome node as well as in the renewal branch, a customer confirmation and a renewal input (the median, in weeks, and the share, a percentage of customers, stay distinct in every format, and the share is never drawn as a single customer's event); staff hours per setup are a customer driver that also feeds cost to serve and gross margin outside the retention pyramid, not a measure shared between the pyramids. Label the business pyramid's measures as a selection of operating indicators, not a complete breakdown of net revenue retention, and explain that the renewal rate counts customers while net revenue retention counts revenue (all customers renewing smaller subscriptions gives 100% renewal and a falling NRR). Describe the baseline hours and wait as measured quantities and the relationship between them as a working explanation still under test, in the figure captions and the TL;DR as well as the prose (the pilot tests whether lowering the hours brings the first schedule forward); label the setup-to-retention link as the working hypothesis with the test that would confirm or weaken it, presenting the fast-versus-slow renewal comparison as evidence to weigh with customer differences and group size, with an inconclusive reading allowed; and attach responsibility to specific measures, in the figures as well as the prose: which product is accountable for, which it influences, which are shared with the customer or the support team, and which sales, pricing and customer success are accountable for. Apply the removal test as a usefulness test (a measure whose removal changes what a team does next week earns its place; one that changes only a slide is decoration), separate from the test of whether the measure causes the outcome.
- **The investor bridge, on an honest clock.** Define annual recurring revenue (ARR), net revenue retention (NRR), gross margin and cash conversion in plain words, state which of them the business outcome feeds and when the effect can first be observed, each measure anchored to its own trigger and observation window rather than a single chronological axis (setup hours as each pilot customer's setup finishes; first-schedule weeks within a quarter of that customer's signing; month-one support requests a month after that customer's activation; gross margin on the ordinary quarterly accounts; renewal rate and the pilot cohort's NRR at the same first-anniversary review, about a year after each signing; company-wide NRR later, as pilot customers become a larger share of the base), with numbered project days (day 20, day 90) stated to count from day 0, the board meeting held within days after closing that adopted the operating plan (as in [[first-hundred-days]] and the shared scenario record; closing is not day 0 and the gap is not assumed to be zero), signing, setup start, activation and go-live kept as distinct customer dates that need not coincide, quarter, activation and going live defined, labelled as the scenario's expected observation schedule, and show the negotiation with the investor about grading product on its natural horizon while still reporting the lagging indicators when they arrive. Relate the metrics to what Parts I and II already defined (revenue, profit, cash, the valuation multiple, the thesis) rather than introducing a parallel vocabulary, but explain revenue, recurring revenue, subscription, gross margin (as an amount and as a share of revenue) and annual recurring revenue at or before their first substantive use in this chapter; where EBITDA is named, expand it and say in plain words that it is a profit measure, not cash, or leave it out. In every format the per-measure windows are labelled as expected observation windows (setup hours when the setup finishes; first-schedule evidence within three months of signing), not as delivery dates.
- **Balance as an allocation.** Show customer value before value capture (charging more than the delivered value shows up as complaints, customers leaving and lost trust, on a timing set by customer choices and contract terms; at Larkspur, where subscriptions renew yearly, mostly at the anniversaries), and show the roadmap as an allocation across innovation, iteration and operation decided before ranking, with the outcome record deciding what enters each slice through three admission routes (an item moves a measure in either pyramid, protects an outcome or meets an obligation, or tests an assumption the record depends on; mandatory obligations and operational safeguards such as the recovery work enter the operation slice by the second route, with their own record); hand the ranking and the cash and capacity limits to [[cannot-fund-everything]] without repeating it.
- **Outcome-centricity has a price list.** State, with Cutler, that it is a causal model plus habits, not a mindset: a few durable outcome lanes per team with a couple of metrics and a weekly rhythm; goals mixed across learning, shipping and outcome types and kept as a habit; powerful ideas imperfectly measured preferred to perfect measures of weak ideas, with the measurement bar improving over time rather than filtering for small ideas. Name the anti-patterns (feature factory, vanity dashboard, afterthought metric, measurement veto, grading product on the sales clock, deckware pyramid).
- **The recorded outcome record.** End with Larkspur's record: customer outcome and metric, business outcome and metric, the pyramids' top three levels, the investor metric and horizon, what product does not own, the lanes and their input metrics for the next two quarters, who agreed (Ines, Priya, Alex, Sam, the investor director) and the review cadence; and say which later chapters test each part of it (priorities in [[cannot-fund-everything]], the chain in [[roadmap-to-revenue]], the gates in [[first-hundred-days]]).
- **Investor questions answered as they are asked.** "What is your north star", "why is product not judged on this quarter's ARR", and "which of our metrics does product actually move", each pointing at the record; the last answer keeps direct influence, indirect influence and accountability apart in every format (product can affect renewal through the customer's experience but is not solely accountable for renewal decisions; sales is accountable for new customers signed, which the setup experience and available capabilities can affect indirectly, and the record does not attribute this quarter's signings to the setup work; never say product moves them "not at all").
- **House rules.** Opening blocks and key points state the practical problem (delivering a setup feature does not by itself get a customer working sooner) before any framework vocabulary; specialist terms (investor and investment fund, closing, diligence, thesis, bookings, revenue, recurring revenue, retention, expansion in both senses, churn, margin, holding period, median, cohort, pilot, onboarding, customer success, lanes, innovation/iteration/operation) explained in ordinary words at first meaningful use in each format, including the TL;DR and the comic, with secondary jargon replaced by ordinary wording in the short formats ("when the investment completed", "customers' repeat subscription payments", "new capabilities, improvements and maintenance"); each paragraph and comic caption with one identifiable purpose (company and cast separate from the investment-plan history; competing requests, agreed outcome and reason for choosing it as separate paragraphs; long captions as short sentences); no unsupported prevalence figures or general timetables; the company leader's decision, authority and funding assumptions explicit; fictional scenarios labelled and consistent with the shared ledger; a meaningful counterargument (an investor with a short holding period can reasonably want lagging results sooner; the answer is a dated plan with leading indicators reported now, not a refusal) with the evidence that would change the judgment; hand-off to [[cannot-fund-everything]]; TL;DR and comic consistent with the article. The short formats say three months rather than quarter, introduce the pilot as a small trial, explain data readiness (records complete and consistent enough to load) and data days (days from signing until those records arrive), define revenue beside its first comic use, and replace vision bet with an explained plain phrase. They also give each early-use measure its activity, interval and starting event (week-one use: scheduling real work in the first week after the customer's account is activated, the day it becomes ready to use, which need not be the signing date; month-one requests: setup-related requests for help in the first month from that same activation date), state the median's even-group rule at its first definition (the average of the two middle waits), and define gross margin as a share of revenue with gross profit as the amount (revenue 100 and direct costs 60 give gross profit 40 and gross margin 40%). Long explanatory paragraphs are split so that each answers one question; comic captions are short paragraphs (definitions; evidence and timing; responsibility or decision) and do not repeat what the artwork and transcript already show. Comic responsibility boards keep the article's assignments exact: data readiness (product, the customer, customer success), early use and month-one requests (product, customer success, support), renewal rate (customer success, pricing), expansion (sales, customer success), new customers (sales). The retention reference is described as a way to judge a proposed target; Larkspur's 105% is an illustrative calculation, not a target it has set.

## Non-goals

- Not a product-management textbook, a discovery or research method, or an OKR manual; the chapter delivers the outcome record and the investor bridge, and links the journal records and sources for the rest.
- Not the prioritization arithmetic (that is [[cannot-fund-everything]]), not the pilot measurement and its six kinds of benefit (that is [[roadmap-to-revenue]]), not team topology or coaching.
- Not a claim that outcome practice guarantees results; no invented study results, no prevalence claims about what companies "usually" measure, no new figures in the shared ledger.
