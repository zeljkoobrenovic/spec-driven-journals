> **IN THIS SECTION, YOU WILL:** Learn to write an outcome record: one key customer outcome in the customer’s own words, one key business outcome the leadership agrees on, the chain of drivers and indicators that connects them, the investor metric each business outcome feeds and the horizon on which it moves, and which results product owns and which it does not. Then use it to answer the investor’s questions and to say no with a record rather than a fight.

> **WHY INVESTORS CARE:** An investor funds a thesis and reads it back in a handful of metrics. It wants to know which of those metrics the product work actually moves, when the movement will show, and why a team that shipped everything on its roadmap can still have moved nothing that matters.

> **WHY YOU SHOULD CARE:** A roadmap without an agreed outcome pair becomes a treaty negotiation between whoever asked loudest, and product gets graded on this quarter’s sales. The record is what lets you build what creates value for customers and show, on an honest clock, what it does for the business.


> **KEY POINTS:**
>
> * **Judge work by outcomes, not outputs.** An outcome is a measurable change in the customer’s or the company’s reality; a feature shipped is a cost until one moves. A strong customer metric would still matter if the product changed completely.
> * **Pair one customer outcome with one business outcome, and connect them.** Two pyramids, drivers and leading indicators under each, joined where they reinforce each other, with the business nodes product influences marked apart from those sales and customer success own.
> * **Bridge to the investor’s metrics on an honest clock.** Say which metric the business outcome feeds, recurring revenue, retention, margin or cash, and on what horizon; report the leading indicators now and the lagging ones when they arrive, and refuse to grade a long instrument on a short scale.

Larkspur is the fictional scheduling-software company this book follows; it sells software that plans maintenance work for businesses whose staff work in the field. Alex leads technology as chief technology officer (CTO), Priya leads product, Ines is the chief executive who leads the company, Sam leads finance, and Morgan is the technology adviser to Larkspur’s investor. It is the week after **closing**, the day the investment was completed. The investor’s growth plan assumes many more customers without staff growing at the same rate, a second country within twelve months and a third within two years. Priya has a list: a customer portal, simpler setup for new customers, the recovery work Alex wants. The chapter [[cannot-fund-everything]] will choose among them against the cash and the team time the board approves. Before it can, someone has to say what the work is *for*.

Morgan asks the question in the form investors use: “What is your north star?” It is a fair question and a trap at once. Answered with a feature list, it tells the investor nothing; answered with a single number, it tells the team nothing. This chapter answers it with an **outcome record**: one customer outcome, one business outcome, the chain between them, and the investor metric at the end of the chain with the clock it runs on. ## Outcomes, Not Outputs

An **output** is what the company shipped: a feature, a release, a redesigned screen. An **outcome** is a measurable change in the customer’s or the company’s reality: a customer who can do something she could not, a cost that fell, a renewal that happened. A **key performance indicator (KPI)** is a number chosen to track an outcome. A **leading indicator** moves first and can be influenced this quarter; a **lagging indicator** confirms later that the outcome happened. A **vanity metric** is a number that rises for reasons unrelated to customer success: logins, clicks, pages viewed, features used.

The test for a strong customer metric is simple to state and hard to pass: it would still matter if the product changed completely, and customers confirm they would pay, renew or expand to get it. If a redesign would invalidate the metric, the metric was measuring the product’s mechanics, not the customer’s success. Larkspur’s customers do not want a setup wizard; they want to be scheduling real work soon after they signed, without waiting on anyone. That sentence survives any redesign. “Wizard completion rate” does not.

| What outcome thinking says | What it does **not** say |
| --- | --- |
| Every piece of work names, before it starts, the outcome it moves | No work starts until the metrics are perfect |
| A feature shipped is a cost until an outcome moves | Shipping does not matter; shipping is how outcomes move, it is just never the finish line |
| Outcomes are validated with customers | Customers dictate the roadmap item by item |
| Usage counts only when it clearly connects to value | Usage data is useless |
| Product is judged over quarters and years | Product is never accountable; the lagging indicators still arrive and are still reported |
| Work that moves no outcome is stopped | Every experiment must succeed; a test that disproves a driver moved the model too |

The author’s own operating model for this, written for product leaders rather than for leaders under investors, is the record [[outcomes]] in the companion journal; this chapter is its translation into the situation the rest of the book describes.

## One Customer Outcome, One Business Outcome

**The customer outcome first.** Priya writes it in the customer’s language, after asking six of them: *a new customer is scheduling real work within a few weeks of signing, without Larkspur’s specialist setting the account up by hand.* Its metric is the **median weeks from signed contract to the first real schedule**, about ten weeks on the twelve implementations of the last two quarters, and its main driver is **setup hours per customer**, about 80 on the same baseline, most of them one specialist’s. Customers confirmed the first half of the test: three of the six said the wait had delayed their own rollout, and two said they had considered a competitor for that reason. That is validation of the outcome, not of any particular feature.

**Then the business outcome, and only one.** Ines asks her executive team and the investor-appointed director to agree on the one business result product optimizes for. Sales wants new bookings; finance wants margin; the investor’s thesis says growth in customer count without proportional staff. They agree on **retention and expansion of paying customers**: a customer who starts fast stays and grows, and the second country is sold to customers who start fast. The other results are not abandoned; they belong to other functions and to later chapters. What the agreement removes is the treaty negotiation in which every function carries its own definition of success and the roadmap is whatever survives the argument.

Why only one of each? Because a record with three customer outcomes and four business outcomes lets every request find a home and decides nothing. One pair is what a team can prioritize with, defend in front of a loud customer, and recite without the slide.

## Two Pyramids and the Chain Between Them

A key outcome on a slide changes nothing. Broken into its drivers, the leading indicators that influence them and the lagging indicators that confirm them, the same outcome tells a team what to improve next and lets the leader trace every roadmap item to the metric it claims to move. Larkspur builds two pyramids, one per outcome, and connects them where they reinforce each other. Each node below the top has at least two children; a node with one child is a line, not a model.

**Customer-outcome pyramid.** Top: median weeks from contract to first real schedule.

- **Setup effort.** Setup hours per customer (leading; about 80). Share of setups needing the specialist’s manual work (leading; three of five in the diligence sample).
- **Customer readiness.** Share of customer data arriving clean enough to load (leading; the largest remaining share of setup hours is data cleaning). Days from contract to the customer returning its data (leading).
- **Early use.** Share of customers scheduling real work in week one after go-live (lagging). Setup-related support tickets in the first month (lagging).

**Business-outcome pyramid.** Top: net revenue retention of paying customers, defined below.

- **Renewal.** Renewal rate at contract anniversary (lagging). Share of customers who reached first real schedule within eight weeks (leading; the join to the customer pyramid).
- **Expansion.** Customers adding technicians or sites within a year (lagging). Second-country customers signed and live (lagging, the thesis test).
- **Cost to serve.** Specialist hours per new customer (leading; the same driver, read as cost). Support tickets per customer in month one (leading).

The two pyramids share nodes on purpose: setup hours are a customer driver and a cost driver; first-schedule-within-eight-weeks is a customer confirmation and a renewal input. That shared structure is the **causal model** the investor is really asking for when it asks for a north star: the near-term things the team can influence, linked to the long-term things that propel the company, with honesty about which links are measured and which are conviction.

**Mark what product does not own.** Renewal depends on the customer success team’s work and on pricing; second-country signings depend on sales; expansion depends on both. Product influences the leading nodes directly and the lagging ones through them. The record says so, because a product leader who claims the renewal rate will be blamed for it, and one who disclaims it will be ignored.

**The removal test.** Take any node out. If next week’s work changes, the node is part of a causal model. If only a slide changes, it is decoration. Larkspur’s “share of customers scheduling in week one” fails the test at first: nobody would do anything differently if it vanished. Priya keeps it only after tying it to a specific action, a check-in call in week one for customers below it, and the node earns its place. Half of most pyramids fail this test, and the halving is the point.

## The Investor Bridge, on an Honest Clock

The investor reads Larkspur through a few metrics, and the outcome record has to end in them or the investor will translate it alone. Four are named here in plain words; they are the ones a company selling software by subscription is judged on, and they sit alongside the revenue, **EBITDA** (earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortization, the profit measure the chapter [[obligations-before-budget]] explains), cash and valuation multiple that Parts I and II already defined.

- **Annual recurring revenue (ARR)** is the yearly value of the subscriptions customers currently pay for, counted as if every one renewed unchanged. It is a stock, not a cash flow, and it moves when customers sign, leave, grow or shrink.
- **Net revenue retention (NRR)** is this year’s recurring revenue from the customers the company had a year ago, divided by what those same customers paid a year ago, after their renewals, expansions and departures. Above 100% means the existing customers grew more than they left. It is the business outcome Larkspur agreed on, expressed the way the investor will read it.
- **Gross margin** is revenue less the direct cost of serving customers, hosting, support and setup included, as a share of revenue. Specialist hours per new customer sit inside it, which is why the same driver appears in both pyramids.
- **Cash conversion** is how much of the profit becomes cash in the bank, after the timing of customer payments and the company’s own bills; the chapter [[obligations-before-budget]] follows the money from earnings to cash.

Now the clock. Sales closes this quarter’s bookings; customer success defends this year’s renewals; product creates value that shows up in retention over quarters and in expansion over years. The setup work funded now will show in first-schedule weeks within a quarter, in month-one tickets within two, in renewals at the anniversaries that fall a year from now, and in NRR after that. Grading product on this quarter’s ARR produces feature trading and no compounding value.

Ines takes this to the investor director as a negotiation, not a principle. The proposal: product is reported on its leading indicators now, every quarter, on the record’s definitions; the lagging indicators are reported when they arrive, at the anniversaries, and product is judged on them then. The director’s reply is the counterargument the chapter owes: an investor with a holding period of about four years cannot wait two of them to know whether the thesis holds. The answer is not to refuse the clock but to date it. The first cohort of eight customers goes through the new setup within ninety days; if their hours and their first-schedule weeks do not move, the thesis is in doubt long before any renewal, and the chapter [[first-hundred-days]] sets the review that decides it. The evidence that would change the judgment is written into the record: leading indicators flat at day 90 reopen the outcome pair, not just the roadmap.

## Balance Is an Allocation

Customer value and business value are a sequence, not a trade-off. Customer value creates the opportunity for business value; capturing more than the company delivers is **over-monetization**, and it announces itself as churn, complaints and declining trust, usually a year after the pricing change that caused it. So the record puts customer value first in time and business value first in the investor conversation, and the pyramids are what keep the two honest with each other.

Balance then has to become a budget, or it stays a slogan. Larkspur classifies every roadmap item as **innovation** (work that advances the vision; the second-country capability), **iteration** (improvements to the existing product for customers or the business; the setup work) or **operation** (work needed to run, secure and maintain it; the recovery work). It decides the split between the three before ranking anything, and compares items only within their category, so a vision bet never loses head-to-head to a bug fix and a loud customer’s request competes for the iteration slice rather than for the whole roadmap. The outcome record decides what enters each slice: an item that moves no node in either pyramid does not enter at all. The ranking inside the slices, against the cash and the team time the board approves, is the chapter [[cannot-fund-everything]]. The author’s record [[balanced-roadmap]] gives the allocation method in full.

## What Outcome Thinking Costs

“Be outcome-oriented” proclaimed as a mindset leaves teams feeling inadequate and changes nothing. Outcome-centricity has a price list, and John Cutler’s is the honest one: a causal model, and the habits that keep it alive. [TBM 435: 20 Unfiltered Operating Takes](https://cutlefish.substack.com/p/tbm-435-20-unfiltered-operating-takes) Larkspur pays it in three ways.

- **Lanes, not pyramids, are where the work happens.** Each team carries a few durable, outcome-oriented lanes, each with a couple of metrics and a directional sense of the opportunity, stable for a quarter or two, revisited on a rhythm and changed when they stop making sense. The onboarding team’s two lanes for the next two quarters are *setup hours and manual share* and *customer data readiness*. The pyramids are the reporting view that connects the lanes to the investor’s metrics; nobody works inside a pyramid. The author’s record [[lanes]] argues this at length, including why the North Star Framework is a teaching tool rather than an operating system. [The North Star Playbook](https://amplitude.com/north-star)
- **Goals are mixed and habitual.** Learning goals next to shipping goals next to outcome goals; goals on leading inputs and on lagging results; set, revisited and retired on a cadence. The habit is the asset; any single goal is expendable. The failure modes are twins: goal motions, where the numbers mean nothing, and the afterthought metric, bolted onto a wish list to satisfy the format.
- **Powerful ideas imperfectly measured beat perfect measures of weak ideas.** A measurement bar that only cheap, already-instrumented ideas can clear is a filter that selects for weakness. Larkspur holds the ambition fixed, a customer scheduling within weeks rather than ten, and lets measurement fidelity improve: the first cohort is measured by hand from time records, and the instrumentation follows. The author’s record [[goals-and-questions]] is the fuller argument.

The anti-patterns are the record read backwards: the **feature factory** (long release notes, no outcome anyone can name); the **vanity dashboard** (engagement up while churn climbs); the **afterthought metric**; the **measurement veto** (a strong idea killed for an imperfect metric while a weak one proceeds on its dashboard); **grading product on the sales clock**; and the **deckware pyramid**, built to align everyone, admired once, consulted by nobody who does work.

One more cost belongs to the leader rather than the team. The record only holds if the executives around product treat it as a partnership rather than a ticket queue: sales bringing commitments into the intake process instead of onto the roadmap, finance reading capacity as well as cash, the investor director hearing the horizon argument as a plan rather than an excuse. The author’s record [[business-collaboration]] treats that relationship; here it is enough to say that the record is written with those people in the room, not presented to them afterwards.

## What to Say When the Investor Asks

**“What is your north star?”** A pair, not a number: customers scheduling real work within a few weeks of signing, and net revenue retention. The chain between them is written down, the leading indicators are reported quarterly, and the first cohort reports at day 90.

**“Why is product not judged on this quarter’s ARR?”** Because the work that moves ARR durably shows first in setup hours and first-schedule weeks, then in month-one tickets, then in renewals a year out. Product is judged on the leading indicators now and on the lagging ones when they arrive, and the dates are in the record. Judging it on this quarter’s bookings would buy feature trading.

**“Which of our metrics does product actually move?”** Directly, the leading nodes: setup hours, manual share, data readiness, first-schedule weeks, month-one tickets. Through them, gross margin and retention. Not directly: renewal, which customer success and pricing own, and second-country signings, which sales owns. The record marks the boundary so nobody claims or disclaims the wrong thing.

With the record agreed, Larkspur can choose. Three requests, one envelope of cash and engineer-weeks, and the record saying which nodes each request moves: that is where the chapter [[cannot-fund-everything]] begins.

## Questions to Consider

1. *Write your key customer outcome in a customer’s words, with its metric. Would the metric still matter if your product changed completely, and have customers confirmed they would pay or renew to get it?*
2. *Which one business outcome has your leadership agreed that product optimizes for, and which investor metric does it feed, on what horizon?*
3. *Take any node out of your pyramid. Does next week’s work change, or only a slide?*
4. *Which of the metrics your investor reads does product move directly, which only through others, and which not at all? Is that boundary written down?*
