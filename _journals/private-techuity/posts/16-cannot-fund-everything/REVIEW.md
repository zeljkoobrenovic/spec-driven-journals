## Current assessment

The chapter now earns its place at the start of Part III. The €500,000 and 24-engineer-week limits turn a familiar prioritization lesson into a decision, and the day-45 review shows that an allocation can change when evidence changes. The distinction between an already funded operating baseline and additional change work is much clearer. ## Active recommendations

1. **High impact — reconcile the shared recovery expenditure.** In the day-45 review, the original €80,000 and four engineer-weeks have already been spent; another €20,000 and two weeks fund the corrective work. [Prove You Can Restore](../27-prove-you-can-restore/index.md) presents the €80,000/four-week allocation as the corrective response to that same failed test. Because the chapters identify the same allocation and recovery work, this is not adequately explained by the guide's allowance for separate Larkspur scenarios. Carry one sequence through the recovery chapter, first-hundred-days plan and toolkit: original authorization, amount spent at failure, incremental authorization, total and remaining reserve.

2. **Medium impact — correct “The option is only worth what it costs to exercise.”** This sentence in “To Probe Further” confuses an option's value with its exercise cost. Flexibility has value because it preserves a potentially beneficial future choice; the cost of exercising that choice is one input. The distinction is explicit in [Damodaran's treatment of real options](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/pdfiles/papers/realopt.pdf). A useful replacement is: “Keeping the option open has a cost; its value depends on the future choices it preserves and the cost of taking them.” Retain the practical question about whether the later stage can actually be funded.

3. **Medium impact — explain why this reserve draw returns to the board.** Inès initially decides the allocation within the board's envelope. At day 45 she takes the revision back because it changes what the envelope funds. State which initial delegation excludes this change, or describe this as reporting a decision within her authority. For example: “The board had reserved approval of recovery overruns, so Inès seeks permission to move €20,000 and two weeks from the reserve.” Otherwise the new example weakens the collection's distinction between a budget, a decision and a consent requirement.

4. **Low impact — shorten the explanation after the worked choice.** The selected and rejected rows already demonstrate how a growth condition affects allocation. Some ensuing abstract restatements slow the transition to the failed test. This is a pacing preference, not an essential correction. Keep the concrete closing questions and the warning that the next failure could delay the onboarding cohort.

## Verification and formats

The allocation arithmetic is sound: all requests total €500,000 and 30 weeks; the chosen work totals €280,000 and 18 weeks, leaving €220,000 and six weeks. The additional recovery work moves those figures to €300,000/20 and €200,000/four. These are fictional allocations, not externally evidenced costs. The summary and comic text convey the scarcity lesson; their recovery references should follow the reconciled sequence. This review does not certify the existing artwork against revised dialogue.
