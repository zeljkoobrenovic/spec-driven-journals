# Spec: Management 101

> Working doc for the post in this folder. The spec drives the post; the post
> is the artifact.

## Intent

State the two-sided manager–report contract I operate by: what I owe every
report as their manager, and what I ask every report to own themselves. The
post turns the "Management 101" chapter of Camille Fournier's *The Manager's
Path* into an operating principle: a manager owes regular 1:1s that are
discussions rather than status readings, prompt private criticism and public
praise, and active advocacy for growth — and a report owes their manager an
agenda, their honest state, and explicit asks, because a manager is a
resource, not a mind reader. The load-bearing idea: being managed well is a
skill, and I hold both sides of the relationship to their half of the
contract.

## Audience

Every report in my organization (so they know what to expect from any
manager here, including me, and what I expect them to own); managers I hold
accountable to the manager side of the contract; peer executives comparing
operating models. First-person declarative.

## Success criteria

- [x] **Principle is quotable** — highlight states the two-sided contract:
      what I owe every report, what I ask every report to own, and that a
      manager is a resource, not a mind reader.
- [x] **The manager side survives** — regular discussion-shaped 1:1s, human
      connection, regular feedback (praise public, criticism private and
      prompt), growth beyond day-to-day execution, promotion clarity, stretch
      opportunities, advocacy, and focus.
- [x] **The report side survives** — think about what you want, bring the
      agenda, ask for help, feedback, opportunities, and promotion clarity,
      speak up when unhappy, own your growth and boundaries.
- [x] **The working-with-a-manager guidance survives** — bring solutions not
      only problems, ask for advice, give grace, control your own side, and
      diagnose whether a problem is the manager, the situation, or you.
- [x] **Credit is explicit** — References name Camille Fournier's *The
      Manager's Path* and the Management 101 chapter.
