# Spec: Lanes

> Working doc for the post in this folder. The spec drives the post; the post
> is the artifact.

## Intent

State where I believe ongoing product work actually lives: in a handful of
**durable, outcome-oriented lanes** per team — each with a couple of metrics
and a directional sense of the opportunity — not in vision-to-tactics
pyramids, strategic pillars, or roll-up taxonomies. The record makes three
moves. First: the work — problem solving, creativity, customer contact —
happens only at the front-line team level, so the lane is the unit of
strategy that can be pressure-tested; roll-ups are reporting devices, not
real things. Second: a good lane is hard to write — durable but revisitable,
outcome-oriented but actionable, non-fluff — and that difficulty is the work
(this is the real lesson of the North Star Framework: it teaches
leading/lagging metrics, actionable inputs, and causal models better than it
installs as a practice). Third: at scale you must admit the true number —
hundreds of lanes for a 150-team company — and build routines, rituals, and
AI-assisted reviews to stay accountable to them, instead of pretending a
one-page pyramid pressure-tests million-dollar bets.

## Audience

My product leaders and product managers (so they know what I will ask to see
instead of a pillar slide); peer executives who commission alignment
pyramids and deserve the argument for why I resist them. First-person
declarative, same voice as the rest of the journal.

## Success criteria

- [ ] **Principle is quotable** — highlight states that strategy is
      operated at the lane level: a few durable, outcome-oriented lanes per
      team, revisited on a rhythm, pivoted when they stop making sense.
- [ ] **The pyramid critique is fair but firm** — vision-to-tactics trees
      and label taxonomies are named as roll-up mechanisms that produce
      deckware and false symmetry; the record concedes their legitimate use
      (a slide to leave leaders aligned) without softening the claim that
      the work is not in them.
- [ ] **The NSF stance lands** — North Star Framework credited as a
      teaching tool (leading/lagging, actionable inputs, causal models,
      vanity-metric avoidance) rather than an installable practice, with
      the workshop-follow-up evidence retold.
- [ ] **The scale argument appears** — the 150-team / 450–600-lane
      arithmetic, the "your lanes are costing you millions" retort, and
      the commitment to review lanes with routines and AI rather than
      refuse the volume.
- [ ] **The tension with [[outcomes]] is addressed** — outcome pyramids as
      causal models (kept) vs pyramids as org-chart roll-ups (rejected)
      are explicitly distinguished, so the two records read as one model.
- [ ] **Checklist is operational** — a derived working checklist (writing
      lanes, testing lane quality, running the lane review, scaling)
      ships in the Checklist tab.
- [ ] **Credit is explicit** — References name John Cutler's TBM 435,
      "20 Unfiltered Operating Takes", as the grounding source.

## Non-goals

- Not roadmap mechanics — [[balanced-roadmap]] covers how work shares a
  roadmap; this record is about the durable lanes above and around it.
- Not org design — [[team-topology]] covers how teams are shaped; lanes
  assume a team and give it direction.
- Not an OKR or metrics tutorial — [[outcomes]] and [[team-objectives]]
  carry the metrics discipline; this record only borrows the causal-model
  idea from them.
- No claim that hierarchy or roll-ups must never exist — the claim is that
  they are reporting views, and confusing them with the work is the
  failure.
