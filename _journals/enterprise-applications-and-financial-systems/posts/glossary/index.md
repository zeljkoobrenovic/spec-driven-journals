> **IN THIS SECTION, YOU WILL:** Find the book's vocabulary in one place: look a term up in the alphabetical index, read a plain-language definition next to the terms it is most often confused with, and follow the link to the chapter that explains it in depth.

> **KEY POINTS:**
>
> * **Look a term up, then read its neighbors.** Each section groups the terms that one part of the book uses, so the rows around a term carry the contrasts that matter: clearing account versus suspense account versus dead letter, system of record versus golden copy, principal versus merchant of record, matching versus reconciliation.
> * **One term, one meaning, one owning chapter.** Every row links to the chapter that owns or best explains the term. The chapters are written to follow these definitions; when a chapter and this page disagree, one of them needs correcting, and the worked example in the chapter is the better test of which.
> * **Vendor and regulatory terms date quickly.** Product names, editions and regulatory status are given as of 2026, following the vendor and regulator documentation cited in the chapters. Check the current source before a definition here goes into a contract, a control or a design decision.

> **[FAILURE PATTERNS THIS SECTION ADDRESSES](where-finance-architecture-goes-wrong.html):**
>
> * **The Many Masters** — one word mastered with different meanings in different systems ("revenue" in a provider report, in the billing engine and in the ledger) is this pattern in its semantic form; an owned definition is the first step out of it.
> * **Spreadsheet Glue** — when nobody has agreed what the words mean, the translations between systems' vocabularies end up in mapping spreadsheets that the close depends on.

## <a id="accounting"></a>Accounting and Finance

The accounting vocabulary an architect needs to read a posting design, a close calendar or an audit finding. [[accounting-for-architects]] teaches the model with worked journal entries; [[finance-processes]] owns the end-to-end processes; [[global-scale]] owns entities, currencies and intercompany; [[onestream]] owns consolidation. These are teaching definitions, not accounting standards: where a definition and IFRS, US GAAP or a regulator differ, the standard wins.

| Term | Definition | Chapter |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Double-entry bookkeeping | Recording every transaction as equal debits and credits, so that assets always equal liabilities plus equity. The balance is an invariant the ledger enforces, not a report that checks it afterwards. | [[accounting-for-architects]] |
| Journal entry | A dated, balanced set of debit and credit lines posted to accounts in one legal entity and period. Once posted it is never edited; it is corrected by another entry. | [[accounting-for-architects]] |
| General ledger (GL) | The complete set of accounts and balances of a legal entity, from which its financial statements are produced. | [[accounting-for-architects]] |
| Book of record | The legally authoritative accounting record of a legal entity: its balanced entries and balances by account, dimension, ledger and period. It is the special case of a system of record for accounting balances; each entity has one per accounting basis. | [[finance-landscape]] |
| Subledger | A detailed ledger for one kind of item (receivables, payables, partner balances, assets) whose total is carried in the general ledger as a single control account. | [[accounting-for-architects]] |
| Control account | The general-ledger account whose balance must equal the sum of the open items in a subledger, such as `Partner payables`. | [[accounting-for-architects]] |
| Subledger-to-GL reconciliation | The periodic proof that each subledger's total equals its control account in the general ledger. One of the most important key controls in a finance estate; Northmere runs it daily. | [[accounting-for-architects]] |
| Summarization key | The attributes on a summarized ledger line (date, entity, account combination, batch) that let the subledger list every line behind it. A summary without one is where traceability is lost. | [[finance-landscape]] |
| Drill-back key | The identifier on a posted entry that leads from the ledger back to the source transaction or subledger lines that produced it. | [[designing-financial-ledgers]] |
| Chart of accounts | The list of accounts a legal entity posts to, with their structure and hierarchy. A group chart is mapped to local and operational charts by rule. | [[accounting-for-architects]] |
| Natural account | The part of the account combination that says *what* an amount is: cash at bank, trade receivables, commission revenue. Kept few and stable. | [[accounting-for-architects]] |
| Dimension (segment) | An attribute on a ledger line that says *where, for whom and why*: entity, brand, business model, market, cost center, intercompany counterparty. Analytical variety belongs in dimensions, not in new accounts. | [[accounting-for-architects]] |
| Trial balance | A list of every account's balance at a date, with total debits equal to total credits. A balanced trial balance proves the double-entry invariant held, not that the books are complete or right. | [[accounting-for-architects]] |
| Accrual | An entry that recognizes revenue earned or an expense incurred before it is billed or paid: accrued revenue (an unbilled receivable) or an accrued expense. | [[accounting-for-architects]] |
| Reversing entry | An entry that exactly negates an earlier one, linked to its original. Corrections in a book of record are reversals plus new entries, never edits. | [[accounting-for-architects]] |
| Revenue recognition | Deciding when and how much revenue to record. Under ASC 606 and IFRS 15, revenue is recognized as the entity satisfies its performance obligations to a customer. | [[accounting-for-architects]] |
| Principal (revenue) | An entity that controls a good or service before it is transferred to the customer. It reports revenue **gross**, with the supplier's share as a cost. Collecting the cash does not make you the principal; control is the test. | [[accounting-for-architects]] |
| Agent (revenue) | An entity that arranges for another party to provide the good or service. It reports revenue **net**: its commission or margin. Contrast principal, and contrast merchant of record, which is a payments role, not an accounting one. | [[accounting-for-architects]] |
| Agency model / merchant model | Stayloft's two ways of selling stays. In the agency model the property collects the traveler's money and Northmere invoices its commission; in the merchant model Stayloft collects at booking, holds the funds and pays the property net of commission. Holding the cash changes payments and safeguarding, not necessarily the revenue answer: both are commonly agent. | [[accounting-for-architects]] |
| Deferred revenue / contract liability | Consideration received or due before the entity has performed, recognized as revenue as the obligation is satisfied, such as a subscription billed in advance. | [[accounting-for-architects]] |
| Funds held for customers | Cash collected from customers for services not yet delivered that will mostly be paid on to suppliers or refunded, not earned. Travel filings often call it deferred merchant bookings; Northmere's account is `Traveler funds held`. It is not deferred revenue. | [[accounting-for-architects]] |
| Clearing account | A balance-sheet account where money waits between two known events, such as a provider's settlement and the bank credit. It should return to zero when the second event happens, and every item in it has an identity and an expected zero date. | [[payments-to-ledger]] |
| Suspense account | An account holding amounts already posted whose destination is not yet known. An accountant must clear it. Contrast clearing account (destination known, waiting) and dead letter (a technical failure that never reached the books). | [[payments-to-ledger]] |
| Event date / posting date | The event date says when something happened; the posting date says which period it belongs to in the books. A late event posts in the open period with its event date preserved. | [[accounting-for-architects]] |
| Period close | The work of completing, checking and locking one accounting period's books so that statements can be produced. Northmere's 2027 group close takes nine working days. | [[finance-processes]] |
| Cut-off | The rule that decides which period a transaction belongs to, including time zone and the moment of the period boundary. A design decision, not a moment. | [[finance-processes]] |
| Functional currency | The currency of the primary economic environment in which an entity operates; its books are kept in it. | [[accounting-for-architects]] |
| Transaction currency | The currency in which a transaction is denominated, such as the price a traveler paid. | [[accounting-for-architects]] |
| Revaluation (remeasurement) | Restating monetary items held in a foreign currency at the current rate, with the difference in profit or loss. | [[accounting-for-architects]] |
| Translation | Converting an entity's statements from its functional currency into the group's presentation currency (US dollars at Northmere) for consolidation. Contrast revaluation, which happens inside the entity's own books. | [[accounting-for-architects]] |
| Cumulative translation adjustment (CTA) | The accumulated difference produced by translating entities at different rates, held in other comprehensive income (OCI), the part of equity outside profit or loss. | [[onestream]] |
| Legal entity model | The register of legal entities and how each platform represents them (SAP company code, Oracle ledger and balancing segment, Workday company). The legal entity is the balancing unit for books, tax and regulation. | [[global-scale]] |
| Multi-GAAP | Keeping books under more than one accounting basis, such as US GAAP for the group and local GAAP for statutory filings, through parallel ledgers or book-specific entries. | [[global-scale]] |
| Intercompany | Transactions between legal entities of the same group, such as recharges, royalties and payment-service fees. Both sides must agree, and they are eliminated in consolidation. The cure for disagreement is an intercompany originator: one component that creates both halves from one calculation with a shared identifier. | [[global-scale]] |
| Transfer pricing | Setting the prices of intercompany transactions; tax authorities require them to follow the arm's length principle: prices unrelated parties would agree in comparable circumstances. | [[global-scale]] |
| Consolidation | Combining the books of a parent and its subsidiaries into group statements: translating currencies, eliminating intercompany balances and attributing outside owners' shares. | [[onestream]] |
| Elimination | A consolidation entry that removes intercompany balances and transactions, so the group does not report trade with itself. | [[onestream]] |
| Non-controlling interest (NCI) | The share of a consolidated subsidiary's equity and profit owned by outside shareholders, shown separately in group equity. | [[onestream]] |
| Top-side journal | An adjustment booked in the consolidation system rather than in any entity ledger, such as a purchase-accounting adjustment. Each one needs an owner and a decision on whether it flows back to the entity books. | [[onestream]] |
| Record-to-Report (R2R) | The process from recording transactions through close, consolidation and reporting. | [[finance-processes]] |
| Procure-to-Pay (P2P) | The process from purchase request and order through supplier invoice, approval and payment. | [[finance-processes]] |
| Order-to-Cash (O2C) | The process from customer order through invoice, collection and cash application. Northmere specializes it into Book-to-Settle, Commission-to-Cash and Subscribe/Usage-to-Cash. | [[finance-processes]] |
| Book-to-Settle / Commission-to-Cash | Northmere's two travel variants of order-to-cash. In Book-to-Settle (merchant model) Northmere holds the customer's money from booking until it pays the partner out, net of commission; in Commission-to-Cash (agency model) the partner collects from the customer and Northmere invoices and collects its commission. | [[finance-processes]] |
| Hand-off (process) | The point where a financial document leaves one system, owner or identifier and continues under another. Most losses, duplicates and delays happen there, so each hand-off needs a carried identifier, an owner on each side and a check. | [[finance-processes]] |

## <a id="architecture"></a>Enterprise Architecture

The terms of the architecture practice itself: the function, its maps and inventories, the reference model of the finance estate, sourcing and modernization, and how teams are organized. Start with [[chief-architect-role]], then [[capability-mapping]], [[application-portfolio]] and [[finance-landscape]].

| Term | Definition | Chapter |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Enterprise architecture | The discipline that treats an enterprise's many locally sensible systems and decisions as one system, accountable for its coherence, the quality and speed of decisions, and the options it keeps open. | [[chief-architect-role]] |
| Operating model (architecture) | Where architects sit, who decides what, and how decisions are made across team boundaries. Three models recur, usually blended: centralized, federated and advisory. | [[chief-architect-role]] |
| Decision rights | Who may take which decision, who must be consulted, who can block a specific aspect of it, and who is only informed. | [[chief-architect-role]] |
| Business capability | What an organization is able to do, independent of how, where, by whom or with which system it does it, such as Partner Payout Management. | [[capability-mapping]] |
| Capability map | The structured, stable list of an enterprise's business capabilities, usually in two or three levels; Northmere's finance map has ten level-1 groups and 45 level-2 capabilities. | [[capability-mapping]] |
| Capability owner | The business leader accountable for how well a capability is performed, across every system and team that implements it. | [[capability-mapping]] |
| Value stream | The end-to-end sequence of stages by which value reaches a stakeholder, such as "settle a merchant-model property". It answers *how value flows*; a capability answers *what we can do*. | [[capability-mapping]] |
| Bounded context | In domain-driven design, the boundary within which one model and its language apply consistently. Inside it every term has one meaning, the ubiquitous language. Capability maps say where to invest; bounded contexts say where one model stops and a translation begins. | [[capability-mapping]] |
| Anticorruption layer (ACL) | A translation layer that keeps an upstream model, often a vendor's, from leaking into a downstream model. The most important ones in finance sit at the ERP boundary and the PSP boundary. | [[capability-mapping]] |
| Application portfolio management | Keeping a fact base of every application, its owners, costs, contracts, fitness and lifecycle, and using it to decide what to invest in, migrate, tolerate or retire. Not to be confused with APM, an alternative payment method. | [[application-portfolio]] |
| TIME model | Sorting applications into Tolerate, Invest, Migrate or Eliminate on business and technical fitness. A time-bound disposition, not a plan. | [[application-portfolio]] |
| Pace layering | Gartner's classification of applications into Systems of Record, Systems of Differentiation and Systems of Innovation by how fast they change and how distinctive they are. Its "systems of record" is a layer, not the data-authority role of the same name. | [[application-portfolio]] |
| Total cost of ownership (TCO) | The full cost of an application over its life: licenses, hosting, run team, integration upkeep, change and audit effort, not licenses alone. | [[application-portfolio]] |
| Finance reference model | The book's layered model of a finance estate: eight layers (operational systems; financial event and subledger; accounting core; close and consolidation; planning; reporting; satellite services; finance data platform) and two cross-cutting planes (integration and control). | [[finance-landscape]] |
| Volume gradient | The way data shrinks as it moves up the layers (billions of events, millions of subledger lines, thousands of ledger lines). It explains why layers exist and where detail must live. | [[finance-landscape]] |
| Fit-to-standard | Designing an implementation by demonstrating the vendor's standard processes and recording only genuine gaps, instead of collecting requirements and building to them. | [[finance-transformation]] |
| Knock-out criterion | A selection criterion whose failure excludes a vendor regardless of score, such as a SOC 1 Type 2 report. Few, testable and set before any vendor is seen. | [[build-versus-buy]] |
| Data exit test | Asking a vendor to export a complete period (journals, open items, master data with history, audit trail, configuration) and loading it elsewhere to prove it is complete and usable. | [[build-versus-buy]] |
| Wrap | Keeping an acquired system temporarily but surrounding it with group standards (canonical events, group chart by rule, group identity, close calendar) under a dated plan. | [[build-versus-buy]] |
| Custom finance platform | A system an enterprise builds itself that performs finance work (billing, payouts, a merchant subledger), usually because of volume, product coupling or a novel business model. Its boundary rules are mandatory standards: it never posts to arbitrary GL accounts, every posting carries drill-back keys and control totals, its subledgers reconcile daily, and corrections are reversals. | [[custom-finance-platforms]] |
| Operational financial fact | A statement about one transaction in business terms ("booking 4471 owes the partner €510 after commission"). Contrast accounting entry, the same reality expressed as debits and credits. | [[custom-finance-platforms]] |
| Explicit retirement test | The questions that decide, component by component, whether a custom finance platform is kept, converged or retired, starting with "would we build this today?" | [[custom-finance-platforms]] |
| Rehost, replatform, refactor, replace, retire, retain | The usual dispositions for a system being modernized. "Replace the system" is an outcome, not a pattern; choose the pattern by what must change. | [[modernization-patterns]] |
| Brownfield conversion / greenfield / selective data transition | The three ERP migration approaches: convert the existing system in place, implement a new one, or move selected data and configuration into a new system. | [[modernization-patterns]] |
| Event interception | Routing a slice of state changes (one entity, one business model) to a new component while the rest still go to the legacy one. Every event must go to exactly one book. | [[modernization-patterns]] |
| Transitional architecture | Components installed only to ease the displacement of a legacy system (interceptors, mimics, temporary mappings, migration clearing accounts), each needing an owner and an end date. | [[modernization-patterns]] |
| Parallel run | Running old and new systems on the same inputs for one or more periods and reconciling their outputs before cutting over. Finance depends on it more than other domains. | [[modernization-patterns]] |
| Cut-over | The switch of a process or data object from the old system to the new one. In finance it belongs on a period boundary, away from quarter-end and year-end. | [[modernization-patterns]] |
| Migration stage-gate design | Moving authority for a book in time-boxed stages, each moving one or more cohorts and ending at a gate: a decision point with written criteria, evidence and named approvers. | [[incremental-modernization]] |
| Cohort | A slice of the business (entities, business-model variants, markets) that changes authority together; at any moment exactly one system holds it. | [[incremental-modernization]] |
| Dual running | Both systems process the same live events for a period, so the new book can be reconciled against the old before the old one is retired. | [[incremental-modernization]] |
| Reverse shadow / point of no return | After cut-over the old system keeps computing without posting, so the new book can be reconciled against it and authority can go back; the point of no return is the last moment authority can return without restating anything already signed. | [[incremental-modernization]] |
| Run-off | Sending new transactions to the new book while old ones complete in the old book: no open-item migration, but two books per entity for the longest transaction horizon. | [[incremental-modernization]] |
| Two-tier ERP | Running a group ERP and one or more second-tier ERPs for subsidiaries under explicit contracts for chart mapping, calendars, rates and evidence. | [[multiple-erps]] |
| Accounting-hub coexistence | Several operational systems publish financial events into one accounting hub that applies the group's posting rules, while their own ERPs keep running. | [[multiple-erps]] |
| Convergence criteria | The factors that decide whether an acquired ERP is converged, tiered or federated: business-model divergence, volume, statutory needs, ownership horizon and economics. | [[multiple-erps]] |
| Redundancy | Several implementations of one capability for comparable scope. Duplication is a decision, not a defect, but it needs to be recorded. | [[redundancy-and-coupling]] |
| Overlap register | The record of every duplicated capability with its scope, cost, control impact, decision, owner and date. | [[redundancy-and-coupling]] |
| Coupling | The degree to which a change in one system forces a change, a delay or a failure in another. In finance it comes in data, semantic, temporal, deployment, organizational and contractual forms. | [[redundancy-and-coupling]] |
| Shadow balance | A balance kept by an operational system (such as a payout engine) that is not reconciled to the book of record. | [[redundancy-and-coupling]] |
| Business-model template | A versioned bundle of canonical event types, posting-rule families, reconciliations and controls for one way of making money (agency, merchant, subscription). Brands bind to it and supply parameters. | [[brands-entities-markets]] |
| Variation point | A place where a shared design expects legitimate difference and exposes a parameter for it, such as a commission basis or a payout schedule. | [[brands-entities-markets]] |
| Market launch kit | The checklist-backed set of configuration items, templates, owners, lead times and gates needed to start trading in a new market or entity, run as a product. | [[brands-entities-markets]] |
| Fork | A separate copy of a shared capability maintained for one brand, market or entity. Legitimate only as a recorded, time-boxed decision with exit criteria. | [[brands-entities-markets]] |
| Ledger-ready | The state in which a payment method, provider or market produces settlement that is matched automatically at an agreed rate. As a launch criterion, nothing goes live until it is; time to ledger-ready measures how long a launch takes to get there. | [[brands-entities-markets]] |
| Team Topologies team types | Skelton and Pais's four types: stream-aligned (one flow of work, such as a brand's bookings-to-statements flow), platform (an internal product such as the ledger and posting platform), complicated-subsystem (specialist parts such as the ERP or tax engine) and enabling (helping others, such as architecture). Team cognitive load limits what each can own. | [[organizing-for-architecture]] |

## <a id="integration"></a>Integration and Data

How money and data move between platforms, who is authoritative for what, and how a number is traced and reconciled. [[integration-architecture]] owns the guarantees; [[finance-data-architecture]] owns master data; [[systems-of-record]] owns authority; [[loosely-coupled-domains]] owns canonical events; [[finance-data-flows]] owns the data platform; [[traceability-and-reconciliation]] owns traceability.

| Term | Definition | Chapter |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Integration style | The basic way two systems exchange data: synchronous API, asynchronous events and messaging, files and batch, change data capture, or data pipelines. Choose by the job, not by the platform you own. | [[integration-architecture]] |
| At-least-once delivery | A delivery guarantee under which nothing is lost but a message may arrive more than once. | [[integration-architecture]] |
| Idempotency | The property that processing the same request twice has the same effect as processing it once. | [[integration-architecture]] |
| Idempotency key | An identifier of the business operation a request represents, derived from business identity, so the receiver can detect a repeat. The same key with different content is an error, not an update. | [[integration-architecture]] |
| Exactly-once effect | Each business fact changes the books once, however many times it is delivered: at-least-once delivery plus idempotent processing plus reconciliation. No middleware delivers exactly-once postings by itself. | [[integration-architecture]] |
| Transactional outbox | Writing the business change and an outbox row in the same database transaction, then relaying the row as an event. It removes the dual-write gap, where a crash between updating a database and publishing an event leaves one without the other. | [[integration-architecture]] |
| Control total | A count or sum (records, amount per currency, debits and credits) that sender and receiver compute independently and compare to prove a transfer is complete and accurate. | [[integration-architecture]] |
| Batch manifest | The sender's declaration of a batch: identifier, scope, record count, control totals and a digest of the content, checked by the receiver before it accepts the batch. | [[integration-architecture]] |
| Replay / backfill | Replay re-delivers records already sent, to recover from a defect or outage; backfill sends records for a past period that were never sent. | [[integration-architecture]] |
| Dead letter | A message the integration layer could not deliver or process. It is a technical failure an engineer must fix and replay. Contrast suspense account, money posted but not yet explained, which an accountant must clear. | [[integration-architecture]] |
| Delivery ledger | The record of every financial message accepted, rejected, delivered, replayed and pending; it makes "money in flight" a measurable metric with an owner. | [[integration-architecture]] |
| Data contract | The agreed schema, semantics, quality rules, owner and change process of a dataset or interface. | [[integration-architecture]] |
| Financial event | An immutable record that something with financial consequences happened (a booking confirmed, a payment captured), written by the system that owns the fact, in business terms, with no debits, credits or accounts. | [[designing-financial-ledgers]] |
| Canonical financial event | An immutable, versioned record of one business fact with financial consequences, in the enterprise's published language and owned by the domain where the fact originates. It states facts, not accounting. | [[loosely-coupled-domains]] |
| Event notification / event-carried state transfer | An event notification says something changed and consumers call back for details; event-carried state transfer puts the needed data in the event. Canonical financial events are the second kind, so they can be replayed years later. | [[loosely-coupled-domains]] |
| Brand adapter kit | The libraries, mappings, tests and guides a producing team uses to publish canonical events correctly without designing its own integration. | [[loosely-coupled-domains]] |
| Integration hub | The governed transport and control layer between producers and finance consumers: it validates, enriches with reference keys, counts, routes, quarantines and replays. It holds no accounting logic. | [[loosely-coupled-domains]] |
| Master data | The shared business objects that transactions reference: legal entities, chart of accounts, partners, bank accounts, products, tax codes. | [[finance-data-architecture]] |
| Master data management (MDM) | The governance, process and tooling that keep master data authored once, correct and distributed. Its four styles are registry, consolidation, coexistence and centralized. | [[finance-data-architecture]] |
| Registry / consolidation / coexistence / centralized (MDM styles) | Registry cross-references records in place; consolidation builds a golden record downstream for reporting; coexistence matches, merges and synchronizes back to sources; centralized authors records in the hub before any source can use them. | [[finance-data-architecture]] |
| Data owner / data steward | The data owner is the business leader accountable for a domain and its rules; owners are business people, not platform teams. The data steward handles daily requests, triage, duplicate resolution and quality exceptions; the technical steward runs the platform. | [[finance-data-architecture]] |
| Data lineage | Metadata showing which datasets and jobs a dataset is built from. It covers the data strand (records) and should also cover the rule strand (the mapping, hierarchy, rate and rule versions that shaped a number). | [[finance-data-architecture]] |
| Golden source | The place where the authoritative version of a master record or attribute is created and maintained. [[systems-of-record]] refines it into the system of record per lifecycle stage. | [[finance-data-architecture]] |
| System of record (SoR) | The system whose value for a data element, at a given lifecycle stage, is authoritative: where it is created and changed, where its history is kept, and what the auditor tests. If another system disagrees, the other system must explain itself. | [[systems-of-record]] |
| System of reference | A system that holds a copy of data authored elsewhere for local use. It never corrects the source; a wrong copy is fixed at the source and redistributed. | [[systems-of-record]] |
| System of engagement | The system where a person or counterparty sees and acts on data, such as a partner portal. A term from Geoffrey Moore's 2011 paper. | [[systems-of-record]] |
| Golden copy (golden record) | The best consolidated version of a record, assembled by matching and merging sources. It becomes a system of record only if the estate declares and enforces that. | [[systems-of-record]] |
| Derived store | A store computed from systems of record and recomputable from them, such as a data warehouse or cache. Authoritative for its own outputs, never for its inputs. | [[systems-of-record]] |
| Attribute group | A set of attributes of one object that share an authority; the true unit of authority, since one partner record can have several authoritative systems. | [[systems-of-record]] |
| System-of-record matrix | The register naming, per object, attribute group and lifecycle stage, the authoritative system, consumers, propagation, reconciliation and retention. | [[systems-of-record]] |
| Authority hand-off | The designed moment when authority for a data element moves from one system to another, with a reconciliation proving nothing was lost. | [[systems-of-record]] |
| End-to-end financial event chain | The full path of one transaction across platforms, from customer event to disclosed figure, with the record, key, invariant and control at every hop. | [[transaction-to-report]] |
| Hop | A step at which a record is created in, or moved into, a system with a different owner, data model or granularity; usually a change of authority. | [[transaction-to-report]] |
| Correlation key | An identifier that travels with a business item across systems so that related records can be joined, such as a booking ID. | [[traceability-and-reconciliation]] |
| Traceability | The ability to follow one item across hops in both directions using stored keys: from a reported number down to its transactions and from a transaction forward to every number it contributed to. Lineage is not traceability. | [[traceability-and-reconciliation]] |
| Reconciliation | Comparing two independent records to prove they agree, or that their differences are known, explained and owned. | [[traceability-and-reconciliation]] |
| Reconciliation catalog | The register of every recurring comparison the estate relies on to prove its records agree, each with sources, keys, tolerance, cadence, owners and where the evidence goes. | [[traceability-and-reconciliation]] |
| Finance data platform | The analytical store that joins operational, subledger and ledger data (FinDB, on Snowflake, at Northmere). It is the book for nothing: it masters metrics, not financial facts. | [[finance-data-flows]] |
| Governed metric | A number with one approved definition, one owner, one implementation, a source of truth, a tie-out control and a certification status. | [[finance-data-flows]] |
| Tie-out | The check that a dataset or metric agrees with the ledger or source it came from, within a stated tolerance, every period. | [[finance-data-flows]] |
| Gross transaction value (GTV) | The total value of transactions processed through the platform, an operational measure. Contrast net revenue, the accounting figure; GTV has an anchor in the books, not a tie-out. | [[finance-data-flows]] |

## <a id="payments"></a>Payments

The vocabulary of card and alternative payments, payment providers and the reconciliation of money with records. [[payments-fundamentals]] owns the lifecycle and roles; [[adyen]] and [[stripe]] own their account structures; [[payments-to-ledger]] owns clearing design and the three-way match.

| Term | Definition | Chapter |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Authorization | The first money step of the payment lifecycle (authorization, capture, clearing, settlement, payout, then possibly refunds and chargebacks): the issuer approves an amount and holds it on the cardholder's funds. No money moves, and authorizations expire. | [[payments-fundamentals]] |
| Capture | The merchant's instruction to collect all or part of an authorized amount. | [[payments-fundamentals]] |
| Clearing (payments) | The exchange of transaction details between acquirer and issuer through the scheme that fixes the obligation, including interchange and scheme fees. Not the same as a clearing account. | [[payments-fundamentals]] |
| Settlement | The scheme nets positions between issuers and acquirers, and funds move from issuer to acquirer. | [[payments-fundamentals]] |
| Payout | The acquirer or PSP pays the merchant in batches, net of fees, refunds, chargebacks and reserve movements. | [[payments-fundamentals]] |
| Refund / void | A void cancels an authorization before capture and moves no money; a refund returns captured money and can settle in a later batch or fail weeks later. | [[payments-fundamentals]] |
| Dispute / chargeback | A dispute is the cardholder's challenge through their issuer; a chargeback is the forced reversal that follows, debited from the merchant through the acquirer. Chargebacks can arrive months after the sale; the merchant contests one by representment, submitting evidence. | [[payments-fundamentals]] |
| Issuer | The bank that issued the card: it approves payments, bills the cardholder, receives interchange and raises chargebacks. | [[payments-fundamentals]] |
| Acquirer | The licensed institution that contracts the merchant, receives funds from the scheme and pays the merchant. It carries merchant credit risk. | [[payments-fundamentals]] |
| Card scheme | A card network such as Visa or Mastercard that sets rules, routes authorization and clearing, and charges scheme fees. | [[payments-fundamentals]] |
| Payment service provider (PSP) | A provider that packages acquiring (its own or partners'), gateway, risk and reporting into one service for merchants, such as Adyen or Stripe. | [[payments-fundamentals]] |
| Payment orchestrator | A layer over several PSPs for routing, retries, normalized data and sometimes a card vault. It never holds funds unless it also acquires. | [[payments-fundamentals]] |
| Payment facilitator (PayFac) | A master merchant that onboards sub-merchants under its own acquiring contract and carries their losses and know-your-customer duties. | [[payments-fundamentals]] |
| Merchant of record (MoR) | The entity whose name is on the cardholder's statement and that carries the payment side of the sale: refunds and chargebacks. A payments role; whether the entity is principal or agent for revenue is a separate accounting test. | [[payments-fundamentals]] |
| Interchange++ (IC++) / blended pricing | Interchange is the fee the acquirer pays the issuer per card transaction, set by the scheme. IC++ passes interchange and scheme fees through at cost plus an explicit markup; blended pricing charges one rate that hides the cost drivers. | [[payments-fundamentals]] |
| Alternative payment method (APM) | Any non-card payment method, such as a wallet, bank transfer or local scheme. Each needs its own cash-recognition and aging rules. | [[payments-fundamentals]] |
| PSP token / network token | A PSP token stands in for a card inside one provider's vault and works only there; a network token is issued by the card scheme and updated when the card is reissued. Portability is the architect's concern. | [[payments-fundamentals]] |
| Rolling reserve | A percentage of a merchant's funds held back by the provider for a rolling period to cover risk. Reserved funds belong to the merchant but cannot be used. | [[payments-fundamentals]] |
| Presentment currency / settlement currency | The currency the customer is charged in and the currency the merchant is paid in. Foreign exchange can occur at several moments between them. | [[payments-fundamentals]] |
| Company account / merchant account (Adyen) | Adyen's company account represents the business and holds merchant accounts; a merchant account is where payments are processed and paid out, and where reconciliation starts. | [[adyen]] |
| Balance Platform (Adyen) | The structure behind Adyen for Platforms: account holders (with legal entities and KYC data), balance accounts holding funds, transfer instruments for payouts, and split instructions that divide each payment. | [[adyen]] |
| Payable batch (Adyen) | The unit Adyen pays out in: if a batch's payable balance is positive when it closes, Adyen instructs a net payout. | [[adyen]] |
| Settlement details report (Adyen) | Adyen's transaction-level report of every credit and debit in a payable batch, with fees; the primary input for settlement matching. | [[adyen]] |
| PSP reference / merchant reference | The provider's key for a payment and the merchant's own key sent in the payment request. Matching depends on both traveling end to end. | [[adyen]] |
| In-flight receivable / batch receivable / cash in transit | Northmere's three clearing accounts for provider money: captured but unsettled; settled into a batch but not paid out; paid out but not yet credited by the bank. | [[adyen]] |
| Balance transaction (Stripe) | Stripe's record of the effect of any charge, refund, fee, transfer or payout on a Stripe balance, with gross, fee, net and availability date. The reconciliation unit for Stripe. | [[stripe]] |
| Stripe Connect charge types | Stripe Connect's three ways to charge for a platform: a direct charge on the connected account, a destination charge on the platform with an immediate transfer, or separate charges and transfers. The choice moves fees, refunds and loss exposure. | [[stripe]] |
| Webhook | A provider's HTTP notification of an event. Treat it as early, not final: verify signatures, expect duplicates, tolerate disorder and confirm against reports. | [[stripe]] |
| Payments-to-ledger | The design that turns provider records, bank statements and the enterprise's own payment records into complete, matched ledger entries, with clearing accounts that go to zero. | [[payments-to-ledger]] |
| Three-way match | Proving one payment complete across three independent records (the enterprise's sale record, the provider's settlement line and the bank's statement line) through three joins, each with its own key. | [[payments-to-ledger]] |
| Expected receivable | The open item created when a payment is captured: what the provider should pay, when, with the keys to match it and an expected settlement date. | [[payments-to-ledger]] |
| Reconciling item | A known difference on a reconciliation with an explanation, an amount and a date by which it will clear. | [[payments-to-ledger]] |
| Break | A reconciling item whose expected date has passed without the matching event; an exception someone must investigate. | [[payments-to-ledger]] |
| Minimum settlement data standard | The settlement-line data Northmere requires payment providers to return so that every line can be matched, written into its provider contracts in 2027. | [[payments-to-ledger]] |
| Payment factory | A central service that executes outgoing payments: the ERPs decide what to pay and when, the factory decides how. It screens, deduplicates, approves, formats and routes payment instructions to banks and returns status. | [[satellite-platforms]] |

| Information produced by the entity (IPE) | Any report, query, extract or spreadsheet the company produces and uses to perform a control or as audit evidence. The auditor must be satisfied it is complete and accurate. A key report is IPE that a key control depends on. | [[controls-and-auditability]] |
| Evidence by design | Building systems so the evidence that a control operated is produced automatically, completely and immutably as a by-product of operating it. | [[controls-and-auditability]] |
| Continuous control monitoring (CCM) | Automated, ongoing testing of control operation across the full population rather than a sample. | [[controls-and-auditability]] |
| SOC 1 / SOC 2 | Service auditor reports: SOC 1 covers a vendor's controls relevant to customers' financial reporting (ISAE 3402 internationally); SOC 2 covers security and availability. SOC 2 is not a substitute for SOC 1. A Type I report covers design at a date; Type II covers operation over a period. | [[controls-and-auditability]] |
| Complementary user entity controls (CUECs) | Controls the customer must operate for a vendor's controls to achieve their objectives, listed in the vendor's SOC 1 report. | [[controls-and-auditability]] |
| Bridge letter | A vendor's statement that no significant changes or failures occurred between the end of its SOC 1 period and a later date. | [[controls-and-auditability]] |
| Material weakness / significant deficiency | Under PCAOB AS 2201, a material weakness is a reasonable possibility that a material misstatement will not be prevented or detected in time, and leads to an adverse ICFR opinion; a significant deficiency is less severe but merits governance attention. | [[controls-and-auditability]] |
| Compensating control | An additional control that addresses a risk while the normal control is absent or deficient, such as during a waiver. | [[architecture-governance]] |
| Control impact assessment | The record of which key controls a change adds, alters or retires and what evidence each will produce, completed with Internal Controls before the decision. | [[controls-and-auditability]] |
| Financial incident | An event in which records or money movements are, or may be, wrong, incomplete, late or unauthorized, whether or not any system is down. Resolved by accounting entries with an approver. | [[resilience-and-security]] |
| Business observability | Monitoring the correctness of financial outcomes (balances, completeness, aging, invariants) as metrics, not only system health. | [[resilience-and-security]] |
| Recovery time objective (RTO) / recovery point objective (RPO) | How long a function may be unavailable, and how much recently written data may be lost, before the consequences are unacceptable. Set per function; DORA requires financial entities to determine them. | [[resilience-and-security]] |
| Point-in-time restore | Returning a database to an earlier moment. For a ledger it is the last resort: it erases good work and evidence; correction by entries is the default. | [[resilience-and-security]] |
| Safeguarding | The obligation on e-money institutions (such as Northmere Payments B.V.) and payment institutions to protect customer funds, typically in segregated accounts protected from other creditors, under the E-Money Directive. | [[accounting-for-architects]] |
| PSD2 / PSD3 and PSR | The EU's second Payment Services Directive, in force, which introduced strong customer authentication (SCA), two-factor authentication for most electronic payments; and its proposed successors, the third directive and the Payment Services Regulation, not yet in force as of 2026. | [[payments-fundamentals]] |
| PCI DSS | The Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard for storing, processing and transmitting card data. Tokenization reduces the scope. | [[payments-fundamentals]] |
| Digital Operational Resilience Act (DORA) | Regulation (EU) 2022/2554, applying since 17 January 2025, on ICT risk, incident reporting, resilience testing and third-party risk for financial entities, including payment and e-money institutions. At Northmere it reaches Northmere Payments B.V., not the whole group. | [[resilience-and-security]] |
| Continuous transaction controls (CTC) | Regimes in which invoice data reaches the tax authority at or near the time of the transaction. Under clearance, a document is not legally an invoice until a government platform accepts it, which puts the invoice on the critical path. | [[satellite-platforms]] |
| Peppol / four-corner model | An international e-invoicing network in which seller and buyer each connect through an accredited service provider and the providers exchange the document. | [[satellite-platforms]] |
| VAT in the Digital Age (ViDA) | The EU VAT package in force since 14 April 2025 that phases in digital reporting, e-invoicing and, from 1 July 2028, deemed-supplier status for platforms facilitating short-term accommodation rental and passenger road transport. Deemed-supplier status for VAT does not make a platform the principal for revenue. | [[global-scale]] |
| Pillar Two | The OECD global minimum tax of 15% per jurisdiction for large groups, which needs an effective-tax-rate dataset per entity and jurisdiction reconciled to consolidation. | [[global-scale]] |
| EU AI Act | The EU regulation classifying AI systems by risk; most finance-process agents are likely not high-risk, while credit scoring of natural persons is. | [[ai-in-finance]] |
| Retention | The period for which records and their traceability must stay readable, set by law per record type, such as ten years for German books. Modernization ends at retrievability, not at go-live. | [[modernization-patterns]] |

## <a id="strategy"></a>Strategy and Governance

The Chief Architect's strategic vocabulary: how decisions are made and recorded, how the target is planned, how vendors and debt are managed, and how AI and long-term change are governed. Start with [[part-4]].

| Term | Definition | Chapter |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Architecture governance | The decision system that sets standards, grants waivers and records decisions, sized so that most decisions are taken quickly by teams and only a few go to a board. | [[architecture-governance]] |
| Architecture decision record (ADR) | A short record of one architecture decision: context, options, decision, consequences and status. Proposed by Michael Nygard in 2011; an accepted record is never edited, only superseded. | [[architecture-governance]] |
| Architecture review board | A standing group with authority to approve, reject or condition architecture decisions. It can say no and make it stick, but concentrates decisions in a few calendars. | [[architecture-governance]] |
| Advice process | Andrew Harmel-Law's model in which anyone may take an architecture decision after seeking advice from everyone affected and from relevant experts, and recording that advice. Harmel-Law pairs it with a weekly, open Architecture Advisory Forum. | [[architecture-governance]] |
| Waiver | A recorded, time-limited exception from a mandatory standard, with an owner, a funded exit plan and, where in ICFR scope, a compensating control. At Northmere it expires within 12 months and may be renewed once. | [[architecture-governance]] |
| Design authority | The standing body that holds decision rights over a program's design: target data model, system boundaries, the global template and every deviation from it. Narrower in scope and faster in tempo than a review board. | [[finance-transformation]] |
| Architecture principle | A durable statement of how the enterprise intends to build and change its systems, with a name, statement, rationale and implications, used where no standard applies. | [[principles-standards-reference]] |
| Standard (architecture) | A specific, mandatory requirement with a scope, owner, version, effective date, conformance checks and a waiver process; how a principle becomes enforceable. | [[principles-standards-reference]] |
| Reference architecture | A solution template for a recurring problem in this enterprise: components, responsibilities, interfaces, variation points and the checks that prove conformance. | [[principles-standards-reference]] |
| Target state | The described future state of the estate; closer to a hypothesis than a blueprint, resting on assumptions the roadmap tests. | [[target-and-transition-states]] |
| Transition architecture | A formal description of the architecture at an architecturally significant point between baseline and target. | [[target-and-transition-states]] |
| Plateau | A relatively stable state of the estate that lasts a limited time. In finance a plateau must be closable at period end, auditable, operable and valuable on its own. | [[target-and-transition-states]] |
| Kill criterion | A condition agreed before work starts under which a route is abandoned or changed. | [[target-and-transition-states]] |
| Vendor tier | A classification of a vendor (strategic, core, tactical at Northmere) by how much depends on it and how hard it is to replace; it sets ownership, exit-plan depth and approval. | [[vendor-portfolio-strategy]] |
| Dependency budget | An explicit, deliberately spent limit on how far the estate may depend on a strategic vendor beyond the role it was chosen for, such as custom objects, extensions holding business logic or embedded agents. Dependency is a budget, not a sin. | [[vendor-portfolio-strategy]] |
| Exit plan | The documented, costed and tested way to stop depending on a vendor while keeping the business running and the books intact, including when the vendor fails. | [[vendor-portfolio-strategy]] |
| Global template | The single, documented design (processes, configuration, chart of accounts, master-data rules, integrations, controls, reports) that every entity, brand and market implements. | [[finance-transformation]] |
| Local variation / variation budget | A local variation is an approved deviation from the global template; a variation budget gives each brand a fixed allowance of open variations, each with a carrying cost and expiry date. | [[finance-transformation]] |
| Technical debt | The extra cost, risk and delay an enterprise carries because its systems were built, or have aged, in ways that make them harder to change or to trust than they need to be. In finance it is paid in close days, manual controls and suspense balances. | [[technical-debt-and-fitness]] |
| Carrying cost | What a debt item costs while it remains, in three columns read side by side and never added: cost carried, risk carried and speed lost. | [[technical-debt-and-fitness]] |
| Debt register | The register of debt items with their carrying cost, owner, dates and fitness functions. | [[technical-debt-and-fitness]] |
| Fitness function | An automated or regular measurement of an architectural property, such as subledger-to-GL tie-out breaks or Level D objects in the posting path, used to gate change. | [[technical-debt-and-fitness]] |
| Exception layer | The finance work that rules, reports and robotic process automation (RPA) never reached: settlement lines without references, invoices without purchase orders, unexplained balance movements. Where AI agents are most useful. | [[ai-in-finance]] |
| Agent control card | The approved record an AI agent needs before it enters a financially relevant process: an owner, a least-privilege service identity, versioned prompts and models under change management, an evidence log that reconstructs each run, and monitoring. At Northmere agents may propose but not post, approve, pay or change master data. | [[ai-in-finance]] |
| Model Context Protocol (MCP) | An open protocol, introduced by Anthropic in 2024, for connecting AI agents to tools and data; a reasonable standard for an agent tool gateway. | [[ai-in-finance]] |
| Stressor analysis | Listing what could happen to the estate, simulating each stressor against the architecture, and investing in the properties that let it survive many of them. Adapted from Barry O'Reilly's residuality theory. | [[long-term-evolution]] |
| Stressor / residue / attractor | A stressor is anything that could happen to the estate's environment; the residue is what remains working after it hits; an attractor is the predictable bad state a system falls into under stress, such as a mapping spreadsheet becoming permanent. | [[long-term-evolution]] |
| Decision service | A queryable registry or check that answers a recurring architecture question directly, without a meeting. | [[long-term-evolution]] |
