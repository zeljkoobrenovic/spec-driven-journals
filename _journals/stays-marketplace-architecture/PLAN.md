# BOOKED — Book Plan and Chapter Contracts

**BOOKED: Architecting Travel Accommodation and Hosted Stays Marketplaces**

*How architects and engineering leaders design, scale, govern and evolve the systems behind accommodation marketplaces — the OTA that sells hotel rooms by rate plan and the hosted-stays marketplace that sells nights in someone's home — from supply connectivity and availability to search, booking, money, trust and the platforms beneath them.*

**Status:** first edition in progress · **Created:** 2026-10-21

## Positioning

The reader is an experienced architect or engineering leader working on a travel or stays marketplace: chief and enterprise architects of travel groups, principal and staff engineers, search, pricing, payments, data and ML leads, platform-engineering leads, product and engineering directors of OTAs, vacation-rental and hosted-stays marketplaces, channel managers and property-management software vendors. The book takes the perspective of a **Chief Architect of a travel group that runs both kinds of marketplace** and must decide what they share.

The central idea: **an accommodation marketplace sells a perishable promise — a specific night, in a specific place, at a specific price — that someone else fulfils.** Its architecture is judged by how reliably that promise holds from the search result to the morning of check-out: that the night shown is available, the price shown is the price paid, the booking reaches the property or host, the money reaches the right party, and the guest and the host can trust each other. The two marketplace types keep that promise with different machinery:

- An **accommodation marketplace (OTA-style)** aggregates professionally run properties. Its hard problems are *supply integration and inventory accuracy*: rate plans and restrictions pushed by thousands of channel managers and property-management systems, cached availability at extreme look-to-book ratios, mapping the same hotel across suppliers, and merchant or agency money flows.
- A **hosted-stays marketplace** aggregates homes run by individuals and professional managers. Its hard problems are *trust and one-unit integrity*: one calendar that must never sell a night twice across platforms, host-authored content, guest–host trust and damage protection, payouts to millions of individuals, and city-by-city short-term-rental regulation.

The book compares the two **wherever their architectures differ** instead of treating them as one, and argues where a group should share a platform and where it should not.

The book is **company-aware but company-neutral**. It draws on the public engineering record of Booking.com, Airbnb, Expedia Group (including Vrbo and Hotels.com), Agoda, Trip.com, channel managers (SiteMinder and others), GDS and connectivity providers, and on standards (OpenTravel, HTNG, iCalendar) and regulation. Company-specific statements are documented facts with a source and a publication date; everything else is labeled as industry practice, reference-architecture inference, recommendation or illustration.

## Comparison discipline

Every chapter in Parts II–VIII has a section titled **"Hotels and Homes: Where the Two Marketplaces Differ"** (instead of LISTED's "Vertical differences"), with a comparison table (accommodation marketplace vs hosted stays) and a short judgement on what can be shared.
