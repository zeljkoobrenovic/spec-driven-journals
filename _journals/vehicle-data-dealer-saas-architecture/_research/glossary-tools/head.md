> **IN THIS SECTION, YOU WILL:** Find the book's vocabulary in one place: read a short definition grouped with the other terms of the same part, decode a Dutch, German, UK or US register word, follow the link to the chapter that owns the term, or look a term or abbreviation up in the alphabetical index at the end.

> **KEY POINTS:**
>
> * **Look a term up, then read its neighbors.** Each section collects one part's terms, so the rows around a term carry the contrasts that cause real design errors: vehicle versus stock item versus channel listing versus market vehicle, plate versus VIN versus vehicle key, stand-time versus days-to-turn, damage estimate versus reconditioning cost, issued versus live artifact.
> * **One term, one meaning, one owning chapter.** Every row links to the chapter that owns or best explains the term, and the definitions follow how those chapters define them. When a chapter and this page disagree, one of them needs correcting.
> * **Register, regulatory and company terms date quickly.** Registers, laws, standards, products and their status are given as of 2026, following the sources cited in the chapters. Check the current source before a definition here goes into a contract, a licence, a register integration or a design decision.

> **[FAILURE PATTERNS THIS SECTION ADDRESSES](where-vehicle-platforms-go-wrong.html):**
>
> * **The Cloned Identity** — the same failure at the level of words: when "vehicle" means the physical car in one system, the dealer's stock stay in another and an advert in a third, records of different cars get merged and one car gets several records.
> * **The Acquisition Archipelago** — acquired products bring their own words for vehicle, customer and "sold"; one shared vocabulary, with one owner per term, is where one platform starts.

<br>

The words that matter most in this book are ordinary ones. **"Sold" means five different things** to market data, the dealer's dossier, the DMS, the register and a business-to-business trade, and **"customer" means a paying organization, a tenant, an enquirer, a buyer and a DMS customer number** depending on who is speaking ([[vehicle-bounded-contexts]]). This glossary pins each term to one definition and one owning chapter, so that the chapters, their readers and the AI tools that read them use the words the same way.

## How to Use This Glossary

The sections follow the book's structure. The first collects the **six evidence labels** every chapter uses and the **fifteen failure patterns** from the Preface; the next nine follow **Parts I to IX**, from the business of vehicle data through identity, value, the dealer workflow, tenancy and billing, distribution and operations to the end-to-end journeys and the Chief Architect's agenda; **Part X** collects the public products and programs the case studies rely on. Then come two reference tables: the **register terms** of the Netherlands, Belgium, Germany, the United Kingdom and the United States, and **Axleby's fictional names**. The **alphabetical index** at the end lists every term, alias and abbreviation with a link to the section that defines it.

Each section opens with the chapters that own its terms, then a `| Term | Definition | Chapter |` table with __NROWS__ rows in all. The definitions are short teaching descriptions, not legal text, standards or vendor documentation; the chapter in the last column gives the full treatment, the sources and the worked example. Rows that combine two or more terms with a slash define all of them, because the difference between them is the point. Acronyms are expanded in the term or the definition. Axleby, named in some rows and in its own section, is the fictional vehicle-data and dealer-software group this book follows ([[meet-axleby]]); its names and numbers are illustrations, not facts about any real company.
