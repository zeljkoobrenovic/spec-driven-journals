> **IN THIS SECTION, YOU WILL:** Trace every capability of the research model behind this book (5 root groups, 11 groups, 28 product bricks and 140 modules) to the chapters that cover it. You will also see where the book departs from the model, which domains it added because the model lacks them, and what it leaves out, and why.

> **KEY POINTS:**
>
> * **Every brick and every module has a home.** All 28 bricks have a *primary* chapter that owns their purpose and terms, and all 140 modules are placed in a chapter that covers them. Coverage is thinnest where the model itself is thin: consumer travel documents (`ctrv`) and the plates webshop (`mats`), which the book treats as boundaries rather than subjects.
> * **The model's journeys are complete; its cross-cutting layer is not.** The model was drawn from personas and their jobs, so it has no owner for DMS integration, CRM, events, ingestion, data-quality operations, onboarding, observability or connected-vehicle data. The book adds nine domains for them, and changes six of the model's own decompositions, from where license checks run to where the stock item lives.
> * **It is a research map, not a description.** The model describes one real Dutch company from public sources, with inferred internals and invented KPI values. The book uses its vocabulary, actors, workflows and stressors, takes every company fact from dated public sources, and sets its examples in Axleby, a fictional group.

> **[FAILURE PATTERNS THIS SECTION ADDRESSES](where-vehicle-platforms-go-wrong.html):**
>
> * **License Blindness** — the model checks licenses at only three of its data interfaces; the coverage map shows every other interface where data leaves unchecked, and the chapter that closes each one.
> * **The Unmetered Call** — only five of the model's bricks record billable usage, although at least nine others also sell something per call, report or transaction; the map names the chapter that puts one meter behind all of them.

<br>
This book was planned from **a capability model, not from a table of contents.** The model lists the **product bricks** (named business capabilities) that a group selling both vehicle data and dealer software needs, with their modules, data, customers, journeys and stress tests. The chapters were drawn so that every brick lands somewhere, and so that what the model lacks is added on purpose rather than by accident.

This page is the audit trail of that planning. It answers four questions: **"Where is my capability covered?"**, **"Where does the book disagree with the model?"**, **"What did the book add?"** and **"What did it leave out?"** The capability map itself, and the method for deciding what the two halves should share, are in [[vehicle-capability-map]]. The service boundaries drawn from it are in [[vehicle-bounded-contexts]]. This page does not repeat their arguments. It is a lookup table and is meant to be used as one.

## Where the Model Comes From

The model is a **Productscape domain model**: a structured description of a product domain (customers and their jobs, product bricks and modules, value streams, data assets, deployments and stress tests) kept as JSON and Markdown in the author's sibling `productscape-workspace` repository, at `productscape-examples/_config/product-domains/mobility/vehicle-data-and-dealer-saas-platform/`. The version used was last updated on 10 October 2026.

| Aspect | Count |
| --- | --- |
| Root groups / groups | 5 / 11 |
| Product bricks | 28 (16 full-stack, 9 service, 3 integration; 13 marked `invest`, 15 `sustaining`) |
| Modules | 140, 4 to 7 per brick, each with a layer (`ui`, `interfaces`, `worker`, `stateless-service`, `service`, `integration`) |
| Brick dependencies | 55; most depended on: the register connector (10 incoming), identity and specification (8), valuation (6), billing (5) |
| Personas / jobs / journeys | 7 / 14 / 11 |
| Value streams | 12 |
| Data assets / stores | 29 / 17 |
| Residuality stressors | 7 (4 integrated, 3 candidate) |

**The model describes a real company.** Unlike the generic reference models behind BOOKED, this one was written about **VWE Automotive**, a Dutch vehicle data and dealer software company, using only **public sources**: its web pages, press releases and third-party coverage, accessed in 2026. The model's own notes say that brick internals (modules, stores, dependencies) are **inferred from product behavior, not taken from VWE architecture documentation**, and that every KPI value is a **modeled seed value, not a reported figure**.

The book draws three rules from that:

- **Nothing in the model is a fact about VWE.** No brick, module, store, dependency, KPI or stressor is presented as VWE's architecture or plans. VWE appears in the book only in [[case-vwe]], built from public, dated sources, where the model is disclosed once as the author's research map.
- **Brand names are replaced.** Several brick and module names carry VWE product names. On this page bricks get neutral names; module **identifiers** are kept exactly, because they are the model's keys, but they describe a capability, not a product. KPI values are never quoted.
- **The running example is fictional.** Axleby, the fictional pan-European vehicle data and dealer software group this book follows (see [[meet-axleby]]), is not a disguised VWE. It operates in four markets, was assembled by acquisitions, and none of its names, prices, numbers or incidents belong to a real company. Where the tables below name an Axleby system, they name the fictional counterpart a chapter uses as its example.

The model is also **Dutch in shape**: one register (the RDW, the Dutch vehicle authority), one registration tax (the Dutch *BPM*, a one-off tax at first registration), Dutch transaction types such as registration in a new name (*tenaamstellen*) and indemnification of the seller (*vrijwaren*). The book generalizes every brick to the Netherlands and Belgium, Germany, the United Kingdom and the United States; where the model's design is Dutch-specific, the chapter's "Across Markets" section says what changes.

### How to read the tables

For each brick the tables give:

- **Primary**: the chapter that covers the brick in depth, owns its main terms and draws its architecture.
- **Also**: chapters that cover one of its modules, use the brick as an input, or depart from its decomposition.
- **Axleby**: the fictional system the chapter uses as its worked example, where one exists.

Coverage was built from three sources and cross-checked: the digest's complete brick and module inventory (every module identifier extracted, not sampled), the "Bricks" field of every chapter contract in the book plan, and a search of each written chapter for the concepts each module stands for (for example, *mutation feed*, *retry queue*, *rating engine*, *sale detection*). Every placement was then checked against the finished chapter. Two chapters cover **all** bricks at map level and are not repeated in every row: [[vehicle-capability-map]] (the capability map and heat map) and [[vehicle-bounded-contexts]] (the context map and entity–owner table). [[scale-and-cost]] covers the cost of every brick; the leadership and case-study chapters use bricks as context and appear only where they own a point.

### Is anything uncovered?

No. **All 28 bricks have a primary chapter, and all 140 modules are covered.** Four bricks deserve a note, because their coverage is real but thinner than their names suggest:

- **`ctrv` and `mats`** are price-listed e-commerce: stickers, vignettes, plates, one-day insurance. The architecture they need (catalog, order, payment, fulfillment) is not specific to vehicles. [[consumer-vehicle-checks]] covers the consumer documents only where they touch vehicle data (eligibility decided by a vehicle's emission class), and [[registration-and-trade-services]] gives plates and materials a short section.
- **`finc`** is treated as a referral to a lender, not as lending. Credit decisions, payouts and consumer-credit supervision stay with the lender; [[deals-and-sales-documents]] covers the hand-off and its rules.
- **`mins` `module-occasion-meter`** (a dealer's share against local competitors) is covered as a benchmark product inside [[data-products-and-insights]], whose main argument is disclosure control rather than dashboards.

The model's **29 data assets** follow their system-of-record brick and are covered with it. Two stores have chapters of their own: the event topic `vehicle-events` in [[events-webhooks-and-sync]], and the append-only `compliance-audit-log` in [[data-licensing-and-governance]]. Retention of what the write-once `archive-store` holds (fiscal and legal records) is set by the schedules in [[privacy-and-retention]] and [[deals-and-sales-documents]].

## Where the Book Departs From the Model

A coverage map that only matches names would hide the places where the book disagrees with the model's decomposition. Six departures matter:

| Model as drawn | Book's position | Why | Where argued |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `dosr` (dossier) and `occm` (stock store) are separate bricks | One consistency boundary for stock item and dossier; pricing advice and signals stay outside | Lifecycle guards (reserve, sell, write off) span stock state, cost and timeline | [[inventory-and-vehicle-dossier]] |
| Appraisal (`tapp`) and the buying app (`occm` `module-buying-pwa`) are separate | One appraisal service with desk and lane modes | Buying discipline must be identical whether the dealer is at a desk or an auction lane | [[vehicle-acquisition]] |
| `vidn` decodes and specifies; no module resolves identity | Identity (VIN-keyed graph with time-bounded plate assignments) is its own context | Plates move, are cloned and change at import; a specification resolver cannot own that | [[vehicle-identity]] |
| Only `vapi`, `fenr` and `cool` call the license check | A license and purpose check on every interface where data leaves, including report files and consumer reports | The model's own residual architecture says "at every data interface"; its edges do not | [[data-licensing-and-governance]] |
| Only `vhis`, `rdwt`, `mats`, `vapi` and `fenr` record billable usage | Every billable event is metered at its source, idempotently, through one meter | Build data, damage estimates, battery certificates, taxation reports and insight are sold too | [[billing-and-metering]] |
| Bricks share databases (for example, valuations held in the analytics warehouse) | State is owned by one context; others hold copies | Shared stores defeat independent change and blur the source of truth | [[vehicle-bounded-contexts]] |

Two smaller departures follow from the same logic. The model sends **per-vehicle ownership-change scores** to clients as lead lists although its own sharing policy allows only aggregated outputs; [[data-products-and-insights]] replaces lists with match-in against the client's own customers and aggregate forecasts. And the model's single register becomes a **register gateway** with one adapter per country in [[registers-and-official-data]].

## Stressors and the Evolution Chapter

The model includes a **residuality analysis**: stressors (shocks the business could face) and the residue each leaves (what the architecture must keep separate to survive it). The method is Barry O'Reilly's residuality theory. The model's naive architecture has every product call the register and partner sources directly, with its own accounts, price lists, tax rules and portal feeds. Its residual architecture adds a cached register with a transaction retry queue, a shared specification, history and valuation core, a portal-neutral publish queue, versioned tax rules, shared identity and billing, and a license register. [[evolving-the-vehicle-platform]] owns stressor analysis and fitness functions for the book. Its stressor table starts from the model's seven (S1 to S7 there) and adds nine; the table below names the chapter that builds each residue.

| Stressor (id) | Status in model | Residue | Where the residue is built |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Register channel unavailable or terms change (`rdwoutage`) | integrated | Register cache with freshness flags; ordered transaction retry queue; register terms as rules | [[reliability-and-dependencies]], [[registers-and-official-data]], [[registration-and-trade-services]], [[journey-sale-to-registration]] |
| Free open data commoditizes plate lookups (`freedata`) | integrated | Products priced on derived intelligence and workflow, not raw register fields | [[dealer-and-data-economics]], [[registers-and-official-data]], [[consumer-vehicle-checks]] |

## How to Use This Map

1. **Match by meaning, not by name.** For each of your capabilities, find the brick that does the same job using the "What it is" column. Expect some of yours to match a module: many organizations run "lead routing" or "metering" as a team while the model buries them inside a brick.
2. **Read the primary chapter first, then the "also" chapters.** The primary chapter owns the terms and the architecture. The "also" chapters show who depends on the capability, which is usually where interface disputes start.
3. **Check the departures before you copy a decomposition.** If your map splits dossier and stock, or appraisal and the buying app, read the chapter that argues for joining them.
4. **Check the nine added domains against your map.** If nobody owns ingestion, data-quality operations, events, DMS integration, CRM integration, onboarding, observability or connected-vehicle data, the chapter explains why the capability needs an owner. These are the capabilities most often found spread over five teams.
5. **Run the stressors.** Take the sixteen stressor rows and ask whether your architecture keeps the named concepts apart. If a register outage stops plate lookups and transactions together, or one portal's change breaks your market data, start with the chapter named for that stressor.
