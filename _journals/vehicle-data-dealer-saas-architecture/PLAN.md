# Book Plan and Chapter Contracts

**Architecting a Vehicle Data and Dealer SaaS Platform**

*How architects and engineering leaders design, integrate, operate and evolve platforms that turn registers, build data, history records and market data into vehicle identity, history and value — and put that data to work in the daily workflows of dealers, dealer groups, traders, lessors and the software around them.*

**Status:** first edition in progress · **Created:** 2026-10-22

## Positioning

The reader is an experienced architect or engineering leader working on vehicle data or automotive retail software: chief and enterprise architects of vehicle-data companies and dealer-software vendors, principal and staff engineers, data and ML leads, integration and platform-engineering leads, product and engineering directors of portals, lessors, insurers and dealer groups that build their own tooling. The book takes the perspective of a **Chief Architect of a group that sells both vehicle data and dealer software** and must decide how the two halves share one platform.

The central idea: **a vehicle data and dealer SaaS platform sells decisions about one physical car — what it is, what happened to it, what it is worth, what to pay, what to ask, where to advertise it and who now owns it — and every decision is only as good as the identity, provenance and freshness of the facts beneath it.** The architecture is judged by how reliably those facts travel: from registers, manufacturers, insurers, workshops and portals into one vehicle record; from that record into valuations, adverts, leads, contracts and official transactions; and back out, corrected, when a fact turns out to be wrong.

The platform has two halves that need each other and pull in different directions:

- **Vehicle data services** sell authoritative facts and derived judgements (identity, specification, history, condition, valuation, market insight) by the call, the report, the file and the subscription. Their hard problems are *identity, provenance, licensing, freshness and accuracy*: resolving one car across plates, VINs and sources; knowing which source said what, when and under which license; correcting facts everywhere they spread; keeping valuations honest when markets move.
- **Dealer SaaS** sells workflow to thousands of businesses of very different sizes. Its hard problems are *workflow fit, tenancy, integration and distribution*: modeling independents and hundred-rooftop groups in one tenant model, syndicating stock to dozens of portals, routing leads into dealer management systems (DMS) and customer relationship management (CRM) systems that the platform does not control, and doing it all under per-module subscriptions and per-lookup billing.

The book argues **where the two halves should share a platform (vehicle identity, the vehicle dossier, accounts and entitlements, metering, licensing, events) and where they should not (release cadence, customer-facing workflow, API contracts for external data clients)**, and it compares markets — the Netherlands, Belgium, Germany, the United Kingdom and the United States — wherever registers, regulation, valuation providers and dealer-software ecosystems change the design.

The book is **company-aware but company-neutral**. It draws on public documentation and the dated public record of Cox Automotive, CDK Global, Reynolds and Reynolds, Tekion, Auto Trader UK, Carfax and S&P Global Mobility, J.D. Power, DAT, Autovista, VWE, mobile.de, AutoScout24 and others; on standards (ISO 3779/3780, STAR, ADF/XML, schema.org, Standard Webhooks, CloudEvents, OpenAPI); and on regulation (GDPR, EU Data Act, EU Battery Regulation, type-approval access rules, the US Driver's Privacy Protection Act, the FTC Safeguards Rule). Company-specific statements are documented facts with a source and a publication date; everything else is labeled as industry practice, reference-architecture inference, recommendation or illustration.

## Comparison discipline

Every chapter in Parts II–VII has a section titled **"Across Markets: What Changes by Country"** with a comparison table (columns: Netherlands / Belgium, Germany, United Kingdom, United States — merge or drop columns only when they are genuinely identical) and a short judgement on what the platform can keep common and what must be market-specific (a market adapter, a rule set, a separate product). Where the markets are genuinely the same for the topic, the chapter says so in one paragraph rather than inventing differences. The JOURNEYS chapters instead end with **"What Changes in Another Market"**, a short section.
