## 1. Design your loop (the reusable pattern)

- [ ] List every competency the role requires — name them, don't leave any implicit
- [ ] Write the stage map: which interview stage owns which competency
- [ ] Decide which competencies deserve two passes (a screen-level check plus an onsite-level deep dive) and which deserve one
- [ ] Assign an interview length to every stage (screens run shorter; onsites typically 45 minutes)
- [ ] For every stage, name who is qualified to run it, including a backup
- [ ] For every question, write the follow-up questions in advance — use a Situation/Action/Result scaffold for behavioral questions
- [ ] For every question, write a Poor/Good/Strong (or Poor/Weak/Good/Strong) rubric before any candidate answers it
- [ ] If the loop includes a written exercise, fix the scoring bands and pass/fail thresholds before the first sample is read
- [ ] Confirm the full set of artifacts exists before scheduling: competency list, stage map, questions, follow-ups, rubrics
- [ ] Copy the design chain (competency → stage → question → follow-ups → rubric) for every new role — do not copy role-specific question content across unrelated roles

## 2. Rubric-quality tests

- [ ] Is every rubric level anchored in observable candidate behavior, not in a vague impression ("seemed sharp")?
- [ ] Could two different interviewers read this rubric and land on the same score for the same answer?
- [ ] Does "Strong" describe something meaningfully harder than "Good" — not just "Good, but more confident"?
- [ ] Does the rubric name concrete numbers or examples where the workbook itself does (e.g., "4–6 unique searches, ~7 hires per quarter" rather than "good volume")?
- [ ] Was the rubric written before any candidate was interviewed against it?
- [ ] Does the rubric avoid rewarding polish or likability where the competency being tested is something else (judgment, accountability, resilience)?
- [ ] For any question missing a rubric, is that a deliberate choice (e.g., a bonus question) or a gap to fill before go-live?

## 3. Reference: the worked example — recruiter (L2+) loop

*Claire Hughes Johnson's Scaling People workbook loop, condensed as the pattern's reference instance — not the pattern to copy verbatim into other roles.*

**Competencies assessed:** collaboration, conscientiousness, willingness to be wrong, intrinsic motivation, structured thinking, resilience, accountability.

**Stage map**

| Stage | Length | Competencies owned |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Recruiter screen | 30 min | Conscientiousness, intrinsic motivation (+ functional expertise, a role-specific dimension outside the seven) |
| Team screen (run in tandem with written project) | 45 min | Accountability, structured thinking |
| Written project (run in tandem with team screen) | Scored async, 2–3 days to submit | Written communication (role-specific dimension outside the seven) |
| Onsite 1 — Collaboration and conscientiousness | 45 min | Collaboration, conscientiousness |
| Onsite 2 — Willingness to be wrong and intrinsic motivation | 45 min | Willingness to be wrong, intrinsic motivation |
| Onsite 3 — Structured thinking (hiring-manager roleplay) | 45 min | Structured thinking |
| Onsite 4 — Resilience and accountability | 45 min | Resilience, accountability |

- [ ] Recruiter screen covers: recruiting life-cycle breadth, current volume and prioritization, business/hiring-manager partnership, how success is measured, optional fit and process-improvement questions
- [ ] Recruiter-screen rubric anchors "Strong" on: full life-cycle recent experience, 4–6 unique searches and ~7 hires per quarter as the volume norm, a consultative/ownership stance toward the business, and specific examples of influencing outcomes with data
- [ ] Team screen runs 35–45 minutes of questions plus 5–10 minutes for candidate questions; probe further whenever the first answer lacks depth — a satisfactory second-pass answer still counts as passing
- [ ] Team-screen Q1 (toughest recent search) rubric anchors "Strong" on a sophisticated, structured process with independent influence on decision-makers and demonstrated results
- [ ] Team-screen Q3 (diverse candidate representation) rubric anchors "Strong" on defining "diversity" at role/team/company level and creating inclusive job descriptions and interview criteria
- [ ] Written project: request one internal writing sample (e.g., a stakeholder or hiring-manager update, redacted of confidential details) and one external sample (e.g., candidate-facing interview prep material, not a rejection template)
- [ ] Written project scoring: rate structure, grammar/syntax, and tone each 1–3, sum the three; **7–9 passes**, **5–6 needs a second reviewer** (recruiter or hiring manager), **3–4 fails**
- [ ] Onsite 1 rubric anchors "Strong" on driving process-improvement work with the other team, prioritizing within org-wide goals, and quoting metrics used to measure success
- [ ] Onsite 2, Q1 (using feedback to inform a decision) rubric anchors "Strong" on clear reasoning about how feedback was weighed and prioritized for impact
- [ ] Onsite 2, Q2 (what a successful career looks like) rubric anchors "Strong" on describing a well-rounded career vision that goes beyond title, and self-driven accountability for getting there
- [ ] Onsite 3 is a roleplay/intake meeting, not a Q&A: the interviewer opens a role and the candidate leads a three-part conversation — (1) defining the scenario and role, (2) formulating a solution, (3) measuring success — while the interviewer probes and challenges
- [ ] Onsite 3 rubric anchors "Strong" on driving the conversation, adjusting the approach when new complexity is introduced, asking questions that go beyond tactical specifics, using both qualitative and quantitative data with clear reasoning, and stating an intent to iterate ("we'll check in after X onsites and adjust if needed")
- [ ] Onsite 4, Q1 (persistence toward a goal) rubric anchors "Strong" on unwavering dedication regardless of obstacle size, with clear articulation of actions, impact, and what would be repeated
- [ ] Onsite 4, Q2 (a difficult team-priorities decision) probes: what the options were, how they were weighed, who was consulted, what data was used, what company values were considered, and how the decision was communicated and defended

## 4. Follow-up scaffold (Situation / Action / Result)

Use this scaffold whenever an onsite question asks for a past example. Adapt the specific prompts to the competency; keep the three-part shape fixed.

- [ ] **Situation** — What was the actual situation? What were the constraints or obstacles, and what caused them?
- [ ] **Action** — What did the candidate specifically do? What was their first step? Who did they involve, and what did they own versus delegate?
- [ ] **Result** — What was the outcome? Were they successful? What did they learn, and would they do it the same way again?

## 5. Go-live check

- [ ] Every stage has an assigned interviewer and a named backup
- [ ] Every question in every stage has written follow-ups
- [ ] Every question in every stage has a written rubric
- [ ] If a written exercise is included, the scoring bands and thresholds are fixed and shared with all scorers in advance
- [ ] The full loop has been reviewed end to end for redundant coverage and silent gaps against the competency list
