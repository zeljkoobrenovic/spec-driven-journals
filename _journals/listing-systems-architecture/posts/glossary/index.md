> **KEY POINTS:**
>
> * **Look a term up, then read its neighbors.** Each section collects one part's terms, so the rows around a term carry the contrasts that cause real design errors: listing versus underlying entity, publication state versus enforcement overlay, full snapshot versus delta feed, contact versus conversation versus lead, entitlement versus effect, system of record versus read model.
> * **One term, one meaning, one owning chapter.** Every row links to the chapter that owns or best explains the term, and the chapters are written to follow these definitions. When a chapter and this page disagree, one of them needs correcting, and the chapter's worked example is the better test of which.
> * **Regulatory and company terms date quickly.** Regulations, standards, product names and their status are given as of 2026, following the sources cited in the chapters. Check the current source before a definition here goes into a contract, a policy or a design decision.

> **[FAILURE PATTERNS THIS SECTION ADDRESSES](where-listing-systems-go-wrong.html):**
>
> * **Vanity Liquidity** — when "contact" means a message in one warehouse, a lead in another and a phone reveal in a third, the group steers on numbers that cannot be compared; an owned definition is the first step out.
> * **The Attribute Junkyard** — the same failure at the level of words: one concept under several names, or one name for several concepts, until filters, feeds, models and teams disagree about what a listing is.

## <a id="g-marketplace"></a>Part I — Marketplace

The business vocabulary: what kind of marketplace this is, what it sells, how it is measured and how it is decomposed. [[marketplace-taxonomy]] owns the business models; [[liquidity-and-network-effects]] owns liquidity and its metrics; [[industry-evolution]] owns the industry's history; [[business-capabilities]] and [[bounded-contexts]] own the two decompositions; [[vertical-requirements]] owns what differs between verticals.

| Term | Definition | Chapter |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Listing marketplace** | An intermediary that publishes offers from many suppliers and helps buyers find them. Its architecture should be judged by how reliably it turns fresh, trustworthy supply into matches with demand. | [[marketplace-taxonomy]] |
| **Endpoint** | The last step of the transaction for which the platform takes responsibility: a contact, a delivered lead, a completed payment and delivery, or a guaranteed outcome. The most useful single axis for classifying a marketplace. | [[marketplace-taxonomy]] |
| **Classifieds** | A marketplace whose job ends when a buyer contacts a seller. The platform never sees the money, so it monetizes visibility and access: insertion fees, paid visibility, professional subscriptions and advertising. | [[marketplace-taxonomy]] |
| **Lead-generation marketplace** | A marketplace (or portal) whose job ends when a qualified enquiry is delivered to a professional such as a dealer, estate agent or employer. The professional pays because the lead has value, so the platform must qualify, count, route and attribute leads. | [[marketplace-taxonomy]] |
| **Transactional marketplace** | A marketplace whose job ends when payment and delivery are complete on the platform. Revenue becomes a take rate on gross merchandise value, and the platform takes on payments, disputes and buyer-protection losses. | [[marketplace-taxonomy]] |
| **Managed marketplace** | A marketplace that takes inventory, quality or outcome risk itself, for example by buying the items it sells. It carries price and inventory risk on its balance sheet. | [[marketplace-taxonomy]] |
| **Horizontal / vertical marketplace** | A horizontal marketplace lists many unrelated categories under one brand and one category tree; a vertical marketplace specializes in one domain, such as property, vehicles or jobs, with data, tools and monetization built for it. | [[marketplace-taxonomy]] |
| **C2C / B2C / B2B** | Consumer to consumer, business to consumer and business to business: the seller-type axis, which decides supply channels, the account model, trader obligations and the unit of monetization. | [[marketplace-taxonomy]] |
| **Marketplace profile** | The combination of answers on six axes (endpoint, scope, seller type, item identity, money flow, risk ownership) that describes one marketplace in one country and category group. A brand is not a profile: one brand can run several. | [[marketplace-taxonomy]] |
| **Gross merchandise value (GMV)** | The total value of goods sold through a marketplace in a period. Only a transactional marketplace observes its true GMV; a classifieds site can at best estimate it. | [[monetization-models]] |
| **Two-sided market** | A market in which a platform serves two distinct groups whose value depends on each other: buyers and sellers, renters and landlords, job seekers and employers. | [[liquidity-and-network-effects]] |
| **Network effect** | A participant's value from the platform depends on how many others use it. Cross-side effects link the two sides (more buyers attract sellers); same-side effects link participants on one side, and can be negative (more sellers compete for the same buyers). | [[liquidity-and-network-effects]] |
| **Liquidity** | The probability that, within a time window that matters to users, a listing receives a qualified contact and a buyer finds a suitable, available listing, measured for a given place and category. What the marketplace actually sells. | [[liquidity-and-network-effects]] |
| **Cell (liquidity cell)** | A geographic area that buyers will travel or search across, crossed with a category whose items substitute for each other. Liquidity is a property of a cell, not of a site, and leadership is won cell by cell. | [[liquidity-and-network-effects]] |
| **Chicken-and-egg problem / seeding** | Buyers do not come without supply and sellers do not list without buyers. Seeding breaks the loop with free private listings, professional feeds, one dense cell first, or distribution from an existing network. | [[liquidity-and-network-effects]] |
| **Multi-homing** | Participating in several competing platforms at once. Professional sellers multi-home cheaply through feeds; buyers multi-home at first and then concentrate on the site with the freshest, most complete supply. | [[liquidity-and-network-effects]] |
| **Disintermediation (leakage)** | Parties who met on the platform complete their deal, or their next one, outside it. It costs fees, hides outcomes and is the route scammers use to escape the platform's protections. | [[liquidity-and-network-effects]], [[messaging-and-leads]] |
| **Liquidity metrics** | The measurements that tell a marketplace whether matches are happening, per cell, fast enough: fresh available supply, early exposure, contact rate, sell-through, seller return rate, each paired with a counter-metric. | [[liquidity-and-network-effects]] |
| **Time to first contact** | The time from a listing becoming searchable (not from submission) to its first qualified contact. Measuring from searchable separates publication speed from demand. | [[liquidity-and-network-effects]] |
| **Contact rate / sell-through** | Contact rate is the share of new listings with at least one qualified contact within a window (48 hours for goods, longer for cars and property). Sell-through is the share of listings sold within N days, hard to measure because most sellers never mark an item sold. | [[liquidity-and-network-effects]] |
| **Counter-metric / ghost share** | A counter-metric guards a liquidity metric against dishonest improvement. Ghost share, the share of contacts and alerts that reach listings already sold or removed, is the counter-metric for supply. | [[liquidity-and-network-effects]] |
| **Portfolio model** | A marketplace group's strategy of owning many local marketplaces, usually one leader per country and vertical, bought or launched one by one, and sharing some capabilities beneath their brands. The group's value is the sum of its local leaderships plus whatever the shared layer saves or enables, which is why groups inherit several stacks of everything. | [[industry-evolution]] |
| **Copy-paste market entry** | Launching a marketplace in a new country by deploying a copy of an existing marketplace's codebase and operating model, then localizing it; widely associated with Schibsted's international expansion. Fast to start and expensive later, because the copies diverge: the classic origin of the Country Fork. | [[industry-evolution]], [[case-adevinta]] |
| **Carve-out / transitional services agreement (TSA)** | A carve-out separates a business from its group for sale; the TSA is the contract under which the seller keeps providing shared services (hosting, platforms, back office) to the sold business for a period. Every shared layer becomes a line in it. | [[industry-evolution]], [[platform-consolidation]] |
| **Business capability** | What the business does, described independently of how, where and by whom: "Saved searches and alerts", not "the alert cron". Capability labels contain no system, vendor, team or channel names. | [[business-capabilities]] |
| **Capability map** | A stable, implementation-free hierarchy of capabilities, comparable across brands. The book's marketplace map has ten level-1 areas: Supply Acquisition, Listing Management, Discovery, Matching and Contact, Trust and Safety, Transactions, Monetization, Professional Services, Data and Insights, and Platform. | [[business-capabilities]] |
| **Value stream** | The end-to-end sequence of stages that delivers an outcome to a customer: list-to-sell, search-to-contact, lead-to-deal and professional onboarding. Streams carry outcome metrics; capabilities carry activity. | [[business-capabilities]] |
| **Heat map (differentiation, maturity, duplication)** | Scoring each capability for how much it makes customers choose us (scored by product), how well it works today with dated evidence (scored by engineering) and how many implementations the group runs (counted by architecture). The combination suggests consolidate, promote, buy, split, invest, protect or keep separate. | [[business-capabilities]] |
| **Core / supporting / generic subdomain** | Core subdomains are where the business competes and the best people should work; supporting subdomains are specific and necessary but not where it wins; generic subdomains are needed by every business. Discovery is core everywhere; lead management is core in verticals. | [[bounded-contexts]] |
| **Bounded context** | The boundary within which one model and its vocabulary apply consistently. In a marketplace "the listing" is a different model in feed ingestion, the catalog, search, trust and safety, monetization and analytics. | [[bounded-contexts]] |
| **Ubiquitous language** | The vocabulary used consistently inside one bounded context, in conversation, documents and code. | [[bounded-contexts]] |
| **Context map** | A map of the bounded contexts and the relationships between them: who is upstream, who adapts to whom, and where translation happens. | [[bounded-contexts]] |
| **Partnership / shared kernel / customer–supplier / conformist** | Context relationships: two teams planning an integration together; a small model two teams co-own (listing identifiers and tenant codes); an upstream that plans for its downstream's needs; a downstream that adopts the upstream model as it is (a checkout conforming to its payment provider). | [[bounded-contexts]] |
| **Anti-corruption layer (ACL)** | A translator a downstream context builds so that a foreign model does not leak into its own: feed ingestion in front of 160 vendors' formats, or a translator in front of a monolith. | [[bounded-contexts]] |
| **Open host service / published language** | An open host service offers one well-defined protocol to many consumers; a published language is the documented shared vocabulary it speaks. The listing event contract is the marketplace's main published language. | [[bounded-contexts]] |
| **Aggregate** | A cluster of domain objects treated as one unit for changes, with one root and one owner; transactions do not cross aggregate boundaries. | [[bounded-contexts]], [[listing-aggregate]] |
| **Domain event** | A past-tense, immutable statement in business language that something meaningful happened in a context, such as `ListingSold`, published so other contexts can react. | [[bounded-contexts]], [[event-driven-listings]] |
| **Vertical requirement profile** | A one-page description of a vertical's physics, written with numbers, against which architecture decisions can be checked. It covers fourteen dimensions, including supply mix, item identity, listing lifetime, attribute density, decision horizon, lead value, monetization unit, regulatory load and fraud typology. | [[vertical-requirements]] |
| **Shared-versus-specific decision** | Placing a capability, or part of one, in one of four layers with stated reasons: shared platform; shared engine with vertical content; vertical capability, group-owned; or brand-local product. "The machinery is generic, the content is specific." | [[vertical-requirements]] |
| **External system of record** | Where the authoritative listing lives outside the marketplace, in a dealer management system, an agency CRM or a multiple-listing service, so the marketplace holds a mirror and the source wins. | [[vertical-requirements]], [[listing-ingestion]] |

## <a id="g-listing"></a>Part II — Listing

The domain model of the thing being sold. [[listing-aggregate]] owns the listing, its identity and lifecycle; [[categories-and-taxonomies]] and [[attribute-schemas]] own classification and structured facts; [[media-pipeline]] and [[location-and-geo]] own photos and place; [[sellers-and-accounts]] owns who lists.

| Term | Definition | Chapter |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Listing** | A seller's public, time-bounded offer about a specific thing: an item to sell, a vehicle, a property to sell or let, a job to fill. It references the underlying thing; it is never the thing itself. | [[listing-aggregate]] |
| **Listing aggregate** | The listing treated as one unit of consistency: identity, owner, category, attributes, price, location, media, contact options and state, with one owning service and a set of invariants that hold after every change. Paid visibility and moderation decisions are held as references, not as flags. | [[listing-aggregate]] |
| **Underlying entity** | The physical or legal thing an offer is about, such as a specific apartment or a specific car, identified by VIN, address or parcel where one exists. It outlives every listing of it. | [[listing-aggregate]], [[entity-resolution]] |
| **Listing ID / public ID** | The listing ID is the aggregate's internal identity: globally unique across brands and countries, never reused, encoding nothing that can change. The public ID is the short, stable identifier shown in URLs and support conversations, mapped one-to-one to it. | [[listing-aggregate]] |
| **External key / request key / entity reference** | Three further keys: the partner's own reference, unique per integration and the idempotency key for feeds; a short-lived key that stops a flaky mobile connection creating a listing twice; and an optional link to the canonical property, vehicle or product. | [[listing-aggregate]] |
| **Relisting** | Putting an item on the market again. Renewals and a sale falling through keep the same listing ID; a delete-and-repost or a property returning a year later gets a new ID, linked to the old one through entity resolution. | [[listing-aggregate]] |
| **Invariant / policy** | An invariant is a rule that holds after every successful command on the aggregate, such as classification in a valid leaf. A policy is a rule enforced elsewhere or later, such as the seller's slot quota or same-seller duplicates. | [[listing-aggregate]] |
| **Listing lifecycle** | The states a listing can be in, the transitions between them, who may trigger each, and what each state means for visibility, contact and retention. Seller intent, the platform clock and trust and safety change it for different reasons. | [[listing-aggregate]] |
| **Publication state / enforcement overlay** | The book models the lifecycle as two dimensions: a publication state owned by the listing (draft, pending review, active, paused, reserved, sold, expired, withdrawn, archived) and an enforcement overlay owned by trust and safety (none, held, restricted, blocked, restored). Visibility, contactability and alerting are derived from both, never stored separately. | [[listing-aggregate]] |
| **Outcome record** | The reason attached to every terminal transition: sold on platform, sold elsewhere, sold through a contact here but paid off-platform, no longer available, or unknown. Without it, sell-through cannot be measured. | [[listing-aggregate]] |
| **Listing version** | An immutable snapshot of the aggregate's seller-visible content after one accepted change, numbered with an integer that increases per listing. It orders events, guards concurrent writes, preserves evidence and drives re-moderation. | [[listing-aggregate]] |
| **Material change** | An edit that alters what was approved (category, price beyond a threshold, images, title, payment terms or contact details) and must therefore pass the moderation gate again. | [[listing-aggregate]], [[publication-pipeline]] |
| **Sold-to-gone latency** | The time each consumer of the listing stream takes to remove a listing after it leaves the active state, declared in that consumer's contract and monitored per consumer. | [[listing-aggregate]] |
| **Category taxonomy** | A controlled hierarchy of classes into which every listing is placed, with the rules that hang off each class. The category selects the contract a listing must meet: schema, fees, moderation rules, facets and ad segments. | [[categories-and-taxonomies]] |
| **Strict tree / polyhierarchy / faceted classification** | A strict tree gives each node one parent; a polyhierarchy allows several; faceted classification avoids deep trees by classifying along independent dimensions. The recommended default is a strict, leaf-only classification tree, with polyhierarchy only in navigation. | [[categories-and-taxonomies]] |
| **Classification tree / navigation tree** | The classification tree is canonical, strict and leaf-only, with stable IDs; navigation trees are derived per brand and country, with aliases, curated groupings and localized labels and slugs that are properties of a node, never its identity. | [[categories-and-taxonomies]] |
| **Canonical tree with country views** | One canonical set of nodes with stable IDs, of which each country enables a subset with its own labels, slugs and navigation. It shares meaning where it exists and keeps local fit where it does not. | [[categories-and-taxonomies]] |
| **Reference data** | Data that other data points at to give it meaning, that changes rarely and must be identical wherever it is used: currencies, country codes, categories, vehicle makes and models, energy classes. | [[categories-and-taxonomies]], [[attribute-schemas]] |
| **Taxonomy versioning** | Publishing the taxonomy as immutable, numbered releases with stable, meaningless node IDs, each release shipping its migration; listings and saved searches record the version they were classified under. | [[categories-and-taxonomies]] |
| **Category migration** | Any taxonomy change that alters which node an existing listing, saved search, URL, rule or metric points at: rename, move, merge, split, retire or add. Merges are deterministic; splits are lossy and need reclassification. | [[categories-and-taxonomies]] |
| **Category prediction** | Suggesting or assigning a listing's category from its title, description, photos or structured data. Best used to suggest the top three, taxonomy-version-aware, and measured by seller acceptance and later correction. | [[categories-and-taxonomies]] |
| **Attribute** | A structured, typed fact about the item, such as frame size, mileage or energy class, that drives filters, facets, sorting, ranking, validation and outbound feeds. | [[attribute-schemas]] |
| **Attribute definition / attribute dictionary** | A definition states what a fact means: stable key, data type, canonical unit, allowed values, cardinality, scope, editability, purposes, sensitivity, mappings, owner and status. The dictionary is the governed collection of definitions. | [[attribute-schemas]] |
| **Category schema** | The binding of attribute definitions to a category: which attributes a listing must, should or may carry, which values apply, which validation rules run and how the form shows them. Schema versions are immutable. | [[attribute-schemas]] |
| **Required / recommended / optional** | Three levels of requiredness. Required blocks publication; recommended is requested and scored but does not block; new mandatory attributes start as recommended with an effective date and are enforced only on create and revise. | [[attribute-schemas]] |
| **Product attribute / instance attribute** | A product attribute belongs to the catalog type (the color of a phone model) and can be inherited from a matched product; an instance attribute belongs to this item (the scratches on this phone) and only the seller knows it. | [[attribute-schemas]] |
| **Entity–attribute–value (EAV)** | A storage model of rows `(listing, attribute, value)` that needs no schema changes and many self-joins. It is where junkyards grow fastest, because a new attribute is an insert no reviewer sees. Alternatives are wide tables, schema-validated documents and typed per-vertical models. | [[attribute-schemas]] |
| **Hybrid attribute storage** | The recommended model for a group: typed core fields on the aggregate, schema-validated documents for long-tail attributes, and typed vertical models where a vertical's depth justifies them. | [[attribute-schemas]] |
| **Schema compiler / validator library** | Treating schemas as source code: a compiler turns the dictionary into JSON Schema documents, form definitions, index mappings and feed mappings per release, and one validator library embedded in every write path makes all channels validate with the same code. | [[attribute-schemas]] |
| **Backward / forward / full compatibility** | Schema-evolution rules: consumers on the new schema can read old data; consumers on the old schema can read new data; or both. In attribute schemas a change of type, unit or meaning is a new attribute, never an edit. | [[attribute-schemas]], [[event-driven-listings]] |
| **Canonical unit** | The single unit each attribute is stored in (centimeters, kilometers, square meters, minor currency units), with the value as entered kept beside it for display, audit and disputes. | [[attribute-schemas]] |
| **Energy performance certificate (DPE)** | France's *diagnostic de performance énergétique*: an energy and climate class with an estimated cost range, mandatory in French residential listings, carried with its date and method because a recalculation can change the class. | [[attribute-schemas]], [[marketplace-regulation]] |
| **Media asset** | One piece of listing media: a photo, video, floor plan, 3D tour reference or document. A listing stores media IDs and an order, never URLs. | [[media-pipeline]] |
| **Original / master / derivative** | The original is the file as received; the master is the sanitized version the platform keeps (re-encoded, oriented, metadata stripped); a derivative or rendition is a version of the master at a given size, crop, format and quality. | [[media-pipeline]] |
| **Image service** | A service that generates derivatives on request from a URL encoding the transformation, behind a content delivery network (CDN). Its variant space should be bounded and its URLs signed and immutable. | [[media-pipeline]] |
| **Media pipeline** | The end-to-end system for listing media: upload or fetch, validation, sanitization, hashing, classification and moderation, master storage, derivative delivery, lifecycle and deletion. | [[media-pipeline]] |
| **Perceptual hash** | A short fingerprint computed from an image's visual content, so re-compressed, resized or lightly edited copies produce similar fingerprints. PDQ is a widely used open algorithm; embeddings catch heavier edits. | [[media-pipeline]] |
| **EXIF stripping** | Removing camera metadata, including GPS coordinates, from uploaded images before they are stored or served, because a phone photo can reveal a seller's home. | [[media-pipeline]] |
| **Media provenance** | Recording whether an image is a photograph, an edited photograph or generated, so that virtual staging and AI-generated media can be labeled; Article 50 of the EU AI Act requires marking of synthetic content from 2 August 2026. | [[media-pipeline]] |
| **Item / seller / handover location; service area** | Four different locations: where the item is, where the seller is based, where buyer and seller meet, and the region a seller serves (for services and jobs, where a point is the wrong shape). | [[location-and-geo]] |
| **Geocoding / reverse geocoding** | Geocoding turns an address or place name into coordinates; reverse geocoding turns coordinates into an address or place. Every result should carry its match level, confidence and provenance. | [[location-and-geo]] |
| **Gazetteer** | A reference dataset of named places with stable identifiers, names in several languages, geometries and parent-child relations. It holds administrative places from official sources and marketplace places curated for how buyers search. | [[location-and-geo]] |
| **Place ID** | A stable identifier for an administrative or named area (country, region, city, district, neighborhood); listings carry the place IDs that contain them, so named-area search is a term filter. | [[location-and-geo]], [[map-search]] |
| **Location precision** | The level of detail at which a location is known, stored or shown, applied three times: capture precision, stored precision and display precision per audience. | [[location-and-geo]] |
| **Precise location / public point** | The precise point is restricted data (for private sellers, personal data) kept in a separate store; the public point is the coarser coordinate a listing may expose, derived by snapping to a grid cell or by a deterministic, seeded offset that cannot be averaged away. | [[location-and-geo]] |
| **Geo index** | A data structure that makes spatial queries cheap: points in a circle, points in a polygon, counts per map tile. Search engines index `geo_point` and `geo_shape` fields natively; grids such as geohash, H3 and S2 give cell keys. | [[location-and-geo]] |
| **Geohash / H3 / S2** | Three cell systems: geohash is a base-32 string interleaving latitude and longitude bits; H3 is Uber's hexagonal hierarchical grid with 16 resolutions; S2 is Google's library that orders cells on a sphere along a Hilbert curve. | [[location-and-geo]] |
| **Private / professional seller** | A private seller sells their own goods outside any trade or business; a professional seller (dealer, agency, merchant, developer, employer) sells in the course of a business. | [[sellers-and-accounts]] |
| **Trader** | In EU consumer law, a person or company acting for purposes of their trade, business, craft or profession. Whether a seller is a trader decides which consumer rules apply and what the platform must collect and show. | [[sellers-and-accounts]], [[marketplace-regulation]] |
| **Trader status** | The platform's record of whether a seller account acts as a trader, held as a declaration, evidence, a verification result and the platform's own assessment, with a reclassification process when signals contradict the declaration. | [[sellers-and-accounts]] |
| **Trader traceability** | The Digital Services Act duty on online platforms that let consumers conclude distance contracts with traders: collect and check the trader's identity, contact, register and payment details before the trader can offer, and keep them for six months after the relationship ends. | [[sellers-and-accounts]], [[marketplace-regulation]] |
| **Organization hierarchy** | The structure of professional supply: legal entities that sell, branches (physical places of business with their own stock and lead routing), networks or franchises above several organizations, and storefronts shown to buyers. | [[sellers-and-accounts]] |
| **Integration principal** | A software client acting on behalf of sellers, such as a real-estate CRM, a dealer management system vendor or a feed aggregator, authorized per seller, branch and channel. | [[sellers-and-accounts]] |
| **Seller account model** | The separation of who acts (user), who is responsible (legal entity), who sells on this brand (seller account), where (branch), who pays (contract and billing account), who is shown (storefront) and which software acts (integration principal). | [[sellers-and-accounts]] |

## <a id="g-supply"></a>Part III — Supply

How inventory gets in and becomes searchable. [[listing-ingestion]] owns channels and feed contracts; [[normalization-and-enrichment]] owns canonical values and provenance; [[entity-resolution]] owns duplicates and canonical entities; [[publication-pipeline]] owns freshness and removal; [[professional-seller-tooling]] owns what professionals use.

| Term | Definition | Chapter |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Ingestion channel** | A distinct path by which listings enter, defined by who uses it, how data moves and its rhythm: the insertion flow, a partner API, a bulk feed, or replication from an external system of record. Every channel should end in one write path. | [[listing-ingestion]] |
| **Insertion flow** | The web and app forms through which private sellers and small professionals create listings, one at a time, with server-side drafts. It is a conversion funnel whose real metric is published-and-contacted, not form completion. | [[listing-ingestion]] |
| **Partner API / bulk feed** | A partner API takes synchronous per-listing writes from software vendors and large professionals; a bulk feed takes files pushed to the marketplace or pulled from a partner on a schedule, hourly to weekly. | [[listing-ingestion]] |
| **Multiple-listing service (MLS)** | A cooperative database of property listings shared among brokers, mainly in North America; portals downstream of it replicate a licensed copy rather than receive listings from sellers. | [[listing-ingestion]] |
| **Feed contract** | The agreement that governs a channel: scope and tenancy, identity, snapshot or delta semantics, field ownership, schema and version, timeliness, media, errors and feedback, limits, reconciliation and change management. | [[listing-ingestion]] |
| **Full snapshot / delta feed** | A full snapshot sends a partner's complete current inventory every time, so absence means removal; a delta feed sends only creates, updates and explicit deletes, so a lost delete leaves a ghost unless the sides reconcile on a schedule. | [[listing-ingestion]] |
| **Ingestion record** | The key internal abstraction of ingestion: one durable, append-only record per partner listing per send, with partner, seller, channel, external key, payload, received time and run ID. | [[listing-ingestion]] |
| **Field ownership / lock** | The contract rule for each field saying whether the feed, the seller in the marketplace's tools, or the platform owns it. A lock set by the seller in the pro tool stops the feed overwriting that field. | [[listing-ingestion]] |
| **Mass-change guard** | A control that holds a snapshot run for a seller when its effect exceeds a threshold, such as removing a large share of live stock, until it is confirmed. It prevents a truncated file from unpublishing a dealer's inventory. | [[listing-ingestion]] |
| **Command gateway** | The ingestion component that turns accepted changes into intent-named commands (`Submit`, `Edit`, `MarkSold`, `Withdraw`) with idempotency keys and expected versions, so feeds pass through the same invariants as the insertion flow. | [[listing-ingestion]] |
| **Run report** | Per-run and per-record outcomes, with stable error codes and three outcomes (success, warning, error), delivered back to the partner by API, file and notification. RFC 9457 "Problem Details for HTTP APIs" is a standard envelope. | [[listing-ingestion]] |
| **Dealer management system (DMS) / transfer service provider** | A DMS is a dealer's own software for stock, sales and service, and the usual source of automotive feeds; a transfer service provider is a vendor that uploads listings to portals on dealers' behalf. | [[listing-ingestion]], [[sellers-and-accounts]] |
| **OpenImmo / RESO** | Real-estate exchange standards: OpenImmo is an XML format used in German-speaking markets; the RESO Data Dictionary and Web API are the US real-estate standards maintained by the Real Estate Standards Organization. | [[listing-ingestion]], [[attribute-schemas]] |
| **Normalization** | Turning a received value into the canonical representation the attribute dictionary defines, without changing what it means: encoding, text, numbers and locale, units, enumerations, named entities, dates, prices, addresses and phone numbers. | [[normalization-and-enrichment]] |
| **Enrichment** | Adding facts the seller did not state, derived from reference data, registries, models or other listings: VIN decoding, geocoding, category prediction, attribute extraction from text and images, translation, market value and quality scores. | [[normalization-and-enrichment]] |
| **Canonical value / original value** | Each attribute value is stored twice: the canonical value in the dictionary's type and unit, used for comparing and filtering, and the original value as received, with its unit and parsing locale, used for display, audit and re-normalization. | [[normalization-and-enrichment]] |
| **Attribute provenance** | The record, per value, of where it came from, when, by which process and version, and with what confidence. The book's source classes are seller, partner feed, registry, catalog, model, moderator and platform. | [[normalization-and-enrichment]] |
| **Vehicle identification number (VIN)** | The 17-character code that identifies a vehicle. Decoding it fills specification attributes, sometimes as a family of candidates that the dealer's statement resolves. Germany's HSN/TSN type-approval codes play a similar role. | [[normalization-and-enrichment]], [[attribute-schemas]] |
| **Listing quality score** | A summary of how usable a listing is for discovery and trust, built from completeness, validity, consistency and confidence of its attribute values. | [[normalization-and-enrichment]] |
| **Duplicate detection** | Finding listings that are copies or near-copies of each other. Detection can be shared across purposes; the decisions about what to do cannot. | [[entity-resolution]] |
| **Entity resolution (ER)** | Deciding which listings refer to the same real-world thing and giving that thing an identity; also called record linkage. | [[entity-resolution]] |
| **Canonical entity / canonical property** | The resulting record of one real thing, with a stable identifier and attributes reconciled from its listings. In real estate it is the canonical property, which outlives every listing of it and never overwrites the listing, because the listing is the seller's statement. | [[entity-resolution]] |
| **Duplicate types** | Five kinds with different remedies: same-seller repost; relisting to game freshness; cross-feed duplicate (one seller, two channels); multi-agency listing (legitimate, several mandates on one property); and stolen or cloned listing (fraud). | [[entity-resolution]] |
| **Blocking** | Generating a small set of candidate pairs with cheap, high-recall keys (category and place cell, normalized address, VIN, text and image fingerprints, account keys) so that expensive scoring runs only on plausible pairs. | [[entity-resolution]] |
| **Probabilistic record linkage / pairwise classifier** | Two ways to score a candidate pair: Fellegi–Sunter weighting of how much each field's agreement says about a match, which can work without labels, or a supervised classifier trained on pairs people labeled. Scores must be calibrated so thresholds mean something. | [[entity-resolution]] |
| **Field collapsing / representative rule** | Field collapsing returns only the top document per cluster in search, with the others available as "also listed by". The representative rule that picks which listing shows is a published ranking parameter, not an engineer's tuning choice. | [[entity-resolution]] |
| **Publication pipeline** | The sequence of stages between a seller's or feed's submission and the listing's visibility on every surface, and the reverse sequence for removal. It has a decision path (validate, media ready, risk gate, entitlements) and a propagation path (index, caches, alerts, feeds). | [[publication-pipeline]] |
| **Freshness SLA** | The target, per vertical and channel, for how quickly a change becomes visible and how quickly an ended listing disappears. SLA stands for service-level agreement; the insertion and removal targets belong on one page. | [[publication-pipeline]] |
| **Platform latency / source latency** | Platform latency runs from the marketplace receiving a change to it being visible; source latency runs from the real-world change (a car sold at the dealership) to the marketplace receiving it. Feeds hide most delay in source latency. | [[publication-pipeline]] |
| **Pre-moderation / post-moderation** | Pre-moderation checks a listing before it is visible; post-moderation publishes first and checks afterwards. Mature platforms choose by risk tier, not globally. | [[publication-pipeline]], [[trust-and-safety-architecture]] |
| **Tombstone** | A record that a listing has ended, kept with its version instead of simply deleting a copy, so a late, older event cannot resurrect the listing. | [[publication-pipeline]], [[event-driven-listings]] |
| **Zombie listing** | A listing that comes back to life in a copy because an out-of-order or replayed event was applied after its removal. | [[publication-pipeline]] |
| **Unpublish propagation** | The mechanism that carries the end of a listing, whether sold, expired, withdrawn or taken down, to every system that holds a copy, with evidence that each one did. Fraud takedowns travel on a priority lane. | [[publication-pipeline]] |
| **Consumer registry** | A list of every system that holds a copy of listing data, the events it consumes, its removal objective and its owner; the starting point for unpublish propagation. | [[publication-pipeline]] |
| **Pro tooling** | The products a marketplace gives professional sellers to run their business on it: inventory and visibility operations, performance and ROI reporting, lead management, pricing and market insight, team administration, and the APIs behind them. | [[professional-seller-tooling]] |
| **Lead management for pros** | The professional's side of a lead: receiving, assigning, answering, qualifying and recording the outcome of buyer contacts, inside the portal or in the professional's own CRM (customer relationship management system). | [[professional-seller-tooling]] |
| **Pro SaaS** | Software sold to professionals as a product in its own right, such as an agent CRM or a dealer website builder, usable whether or not they list on the group's portals. When a group owns it, neutrality towards competing portals becomes an architecture requirement. | [[professional-seller-tooling]] |
| **Lead ledger** | One store of deduplicated leads across channels (messages, tracked calls, bookings, reservations), with a stable lead identifier and a buyer-deduplication key; the base for billing, reporting and CRM delivery. | [[professional-seller-tooling]], [[messaging-and-leads]] |
| **Multi-diffusion** | Publishing an agency's listings to many portals at once from one CRM, common in French real estate. | [[professional-seller-tooling]] |
| **Neutrality controls** | Four checkable controls that make a group-owned pro SaaS neutral: separate tenancy, API parity with other vendors, a data boundary limited to what the agency chose to publish, and ranking blindness to the publishing software. | [[professional-seller-tooling]] |

## <a id="g-discovery"></a>Part IV — Discovery

Matching intent to inventory. [[search-architecture]] owns the index; [[query-understanding]] owns the step from query string to structured query; [[ranking]] owns the order of results; [[recommendations]] owns unrequested suggestions; [[map-search]] owns place-centric search; [[saved-searches-and-alerts]] owns standing demand.

| Term | Definition | Chapter |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Search document** | The denormalized representation of one listing in the index, assembled from several sources and shaped for queries, not for writes. | [[search-architecture]] |
| **Index / index alias** | An index (a collection in Solr) is a named set of documents with one mapping and one layout of shards and replicas; an alias is a stable name pointing at one or more physical indices, which makes zero-downtime switches possible. | [[search-architecture]] |
| **Search index design** | The decisions about the search document, typed versus flattened attribute fields, index topology (per country, vertical or tenant), shard sizing and the write and read paths. The rule underneath: the search index is a read model, never the system of record. | [[search-architecture]] |
| **Facet (aggregation)** | The counts per value shown beside results: brands, sizes, makes, price bands, rooms. Facets are a large share of query cost and can be approximate, capped, cached and served on a separate request. | [[search-architecture]] |
| **Insertion latency / removal latency** | The two freshness measures of a search layer: time from a listing becoming publishable to being findable, and time from it being sold, expired or taken down to disappearing from results, facets, counts and alerts. | [[search-architecture]] |
| **Update storm** | A burst of document updates caused by something other than seller edits: bulk price changes, seller-level changes that fan out, bump campaigns, or counters written into ranking fields. | [[search-architecture]] |
| **External versioning** | Writing each search document with the listing's monotonic version as its version, so the engine rejects older writes; the reliable defense against out-of-order updates resurrecting sold listings. | [[search-architecture]] |
| **Reindexing** | Rebuilding an index's documents, usually into a new physical index with a new mapping, then switching readers to it through an alias. Done safely it is versioned, throttled, validated before the swap and never run at peak. | [[search-architecture]] |
| **Search contract** | The API product applications use to search, expressed in marketplace terms (category, attribute keys, geo, sort keys, ranking profile) rather than engine terms, so the engine can change behind it. | [[search-architecture]] |
| **Rebuild drill** | A scheduled rebuild of an index from the event stream and read models into a fresh index, compared with the live one; the practical test against Index-as-Database. | [[search-architecture]] |
| **Query understanding** | The steps that turn a raw query string into a structured query: normalized and analyzed text, a predicted category, extracted attribute values and places, corrections, expansions and, increasingly, a semantic representation. | [[query-understanding]] |
| **Query categorization** | Predicting the category, or a ranked list of categories with probabilities, that a query is about. It is versioned with the taxonomy, calibrated and monitored like any decision model. | [[query-understanding]] |
| **Query tagging (attribute extraction)** | Recognizing spans of a query that name attribute values or places ("gazelle" as brand, "T3" as three rooms, "Lyon 7" as an arrondissement). | [[query-understanding]] |
| **Interpretation** | The structured result of query understanding: categories, filters, boosts, corrected text and optionally an embedding, each with a confidence that decides whether it is applied as a filter, a boost or only a suggestion. | [[query-understanding]] |
| **Null and low-result recovery** | What the system does when an interpretation returns nothing or too little: correct, relax, widen, broaden, or offer a saved search. | [[query-understanding]] |
| **Autocomplete / query suggestions** | Completing or proposing queries as the buyer types, ideally only queries that return live inventory and with the interpretation attached ("e-bike in Bikes › E-bikes"). | [[query-understanding]] |
| **Hybrid retrieval** | Running lexical, structured and dense (embedding-based) retrieval for the same request and fusing the results, often with reciprocal rank fusion (RRF). | [[query-understanding]] |
| **Approximate nearest neighbor (ANN) / HNSW / IVF** | ANN search finds similar vectors quickly without comparing all of them. HNSW (hierarchical navigable small world) graphs and IVF (inverted-file) indexes are two common structures, with different behavior under heavy filtering and frequent updates. | [[query-understanding]], [[recommendations]] |
| **LLM query compiler** | A large language model (LLM) that turns a natural-language request into the marketplace's existing structured query, which the classic engine then executes. | [[query-understanding]], [[ai-in-listing-systems]] |
| **Ranking** | In the EU Omnibus Directive, "the relative prominence given to products … irrespective of the technological means used". Reserved slots, carousels, highlighted cards and map pins are all ranking, even when no score is involved. | [[ranking]] |
| **Sort order / default sort** | The ordering mode a user can choose (newest, price, distance, best match). The default sort is the most political decision in a classifieds product, because it decides who wins without anyone choosing. | [[ranking]] |
| **Relevance model / ranking policy** | The relevance model estimates how likely a buyer is to act on a listing for a query; the ranking policy is the versioned set of rules applied to its output: freshness, paid slots and floors, per-seller caps, diversity, exploration and trust demotions. | [[ranking]] |
| **Exposure** | The attention a listing receives, usually modeled as impressions weighted by position; treated in fair-ranking work as a resource the ranking allocates among sellers. | [[ranking]] |
| **Bump** | A paid product that re-dates a listing so it reappears near the top of date-sorted results. It is a ranking event, not a lifecycle transition, and its timestamp is kept separate from the creation time. | [[ranking]], [[monetization-models]] |
| **Learning to rank (LTR)** | Using supervised machine learning, trained on logged outcomes, to order candidates. For a marketplace the default primary label is a contact, with clicks and saves as auxiliary tasks; a gradient-boosted tree (GBDT) ranker with a LambdaMART-style objective is a common starting point. | [[ranking]] |
| **Position bias** | Higher positions get more clicks and contacts regardless of relevance, so logged outcomes overstate the quality of whatever was shown on top; corrected with randomized exploration or propensity weighting. | [[ranking]] |
| **Paid-organic blending** | How paid placements compete with organic results: fixed slots, a score multiplier, a separate auction merged into floating slots, or unified ranking with a relevance floor and a cap on paid positions. The relevance model never reads entitlements. | [[ranking]] |
| **Relevance floor / guaranteed exposure** | A floor makes paid listings eligible only above a relevance threshold for the query; guaranteed exposure sells a number of impressions in relevant contexts, with an extension when delivery falls short, instead of an unconditional position. | [[ranking]] |
| **Ranking transparency** | The obligations and the machinery to disclose the main ranking parameters to business users (P2B Regulation, Art. 5) and consumers (Omnibus Directive), label paid ranking, and explain recommender systems (DSA, Art. 27), generated from the same policy file the ranking runs on. | [[ranking]] |
| **Interleaving** | Comparing two rankers on the same traffic by merging both rankers' results on one page and crediting each contact to the ranker that contributed the listing; far more sensitive than a standard A/B test. | [[ranking]], [[experimentation]] |
| **Recommendation** | A list of listings, searches or sellers the user did not explicitly request, chosen from what is known about the context, the user or both. Surfaces include similar listings, the home feed, recommended searches, low-result fallbacks and recommendation e-mails. | [[recommendations]] |
| **Similar listings** | Recommendations anchored on one listing, usually on its detail page, built from attribute and geography similarity, embeddings and co-view or co-contact neighbors. | [[recommendations]] |
| **Content-based / collaborative filtering** | Content-based methods find nearest neighbors on attributes, price, location, text and images and work from minute one; collaborative methods (item-to-item co-views, matrix factorization, session models) learn from behavior and are absent for new listings. | [[recommendations]] |
| **Candidate generation** | The stage that selects a few hundred plausible items from the whole live inventory, from several sources, before filtering and re-ranking. | [[recommendations]] |
| **Serving-time filter / send-time check** | The availability, eligibility, trust, duplicate, already-contacted and consent checks applied to every candidate when it is served, using the same status read model as search; for e-mail and push, applied again when the message is sent. | [[recommendations]], [[saved-searches-and-alerts]] |
| **Cold start** | Recommending without the interaction history collaborative methods need, in three forms: a new listing, a new or anonymous user (where the session is the user), and a new market or category. | [[recommendations]] |
| **Map search** | Discovery in which the result set is displayed on, and scoped by, a map. It is a separate search client with its own latency budget and query mix: dozens of queries per session, counts and clusters at every zoom. | [[map-search]] |
| **Bounding box (viewport) / drawn polygon / named area** | Three geo scopes: the rectangle of the visible map, cheap but cacheable only if snapped to tiles; a polygon the user traces, which must be normalized before querying; and a place ID filter, the cheapest and most cacheable. | [[map-search]] |
| **Isochrone / isochrone search** | An isochrone is a polygon enclosing every point reachable from an origin within a time budget for a mode of transport and departure time; isochrone search filters listings by travel time instead of distance, alternatively by computing per-listing travel times after retrieval. | [[map-search]] |
| **Geo clustering** | Grouping nearby results into a marker with a count that splits as the user zooms in, by server-side grid aggregation, client-side clustering or pre-rendered vector tiles. Aggregations can leak precise locations that pins hide. | [[map-search]] |
| **Saved search** | A persisted, normalized search query with an owner, a notification policy and a lifecycle, stored with the taxonomy and gazetteer versions it was written against, never as a URL or engine string. | [[saved-searches-and-alerts]] |
| **Percolation** | Matching a document against stored queries rather than a query against stored documents; also called reverse or prospective search. At scale it needs partitioning and pre-filter keys (category, place cells, price band) to select candidates. | [[saved-searches-and-alerts]] |
| **Match set** | For one saved search, the set of live, alertable listings that satisfy its query right now. Listings enter, stay in and leave it. | [[saved-searches-and-alerts]] |
| **Notify on entry, retract on exit** | The core alert rule: send a notification when a listing enters a match set, cancel pending notifications and digest items when it leaves, and ignore changes that keep it in. | [[saved-searches-and-alerts]] |
| **Watcher / price-drop alert** | A watcher is a user who has favorited a specific listing; a price-drop alert notifies watchers (and saved searches whose price limit the new price now meets) when a listing's price falls. | [[saved-searches-and-alerts]] |
| **Alert fan-out** | Turning matches into notifications across push, e-mail and in-app channels, with deduplication against a match ledger, frequency caps, quiet hours, consent and provider quotas. | [[saved-searches-and-alerts]] |
| **Digest** | A scheduled message collecting matches since the last digest; it needs retraction and a send-time availability check, or it becomes the most visible form of Ghost Inventory. | [[saved-searches-and-alerts]] |
| **Alert latency / alert-to-contact rate / ghost-alert rate** | Alert metrics: time from a listing becoming searchable to the notification being accepted by the provider; contacts within 48 hours per alert sent; and the share of alert items whose listing is unavailable when opened. | [[saved-searches-and-alerts]] |
| **Alert hold / risk gate** | A short delay before a new account's first listings fan out, and a rule that listings above a risk threshold are not alertable until cleared, because alerts amplify whatever they carry, including scams. | [[saved-searches-and-alerts]] |

## <a id="g-trust"></a>Part V — Trust

Keeping the marketplace safe and lawful. [[trust-and-safety-architecture]] owns the moderation pipeline, enforcement and T&S metrics; [[fraud-prevention]] owns risk scoring and friction; [[identity-and-verification]] owns identity levels and badges; [[marketplace-regulation]] owns the legal obligations and compliance by design. Regulatory names and status are given as of 2026; the regulation's own text wins over these summaries.

| Term | Definition | Chapter |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Trust and safety (T&S)** | The capability that keeps listings, users, messages and transactions safe and lawful, in five functions (policy, detection, decision, enforcement, review and redress) plus measurement and transparency across all five. | [[trust-and-safety-architecture]] |
| **Policy / policy registry** | A policy is a named, versioned rule of conduct with a scope, a rationale and a legal or contractual ground. The registry holds policies as data, with country overlays, so that rules, models, reviewer guidelines, notices and reports cite the same policy ID and version. | [[trust-and-safety-architecture]] |
| **Moderation pipeline** | The components that evaluate content and behavior against policies and produce decisions: synchronous gates, asynchronous detectors, random sampling, human review queues and a decision service. | [[trust-and-safety-architecture]] |
| **Synchronous gate / asynchronous detectors** | The gate runs rules, list lookups and a light model inside the user's action (publish, edit, send, log in) and returns allow, hold or reject within a strict budget; detectors run on events afterwards (image models, stolen-photo search, LLM classifiers, price anomalies, graph features). | [[trust-and-safety-architecture]] |
| **Risk-tiered moderation** | Choosing pre- or post-moderation, and which checks run where, by category risk, seller trust level and listing risk score; a risk decision, not a technical one. | [[trust-and-safety-architecture]] |
| **Random sampling** | Drawing unbiased samples of live listings and messages for blind human labeling; the only way to measure prevalence and recall, and to correct the selection bias of labels from flagged items. | [[trust-and-safety-architecture]] |
| **Decision service / decision record** | The only component that writes moderation decisions, combining rule hits, scores, actor context and human input into an action; the decision record holds the decision, evidence, policy and model versions and reviewer, and every regulatory output is a view of it. | [[trust-and-safety-architecture]] |
| **Case / case management** | A case is the unit of human work: the items, evidence, decisions and communications that belong together. Case management provides queues, prioritization by expected harm times uncertainty, reviewer tooling and service-level timers. | [[trust-and-safety-architecture]] |
| **Enforcement action** | The concrete restriction a decision imposes: warning, reach limit, demotion, hold, removal, suspension or payment freeze, ordered on an enforcement ladder. | [[trust-and-safety-architecture]] |
| **Demotion** | Pushing content down in ranking for trust reasons. Under DSA Article 17 it is a restriction that needs a statement of reasons, so actor-trust down-ranking belongs behind the decision service, not inside a ranking feature. | [[trust-and-safety-architecture]], [[marketplace-regulation]] |
| **Enforcement propagation** | Publishing `EnforcementApplied` and `EnforcementLifted` events and tracking each consumer's acknowledgement, so a removal reaches search, alerts, caches and SEO pages, and a reversal restores the listing and its ranking state. | [[trust-and-safety-architecture]] |
| **Notice and action** | The DSA mechanism by which anyone can notify a platform of illegal content, and the platform must process the notice and inform the notifier and the affected user. A notice is an entity with a lifecycle, not an e-mail. | [[trust-and-safety-architecture]], [[marketplace-regulation]] |
| **Trusted flagger** | An organization granted that status by a national Digital Services Coordinator, whose notices platforms must process with priority. | [[trust-and-safety-architecture]] |
| **Appeal (internal complaint)** | A user's challenge to a decision through the free, electronic complaint system the DSA requires for at least six months after the decision. An appeal creates a new decision linked to the original, which is never overwritten. | [[trust-and-safety-architecture]] |
| **Statement of reasons** | The clear and specific explanation a platform must give a user for each restriction (removal, demotion, payment restriction, suspension), including facts, automation used, ground and redress, and submit without personal data to the DSA Transparency Database. | [[marketplace-regulation]], [[trust-and-safety-architecture]] |
| **Prevalence / exposure-weighted prevalence** | The share of live listings or delivered messages that violate a policy, estimated from a blind random sample; and the same share weighted by views or contacts, which measures harm as buyers experience it. | [[trust-and-safety-architecture]] |
| **Time to action** | Time from first availability or first report to an enforcement acknowledged by all consumers, not merely decided. | [[trust-and-safety-architecture]] |
| **Precision / recall (T&S)** | Precision is the share of enforcement actions that were correct, from second review and appeals; recall is the share of violating items the pipeline caught, which only the random sample can estimate. | [[trust-and-safety-architecture]] |
| **Appeal overturn rate** | Reversed decisions as a share of appealed decisions, reported per policy and per detector. A reversed automated decision is a confirmed false positive and the best label the platform gets. | [[trust-and-safety-architecture]] |
| **Fraud signal** | An observable property of an item, actor, session or relationship that changes the probability of fraud: device, network, account, behavior and velocity, content, price, media reuse, graph links and external reports. | [[fraud-prevention]] |
| **Risk score** | A calibrated estimate of the probability of fraud for a specific action, such as publishing a listing, sending a message, logging in or releasing a payout. | [[fraud-prevention]] |
| **Risk scoring service** | A shared service that answers "how risky is this action, by this actor, right now?" for many callers, reading precomputed features and combining rules (fast, backtested, shadowed before enforcement) with models (durable). | [[fraud-prevention]] |
| **Step-up friction** | An additional check imposed in proportion to risk, which genuine users pass easily and fraudsters do not, such as phone verification for a new account sending many first contacts. The threshold is set by an explicit cost model owned by the business. | [[fraud-prevention]] |
| **Identity graph** | Links between accounts through shared devices, phone numbers, payment accounts, IP ranges, image hashes and near-identical text, used to propagate known fraud, detect rings and scope enforcement to a whole ring. | [[fraud-prevention]] |
| **Authorized push payment (APP) fraud / purchase scam** | Fraud in which the victim willingly sends money, usually by bank transfer, to someone who is not who they claim to be. A purchase scam, paying for goods that never arrive, is the most common APP type, which is why classifieds controls are mostly pre-payment. | [[fraud-prevention]] |
| **Payment-link phishing** | A fake buyer sends the seller a link or QR code to a counterfeit "payment received" or courier page that harvests card or bank details, often impersonating the platform's own payment product. | [[fraud-prevention]], [[messaging-and-leads]] |
| **Account takeover (ATO)** | Credentials phished or stuffed so that an aged, reputable account posts scam listings or redirects payouts. Defended with login risk, phishing-resistant authentication and step-up on sensitive actions. | [[fraud-prevention]], [[identity-and-verification]] |
| **Verification of Payee** | A check, mandatory for euro-area payment providers before credit transfers since 9 October 2025 under the Instant Payments Regulation, that the payee name matches the account. It helps but does not stop a victim paying a real person acting as a mule. | [[fraud-prevention]] |
| **Identity / account / profile / claim** | The identity is the person or organization the platform has evidence about; the account is the login; a profile is a brand-specific persona on an account; a claim is a statement about an identity with its method, source, assurance, time and expiry. | [[identity-and-verification]] |
| **Identity assurance / authenticator assurance** | Two separate confidences, after NIST SP 800-63-4 (2025): how sure the service is that a person is who they claim to be, and how sure it is that the person presenting the credential is the account holder. A single "verified" flag hides which one is missing. | [[identity-and-verification]] |
| **Identity level** | A named combination of claims that a policy can require, derived and recomputed from claims rather than stored. The book's ladder runs from anonymous and contactable (confirmed e-mail) through reachable, protected (passkey or second factor) and identified (proof of person) to payee-verified. | [[identity-and-verification]] |
| **KYB / KYC** | Know your business: verification that an organization exists and is who it claims, on a business ladder from declared trader to registered business to accredited professional. Know your customer: the payments-industry checks a regulated payment provider must run before a user can receive money. | [[identity-and-verification]] |
| **Verified badge** | A public statement the marketplace makes to one user about another, such as "identity verified" or "registered business". It must say what was checked and disappear when the claim expires or is revoked. | [[identity-and-verification]] |
| **Passkey** | A phishing-resistant authentication credential based on FIDO standards that the user unlocks with a biometric, PIN or pattern on their device; bound to the site, with no shared secret to steal. | [[identity-and-verification]] |
| **Pre-hijacking** | An attack in which someone creates an account with a victim's e-mail address before the victim does and keeps access afterwards. Merging accounts across brands by matching e-mail addresses recreates it. | [[identity-and-verification]] |
| **eIDAS 2.0 / EU Digital Identity Wallet** | The revised EU regulation on electronic identification and its wallet, through which citizens can prove identity attributes; private platforms must accept it only in defined cases and after transition periods. Bank-based schemes such as BankID play the same role in some countries. | [[identity-and-verification]] |
| **Digital Services Act (DSA)** | Regulation (EU) 2022/2065, applicable to all platforms since 17 February 2024: notice and action, statements of reasons and the Transparency Database, trader traceability, internal complaints, transparency reports, trusted flaggers, ad and recommender transparency, and extra duties for very large platforms. | [[marketplace-regulation]] |
| **Hosting service / online platform / very large online platform** | The DSA's nested categories: a service storing information for users; one that also disseminates it to the public; and one with more than 45 million average monthly EU users, which carries additional risk-assessment and audit duties. | [[marketplace-regulation]] |
| **DSA Transparency Database** | The European Commission's public database of statements of reasons, accepting single statements or batches of up to 100 that fail as a whole if one is invalid. | [[marketplace-regulation]] |
| **P2B Regulation** | Regulation (EU) 2019/1150 on fairness for business users of online intermediation services: clear terms, disclosure of the main ranking parameters and of any differentiated treatment of the provider's own services, and access-to-data descriptions. | [[marketplace-regulation]], [[ranking]] |
| **Omnibus Directive / UCPD** | Directive (EU) 2019/2161 amended EU consumer law, including the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive (UCPD): undisclosed paid ranking is always unfair, ranking parameters must be disclosed, review authenticity claims need checks, and price reductions must show the prior 30-day lowest price. | [[marketplace-regulation]] |
| **General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR)** | Regulation (EU) 2016/679. For listing systems its main architectural principles are data minimization, storage limitation and accountability, plus erasure rights and limits on solely automated decisions. | [[marketplace-regulation]] |
| **DAC7** | Council Directive (EU) 2021/514 on tax reporting by digital platforms: a reporting platform operator collects and verifies seller information and reports each reportable seller's consideration per quarter to the tax authority by 31 January of the following year. | [[marketplace-regulation]] |
| **Reporting platform operator / excluded seller** | Under DAC7, an operator that connects sellers with users for relevant activities (sale of goods, rental of property or transport, personal services) beyond mere listing; a seller with fewer than 30 sales of goods and no more than €2,000 consideration in the period is excluded. | [[marketplace-regulation]] |
| **Obligation / control / evidence** | The three artifacts of compliance: a duty in a legal source with its article, jurisdiction and conditions; the system behavior that meets it; and the data and query that prove the control worked. | [[marketplace-regulation]] |
| **Compliance by design / compliance by project** | Building controls into platform components with the evidence query written at the same time, versus building one exporter per new obligation. The first is cheaper from the second law onward. | [[marketplace-regulation]] |
| **UK Online Safety Act / DMCC Act / US INFORM Act** | Non-EU rules with marketplace consequences: the UK's online-safety duties, the UK Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024's consumer-protection powers, and the US INFORM Consumers Act's verification and disclosure duties for high-volume third-party sellers. | [[marketplace-regulation]] |
| **Fair housing rules** | US rules (24 CFR Part 100) that prohibit housing advertising indicating a preference on protected grounds; they constrain listing text, ad targeting, recommendations and neighborhood data. | [[marketplace-regulation]], [[map-search]] |

## <a id="g-money"></a>Part VI — Money

Conversion and revenue. [[messaging-and-leads]] owns contacts, conversations and leads; [[transactions-and-payments]] owns the move into payments; [[monetization-models]] owns revenue models and their KPIs; [[products-and-entitlements]] owns what is sold and delivered; [[advertising-platforms]] owns the ad stack. Payment-industry depth is in the sibling book's [[payments-fundamentals]].

| Term | Definition | Chapter |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Contact** | Any action by which a buyer reaches a seller about a listing: a chat message, an e-mail form, a phone call, a callback request, a booking or an application. | [[messaging-and-leads]] |
| **Conversation / message** | A conversation is the platform-held thread of messages between a fixed set of participants about one listing; a message is one immutable unit of content within it, with mutable metadata (delivery state, read receipts, safety verdicts, redactions). | [[messaging-and-leads]] |
| **Lead** | A record that a specific prospective buyer expressed interest in a specific professional's listing or service, created by a defined rule, delivered to that professional and tracked to an outcome. The creation rule is a business decision encoded in software. | [[messaging-and-leads]] |
| **Lead routing** | Deciding which professional entity (dealer, rooftop, agency branch, individual agent) receives a lead, through which delivery channel and with what service-level expectation. | [[messaging-and-leads]] |
| **Message safety gate** | A synchronous verdict on each message before delivery, within a fixed latency budget, with a vocabulary richer than allow and block: deliver, deliver with warning, withhold, block. | [[messaging-and-leads]] |
| **Warn in context** | Showing safety advice at the moment a risky pattern appears in a conversation (payment keywords, a move to another app, a flagged counterpart) when the gate is not confident enough to withhold. | [[messaging-and-leads]], [[fraud-prevention]] |
| **Lead contract** | One versioned event and API schema for leads across a group: stable lead ID, source channel, timestamps, listing and seller references, consented contact data, typed qualification fields and the routing decision, with each delivery adapter implementing it. | [[messaging-and-leads]] |
| **Lead qualification / lead attribution** | Qualification adds what the professional needs to judge a lead (financing, trade-in, pre-qualification); attribution connects a lead to a sale, mandate, lease or hire and reports it in a way both sides accept. | [[messaging-and-leads]] |
| **Transactional pivot** | A classifieds marketplace moving its endpoint from contact to completed payment and delivery, typically starting with shipping and protected payment for private sales; leboncoin, Kleinanzeigen and Marktplaats have all added transactional layers on top of classifieds. | [[transactions-and-payments]] |
| **Marketplace payments** | Taking payment from a buyer, holding it and paying it out to a seller, usually through a payment service provider's marketplace product so that the provider, not the marketplace, is the licensed party. | [[transactions-and-payments]], [[payments-fundamentals]] |
| **Payment service provider (PSP)** | A company that processes payments for merchants and platforms; its marketplace product handles onboarding and KYC of payees, split payments, holds and payouts. | [[transactions-and-payments]] |
| **Merchant of record** | The party whose name is on the buyer's payment statement and which carries the payment-side obligations, refunds and chargebacks. In protected private sales the seller usually remains the seller and the platform an intermediary. | [[transactions-and-payments]], [[payments-fundamentals]] |
| **Buyer protection** | A promise that the buyer gets their money back if the item does not arrive or is not as described, funded by a buyer fee. It is a liability to provision, not only a fee to collect. | [[transactions-and-payments]], [[monetization-models]] |
| **Hold and release (escrow-like hold) / payout** | Keeping the buyer's payment in a held balance at the licensed provider until a release event (buyer confirmation, a delivery scan plus a waiting period, a handover code), then making it available for payout to the seller's bank account. Often called escrow informally, though legally it usually is not; payouts and payout-account changes are prime fraud targets. | [[transactions-and-payments]] |
| **Order state machine** | The states of a protected sale, held on the order rather than the listing: placed, paid (listing reserved), awaiting hand-in or pickup, in transit, delivered, completed, disputed, refunded, cancelled or returned, with a card chargeback possible after completion. Every waiting state has a deadline, and each transition emits events for the ledger, trust and safety and messaging. | [[transactions-and-payments]] |
| **Dispute / chargeback** | A dispute is opened with the platform under its buyer protection; a chargeback is opened by the buyer's card issuer under card-scheme rules. Both can occur for the same order, on different clocks. | [[transactions-and-payments]] |
| **Monetization model** | The combination of who pays, for which unit, at which moment in the funnel and on what pricing basis. Each part becomes a system, and each model spends a different part of liquidity. | [[monetization-models]] |
| **Price structure / unit of charge** | Two-sided markets set a price structure, which side pays and for what, not just a price level. The unit of charge is the thing whose quantity sets the bill: a listing, a slot, a lead, a sale, an impression. | [[monetization-models]] |
| **Average revenue per advertiser (ARPA)** | Rightmove's KPI: monthly revenue from agency and new-homes advertisers divided by the number of advertisers, averaged over the year. Autotrader's equivalent per retailer forecourt is ARPR (average revenue per retailer). | [[monetization-models]] |
| **Take rate** | Marketplace revenue divided by gross merchandise value, as eBay defines it: the share of transaction value the platform keeps. | [[monetization-models]] |
| **Value-added services (VAS)** | The industry name for paid options bought on top of a basic listing: bumps, top or featured positions, highlights and galleries, longer durations. The core of the freemium paid-visibility model. | [[monetization-models]] |
| **Insertion fee** | A charge to publish a listing, usually keyed by category, region and seller type. It spends supply, because some sellers stop listing. | [[monetization-models]] |
| **Paid visibility / top slot / highlight** | Free listing with paid prominence: a top or featured slot reserves positions above or within organic results; a highlight or gallery changes how the card looks. Every promoted result must carry a paid label. | [[monetization-models]] |
| **Professional subscription** | A monthly contract granting listing slots, boosts, tools, statistics and a level of prominence; the center of gravity of vertical portals' revenue. | [[monetization-models]] |
| **Depth product** | Per-listing tiers (standard, premium) that change a listing's prominence and presentation, so revenue is listings times yield; exposed to supply leaving. | [[monetization-models]] |
| **Pay per lead / share of voice / pay at close** | Lead-based models: a price per billable lead; connections distributed in proportion to each professional's share of advertising spend in an area; and a fee only when a transaction closes with a lead. Each needs new systems: billable-lead rules, an allocation engine, an outcome pipeline. | [[monetization-models]] |
| **Billable lead** | The precise, contractual definition of a lead the professional pays for, with deduplication (one buyer, several channels, one lead), validity rules and a dispute and credit process. | [[monetization-models]] |
| **Product catalog** | The versioned definition of everything sellable: products, packages and add-ons, and the effects each grants. Reference data shipped as releases. | [[products-and-entitlements]] |
| **Product / package / add-on** | A product is one sellable thing with a defined effect ("Bump", "Top listing (7 days)"); a package bundles products and quotas on a contract ("150 slots + 20 boosts per month"); an add-on is sold on top of a package, possibly metered. | [[products-and-entitlements]] |
| **Pricing engine** | The component that computes the price of a catalog item for a specific buyer, context and date from effective-dated price lists, discounts and promotions. | [[products-and-entitlements]] |
| **Entitlement** | A right, held by an account or listing for a period, to receive a defined effect, such as "may publish up to 150 vehicles" or "appears in a top slot until Sunday 23:59", with a holder, product version, validity window, optional quantity and source. | [[products-and-entitlements]] |
| **Quota** | A countable entitlement: listing slots, boosts, leads, credits. | [[products-and-entitlements]] |
| **Entitlement ledger** | The append-only record of what each account or listing holds and has used, with idempotent entries and explainable balances; the system of record for "what was sold versus what was delivered". | [[products-and-entitlements]] |
| **Effect / effect projector / enforcement point** | An effect is the projection of active entitlements that one place in the platform needs; the projector publishes effect changes from ledger events; enforcement points (insertion, ranking, display) read effects, tolerating bounded staleness, and never the ledger live. | [[products-and-entitlements]] |
| **Delivery evidence / delivery reconciliation** | The record of what the platform actually did (listings under a slot, paid impressions, boosts executed, leads routed), compared daily with what was granted and what was billed. | [[products-and-entitlements]] |
| **Ad stack** | The systems that decide, serve, measure and bill advertising on a marketplace's surfaces: consent, ad server, header bidding, supply-side connections, audience services, creative rendering, measurement and policy. | [[advertising-platforms]] |
| **Ad server** | The publisher's decision engine that chooses, for each ad slot, among direct campaigns, deals and programmatic bids, renders the creative and reports delivery. | [[advertising-platforms]] |
| **Supply-side / demand-side platform (SSP / DSP)** | An SSP is an exchange that sells a publisher's impressions to many buyers in real-time auctions; a DSP is the buyer's system that bids in them. | [[advertising-platforms]] |
| **Header bidding / Prebid** | Running an auction among several SSPs before the ad server decides, so programmatic demand competes with direct campaigns. Prebid is the open-source implementation most publishers use, in the browser or server-side. | [[advertising-platforms]] |
| **Sponsored listing** | A seller's own listing promoted into prominent positions in search results or recommendations for payment per click, per sale or per day, served by the marketplace's search stack under relevance floors and slot caps, and labeled as paid. | [[advertising-platforms]] |
| **Retail media** | Advertising sold to brands, such as car makers, banks and insurers, against the marketplace's own audiences and placements, often with closed-loop measurement against on-platform outcomes; closer to a data product than to a banner. | [[advertising-platforms]] |
| **First-party audience** | A segment of users defined from the marketplace's own data (searches, saved searches, viewed listings, enquiries), built under the consent and purposes users granted, and activated on or off the platform. It is a data product with purpose limits. | [[advertising-platforms]] |
| **Consent management platform (CMP) / TCF** | The CMP asks for and records consent; in Europe it usually implements the IAB Transparency and Consent Framework (TCF), which encodes choices in a TC string passed to ad vendors. | [[advertising-platforms]] |
| **Ad load / CPM / CPC / viewability** | Ad load is the share of positions given to advertising; CPM is price per thousand impressions (eCPM the effective figure after all sources); CPC is price per click; viewability is whether an ad had a chance to be seen. | [[advertising-platforms]] |
| **Consent or pay / consent or contextual** | Consent or pay asks users to consent to tracking or pay, which the European Data Protection Board found in 2024 rarely valid for large platforms; consent or contextual shows users who decline ads targeted by page context, which a listing page states unusually well. | [[advertising-platforms]] |

## <a id="g-platform"></a>Part VII — Platform

The distributed system underneath. [[event-driven-listings]] owns listing events, the outbox and schema evolution; [[listing-data-management]] owns the system of record, read models and erasure; [[web-and-seo-architecture]] owns the customer-facing stack; [[data-platform]] owns tracking, data products and governed metrics; [[experimentation]] owns two-sided testing; [[ml-platform]] owns models in production; [[multi-country-platforms]] owns tenancy; [[reliability-and-scale]] owns journey objectives and cost.

| Term | Definition | Chapter |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Listing event** | A domain event about one listing aggregate, emitted by the listing's owning context on every invariant-preserving transition (submitted, published, updated, price changed, paused, reserved, sold, expired, removed); the published language other contexts use to learn about listings. | [[event-driven-listings]] |
| **Change event (change data capture, CDC)** | A row-level record of an insert, update or delete, read from a database's transaction log. It reflects the table, not the business intent, so it is allowed as a source but never as a contract. | [[event-driven-listings]] |
| **Event notification / event-carried state transfer / event sourcing** | Three uses of events: saying only that something changed so consumers call back; carrying the state consumers need to keep their own copy; and making the events the system of record. Listing events should carry state. | [[event-driven-listings]] |
| **At-least-once delivery / idempotent consumer** | Practical pipelines may deliver an event twice after a retry or restart; a consumer is idempotent if processing an event twice has the same effect as once. | [[event-driven-listings]] |
| **Version-gated apply** | Each event carries the listing's aggregate version, and a consumer applies it only if the version is newer than the one it holds, which makes consumers idempotent and order-safe and lets missed events self-heal. | [[event-driven-listings]] |
| **Transactional outbox** | Writing the event into an outbox table in the same database transaction as the state change, then relaying it to the broker. It solves the dual-write problem (a change committed without its event, or the reverse) and lets domain code choose the event's shape. | [[event-driven-listings]] |
| **Translation layer (CDC translator)** | An anti-corruption layer that consumes a legacy system's raw change stream, infers the business transition and emits contract events, so that only the translator reads the monolith's tables. | [[event-driven-listings]] |
| **Schema registry / schema evolution** | The registry stores every version of an event schema and rejects incompatible changes at publish time; schema evolution is the discipline of changing contracts without breaking consumers. For a shared listing stream the book recommends full transitive compatibility, plus human review of changes in meaning. | [[event-driven-listings]] |
| **Replay / backfill** | Re-delivering state to consumers that already processed it: replaying from the log or a compacted topic, or backfilling from the system of record after a correction or schema addition, without starving live traffic. | [[event-driven-listings]] |
| **Dead-letter queue (DLQ)** | A place for events a consumer could not process. For consumers that need every delta, a failed event parks that listing until the failure is resolved while other listings continue. | [[event-driven-listings]] |
| **Listing system of record (SoR)** | The one store in which a change to a listing becomes true: it accepts writes, nothing else does, and it is complete for the listing's own data. It need not be one database, but each fact has one authoritative owner. | [[listing-data-management]] |
| **Read model** | A copy of listing data shaped for one way of reading it, derived from the system of record, usually from the event stream, and rebuildable from it: the search index, a detail-page cache, pro statistics. The read side of CQRS (command query responsibility segregation). | [[listing-data-management]] |
| **Copy kinds** | The book distinguishes replicas, read models, caches, archives, evidence stores, analytical copies and external copies, each with its own rebuild and retention rules. Every copy must be either rebuildable or governed. | [[listing-data-management]] |
| **Staleness / staleness budget** | Staleness is the gap between the system of record and what a screen shows; a staleness budget is the written maximum lag for one read path, with the measurement that proves it. | [[listing-data-management]] |
| **Read-your-writes / monotonic reads / bounded staleness / removal priority** | The consistency guarantees users notice: sellers see their own change at once; buyers never see an older state after a newer one; derived surfaces lag by a measured maximum; and removals have a tighter bound than additions. | [[listing-data-management]] |
| **Erasure propagation** | Carrying a GDPR erasure request to every copy of a person's data, where each owner deletes, anonymizes, retains with a named legal basis and end date, or reports not held, and an erasure ledger records every outcome as proof. | [[listing-data-management]] |
| **Crypto-shredding** | Encrypting each subject's personal fields with a per-subject key and erasing them by destroying the key, for stores that must stay immutable, such as raw data zones and evidence stores. | [[listing-data-management]] |
| **SEO architecture** | The components and rules that decide which URLs exist, what each URL returns over its lifetime, how pages are rendered and how search engines learn about changes. SEO is search engine optimization. | [[web-and-seo-architecture]] |
| **Crawl budget** | How much a search engine is willing and able to crawl on a site; not a fixed allowance, and wasted on facet combinations and dead listing pages. | [[web-and-seo-architecture]] |
| **Landing page** | A search result page the platform has deliberately made indexable, with a canonical URL, curated title and copy, internal links, and a rule saying when it may exist; the main durable organic asset of a listing site. | [[web-and-seo-architecture]] |
| **Landing page generation** | The process that decides which category, location and attribute combinations get an indexable URL, using demand, supply, distinctness, depth and quality rules, keeps the set current and retires pages cleanly. Indexability is a computed state: indexable, thin (`noindex, follow`), gone or redirected. | [[web-and-seo-architecture]] |
| **Backend-for-frontend (BFF)** | A server-side component owned by the team that owns a client (iOS and Android apps, or the web front end), aggregating domain APIs into the shape that client needs and absorbing differences in release cadence. | [[web-and-seo-architecture]] |
| **Rendering modes** | Client-side rendering (CSR) builds pages in the browser; server-side rendering (SSR) with hydration sends full HTML and then attaches interactivity; streaming SSR sends chunks as data arrives; static or incremental generation builds ahead; dynamic rendering for crawlers only is a discouraged workaround. | [[web-and-seo-architecture]] |
| **Redirect registry / URL and canonical service** | The URL service builds every public URL from domain identifiers; the redirect registry maps every retired URL to its successor or a gone status (`301`, `410`), with reason and date, for web and apps alike. | [[web-and-seo-architecture]] |
| **Structured data** | Machine-readable markup, usually schema.org vocabulary, that tells search engines what a page is: product snippets versus merchant listings, `JobPosting`, real-estate types. Search engines' support changes; Google removed vehicle-listing documentation in 2025. | [[web-and-seo-architecture]] |
| **Core Web Vitals** | Google's user-experience measures at the 75th percentile of page loads: Largest Contentful Paint (LCP, good at 2.5 s or less), Interaction to Next Paint (INP, 200 ms or less) and Cumulative Layout Shift (CLS). Treated as budgets owned by page owners. | [[web-and-seo-architecture]] |
| **Answer engine** | An AI assistant or AI search mode that answers questions directly, sometimes citing or linking to sources; a new referral channel for marketplaces and a new class of crawler. | [[web-and-seo-architecture]], [[ai-in-listing-systems]] |
| **Data platform** | The shared pipelines, storage, compute, metadata and access controls through which a group's data is collected, governed and used for anything other than serving the live product. | [[data-platform]] |
| **Tracking plan** | The governed catalog of behavioral events a product emits: each event's name, trigger, required properties and types, identifiers, consent category and owner, versioned like any contract. Every event carries a common envelope including tenant, identifiers and consent state. | [[data-platform]] |
| **Data contract** | A versioned, machine-readable agreement between a data producer and the platform: schema, semantics, personal-data classification, ownership, freshness and retention. Records that fail it go to quarantine, which feeds back to the producer. | [[data-platform]] |
| **Data product** | A dataset or small family of datasets that a named team owns and publishes for others, with a contract, documentation, quality and freshness service levels, access policy and support. Source-aligned products mirror sources; consumer-aligned products serve one purpose. | [[data-platform]] |
| **Data mesh** | An organizational approach in which domains own and publish their data as products on a shared self-service platform under federated governance. The book's reading of the record: ownership federates, the substrate and the rules do not. | [[data-platform]] |
| **Data warehouse / lakehouse** | A warehouse stores data in an engine's managed format with tightly coupled metadata, storage and query; a lakehouse keeps data in open direct-access formats (such as Parquet with an open table format like Apache Iceberg) with warehouse-grade performance and first-class machine-learning support. | [[data-platform]] |
| **Governed metric** | A business measure with a named owner, a written definition, a single computation in code, a version, and rules for restatement when the definition or data changes. Liquidity metrics must be governed because small definitional choices change them by large factors. | [[data-platform]] |
| **Metrics layer (semantic layer)** | The component that holds governed metric definitions and computes or serves them to every consumer, so dashboards, experiment readouts and dealer reports do not each reimplement "contacts". | [[data-platform]] |
| **Online controlled experiment** | Randomly assigning units (users, sellers, listings, regions, time periods) to variants, exposing them, measuring outcomes and attributing the difference to the variant. | [[experimentation]] |
| **Interference** | The outcome of one unit depends on the treatment of others, as when treated and control sellers compete for the same buyers' attention. The default condition for ranking, pricing and visibility tests, not an edge case: the measured effect includes a transfer between groups. | [[experimentation]] |
| **Cluster randomization** | Assigning groups of units that interact mainly with each other, such as a region × category cell, as one unit, which contains most interference at the cost of fewer effective units. | [[experimentation]] |
| **Switchback / two-sided / geo experiment** | Designs for marketplaces: alternating treatment over time slots (with carryover between slots to handle); randomizing buyers and listings together; and comparing whole regions. | [[experimentation]] |
| **Primary metric** | The one metric a test is designed and powered to move, also called the overall evaluation criterion, chosen before the test starts. | [[experimentation]] |
| **Guardrail metric** | A metric that must not degrade beyond a set threshold for a change to ship, whatever the primary result: liquidity outcomes, the other side of the market, and the control group's own trend. | [[experimentation]] |
| **Sample ratio mismatch (SRM)** | A difference between the observed and the designed split of units, which signals broken assignment or logging; the result should not be read. | [[experimentation]] |
| **Variance reduction (CUPED) / sequential testing** | Using pre-experiment data to remove predictable variation, roughly halving required samples in published cases; and methods whose p-values and intervals stay valid when results are checked continuously. | [[experimentation]] |
| **ML platform** | The shared tooling that lets teams build, deploy and operate models without rebuilding infrastructure: data access, feature pipelines, training orchestration, a model registry, serving, a prediction log and monitoring. | [[ml-platform]] |
| **Feature store** | A system that defines features once and serves them consistently to training (offline, point-in-time-correct history) and inference (online, low-latency values); justified once real-time behavioral features drive decisions. | [[ml-platform]], [[fraud-prevention]] |
| **Point-in-time correctness / training–serving skew** | Training must use feature values as they were when the decision was taken; skew is any difference between the features a model saw in training and those it receives in production. | [[ml-platform]], [[ranking]] |
| **Drift** | Change in the input distribution (data drift), in the relationship between inputs and outcomes (concept drift), or in what a label means (label drift); in marketplaces caused by taxonomy changes, seasons, adversaries and feedback loops. | [[ml-platform]] |
| **Automated valuation model (AVM)** | A model that estimates the market value of a specific asset, usually a property, from its characteristics, location, comparable transactions and market trends, such as Zillow's Zestimate. | [[ml-platform]] |
| **Model governance / decision tiers** | The rules for who may deploy a model, what evidence its deployment requires, how it is monitored and who answers for it, scaled to the decision it makes: inform, order, gate or commit. Tiers attach to uses, not models; moving up a tier is a new launch. | [[ml-platform]] |
| **Proxy label** | An observable stand-in for the outcome a model should predict, such as contacts for relevance, delisting for sale or moderator decisions for abuse; each has a bias, and choosing it is choosing what the marketplace optimizes. | [[ml-platform]] |
| **Brand / market / tenant** | A brand is the consumer-facing marketplace identity; a market is one country in which a brand operates; a tenant is the unit a shared capability scopes, configures, isolates, releases and bills by, usually brand × country. | [[multi-country-platforms]] |
| **Tenancy model** | The decisions that answer, for every shared capability: what a tenant is, how it is identified, how strongly it is isolated, how its configuration is resolved, how changes are released to it, how its costs are attributed and how it could leave. | [[multi-country-platforms]] |
| **Tenant isolation (logical / partitioned / dedicated)** | A tenant key on every row and message; a schema, index, topic or bucket per tenant on shared infrastructure; or a cluster, database or account per tenant. The recommended default is partitioned, so a tenant can be moved or handed over as a unit. | [[multi-country-platforms]] |
| **Global component** | A capability built and run once for the group, with a stable contract, serving many tenants beneath their local products, such as identity, the trust-and-safety pipeline or the search engine. | [[multi-country-platforms]] |
| **Configuration over code** | Expressing differences between tenants that are data as governed configuration with a schema per key, a resolution hierarchy (group, brand, tenant, profile, segment, experiment) and change control, not as branches in code. | [[multi-country-platforms]] |
| **Extension point** | A defined interface in a shared capability where a tenant-owned component plugs in, used when a difference is behavior rather than a value: configure, then extend, and fork only under a decision record with an expiry date. | [[multi-country-platforms]], [[vertical-requirements]] |
| **Release rings / group-urgent change** | Rolling changes to a global component through rings of tenants, smallest first; some classes of change (fraud rules, security fixes, legal takedowns) bypass the rings and apply everywhere at once. | [[multi-country-platforms]] |
| **Tenant exit path / separability drill** | Every shared capability documents how a tenant's data is exported, handed over, run transitionally and switched off; once a year a drill proves it can be done. | [[multi-country-platforms]] |
| **Platform models** | Ways to share across markets, from fork per country through federation with coordinated choices, shared core with local configuration, global components beneath local products and platform as product, to a converged product platform. Decided per capability, not for the group. | [[multi-country-platforms]] |
| **Centralization pendulum** | The tendency of marketplace groups to swing between centralizing for synergy and decentralizing for focus, often after a change of owner or strategy; the architecture should survive the swing without being rebuilt. | [[multi-country-platforms]], [[organizing-marketplace-engineering]] |
| **Service level indicator / objective (SLI / SLO) / error budget** | An SLI is good events divided by total events; an SLO is the target for it; the error budget is 100% minus the SLO, the unreliability a team may spend. | [[reliability-and-scale]] |
| **Journey SLO** | An objective set on a marketplace journey that creates or protects liquidity (search, listing view, listing visible, listing gone, message delivered, alert sent, insertion), measured from the user's side, with an owner and an error-budget policy. | [[reliability-and-scale]] |
| **Degradation mode** | A predefined, tested reduction of functionality that a journey switches to under load or partial failure so its essential part keeps working, in steps from shedding bots, trimming optional work and serving from cache to browse only. | [[reliability-and-scale]] |
| **Read-only warm standby** | A multi-region posture in which a second region serves browse and listing pages within minutes while writes queue or pause; with multi-zone deployment, the right answer for most journeys of a country-scoped classifieds group. | [[reliability-and-scale]] |
| **Bot management** | Policy, crawler verification, edge classification, quotas, product design and contracted access that separate wanted crawlers from scrapers and contested AI crawlers. In the EU the sui generis database right adds a legal layer. | [[reliability-and-scale]] |
| **Cost per search** | The fully allocated run cost of the search path (engine, gateway, caches, observability) divided by search requests, reported separately for human and automated traffic. Companions are cost per live listing per month and cost per contact. | [[reliability-and-scale]] |
| **FinOps** | The practice of managing cloud and technology cost through shared engineering, finance and business ownership; in marketplaces made concrete through unit costs per listing, search and contact. | [[reliability-and-scale]] |

## <a id="g-leadership"></a>Part VIII — Leadership

The Chief Architect's agenda. [[platform-consolidation]] owns what to consolidate after acquisitions; [[organizing-marketplace-engineering]] owns team design and governance; [[modernization-and-migration]] owns moving a live marketplace; [[ai-in-listing-systems]] owns AI across the lifecycle; [[long-term-evolution]] owns decade-scale thinking.

| Term | Definition | Chapter |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Consolidation sequence** | The ordered, gated stages through which an acquired marketplace's capabilities move onto group platforms: day-one controls; invisible and contractual capabilities (cloud, data, advertising, supplier contracts such as payments); trust and identity; shared engines; and, last and optionally, the product platform. What users cannot see and what gains value from pooling moves first; each gate is a measured condition, not a date. | [[platform-consolidation]] |
| **Integration options** | The choices for an acquired marketplace, from most to least reversible: hold separate, federate under group controls, share services beneath its own product, replatform onto a sibling, merge brands, or divest. They are chosen per business and may be combined over time; an acquisition without a recorded option drifts into the Acquisition Archipelago. | [[platform-consolidation]] |
| **Synergy case / synergy ledger** | The quantified hypothesis that combining a business with the group raises combined profit: cost and revenue synergies, weighed against dis-synergies (traffic loss, churn, double running) and the separation cost if the business is sold again. Deal models usually show only the first two. A synergy ledger tracks it as one line per synergy or dis-synergy, each with an owner, dated evidence and the consolidation stage it depends on. | [[platform-consolidation]] |
| **Brand migration** | Moving a marketplace's users, sellers, partners and search-engine presence from one consumer brand or domain to another, as in a rebrand, the removal of a former parent's name or a merger of portals. It is an architectural event, planned as a workstream separate from any platform migration; organic search traffic is the main risk. | [[platform-consolidation]] |
| **Marketplace team topology** | The assignment of team types to a marketplace group's journeys, bounded contexts and shared capabilities, together with the interaction mode between each pair of teams. | [[organizing-marketplace-engineering]] |
| **Stream-aligned / platform / complicated-subsystem / enabling team** | Team Topologies' four types: teams on a flow of work, such as a buyer or seller journey; teams providing an internal product with a contract; teams owning work that needs deep expertise, such as ranking or AVMs; and temporary teams that help others adopt or migrate. | [[organizing-marketplace-engineering]] |
| **Architecture governance** | The decision rights, forums, records, principles and standards through which architectural decisions are made, communicated and revisited. | [[organizing-marketplace-engineering]] |
| **Decision rights / decision record** | Who may decide what (group, platform, brand, team); and the short document capturing a decision's context, decision, status, consequences and review date, kept with the code. An architecture decision record (ADR) is the common form. | [[organizing-marketplace-engineering]] |
| **Advice process** | Andrew Harmel-Law's model in which anyone can make an architectural decision, provided they first seek advice from everyone meaningfully affected and from relevant experts, and record it. Advice is public and decisions are recorded. | [[organizing-marketplace-engineering]] |
| **Platform funding models** | Ways to pay for platforms: a central program budget, fixed allocation (a "platform tax"), showback of usage-based cost without charging, chargeback by usage, or an internal market. Policy-driven platforms such as identity and trust and safety suit fixed allocation. | [[organizing-marketplace-engineering]] |
| **Conway's law** | The observation that systems tend to mirror the communication structure of the organization that builds them, which makes team design an architectural decision. | [[organizing-marketplace-engineering]] |
| **Strangler fig** | Replacing a legacy system piece by piece by routing traffic for one capability at a time to new components, until the old system can be switched off. | [[modernization-and-migration]] |
| **Branch by abstraction / event interception** | Two migration techniques: putting an abstraction in front of a capability so old and new implementations can be swapped behind it; and capturing the legacy system's changes as events so new components can be fed without changing its code. | [[modernization-and-migration]] |
| **Parallel run / shadow traffic / canary** | Three ways of running old and new side by side. Shadow traffic (a dark launch) sends the new system a copy of real requests or events and discards its output after comparison; a parallel run has both systems compute on real requests, with one authoritative and the other compared; a canary serves a small share of real traffic from the new system. | [[modernization-and-migration]] |
| **Migration stage gate** | A decision point in moving one slice of a system, with exit criteria agreed beforehand and a defined rollback, that must be passed before more traffic or responsibility moves. Veldmark uses five per slice: contract, shadow, canary, ramp and retire; the move to retire is the recorded point of no return. | [[modernization-and-migration]] |
| **Relevance parity** | The condition that a new search path produces outcomes for buyers and sellers that are not worse than the old path's, within agreed tolerances, measured on real queries (replayed comparisons of top results, then online tests); not identical results. | [[modernization-and-migration]] |
| **URL migration** | The controlled change of public URLs, or the deliberate decision not to change them, with old-to-new mappings, permanent redirects, consistent canonicals and sitemaps, and monitoring of crawl and traffic that preserve search-engine signals. | [[modernization-and-migration]], [[web-and-seo-architecture]] |
| **Feature parity trap** | The mistake of insisting that a new system reproduce every feature of the old one before launch, including features nobody uses, which delays migration indefinitely. The remedy is a behavior catalog in which each legacy behavior is carried, changed or retired. | [[modernization-and-migration]] |
| **AI listing assistant** | A feature in the insertion flow that proposes listing content (category, attributes, title, description, price) from photos, voice or short text, for the seller to accept, edit or reject. | [[ai-in-listing-systems]] |
| **Structured-output compiler** | The pattern in which a model translates unstructured input into a structure the marketplace already has (an attribute set, a filter query, a policy label), so the structured system of record stays in charge. | [[ai-in-listing-systems]] |
| **Agentic search** | Search in which a model plans steps and calls tools (keyword or vector search, filters, comparisons, external lookups) over several turns, rather than returning one ranked list for one query; suited to high-consideration journeys. | [[ai-in-listing-systems]] |
| **Agent-to-marketplace interface / Model Context Protocol (MCP)** | An interface through which external AI agents call the marketplace's capabilities as tools; MCP is an open protocol, introduced by Anthropic in 2024, for connecting agents to tools and data. | [[ai-in-listing-systems]] |
| **Autonomy level** | How far an AI feature acts without a person: it suggests, drafts for confirmation, acts with confirmation, or acts autonomously. | [[ai-in-listing-systems]] |
| **AI governance for marketplaces** | The register, rules and controls that decide which AI uses may run, at what autonomy, with which data, evaluated how, at what cost and with whom accountable. | [[ai-in-listing-systems]] |
| **AI gateway / evaluation harness** | The gateway is the control point every model call passes (authentication, routing, rate limits, cost metering, logging, a kill switch per feature); the harness runs golden sets per language, category and use before every prompt or model change and on sampled production traffic. | [[ai-in-listing-systems]] |
| **EU AI Act** | Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, with obligations phased in from 2025: transparency for generated content (Article 50), duties for general-purpose AI model providers including respecting text-and-data-mining reservations, and stricter rules for high-risk uses. | [[ai-in-listing-systems]] |
| **Stressor analysis** | Listing what could happen to a marketplace group (a sale, a law, a fraud wave, a lost channel), recording for each the attractor, what breaks in the current architecture and the residue that would survive it, then counting which components and contracts carry the most residues so they get the most care. Adapted from Barry M. O'Reilly's residuality theory. | [[long-term-evolution]] |
| **Stressor / residue / attractor** | A stressor is anything that could happen to the business or its environment, likely or not, technical or not; the attractor is the state the business is pushed into when it hits (how people, partners and the market will actually behave, such as a carve-out pushing it into transitional services); the residue is what remains of the architecture afterwards, and the structural change that would let it survive. | [[long-term-evolution]] |
| **Marketplace fitness function** | A measured, owned, regularly evaluated statement about a stage of the liquidity machine, such as "a sold listing disappears from every registered consumer within two minutes at p95". Unlike a business KPI it has an architectural owner and remedy; unlike an SLO it measures the whole machine, not one service. Veldmark's scorecard has twelve. | [[long-term-evolution]] |
| **Optionality / separability** | Keeping future choices open: listing data and events in portable, documented forms, and every tenant able to leave the platform, so a sale, a merger or a new vertical does not force a rebuild. | [[long-term-evolution]], [[multi-country-platforms]] |
